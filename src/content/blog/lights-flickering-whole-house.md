When lights flicker or dim in **one** fixture, the problem is usually the bulb, the dimmer or that fixture's wiring. When they flicker **all over the house**, the cause sits further upstream: where power enters your home, at the main panel, or on the utility's equipment outside.

The most common causes are **a large appliance starting up**, **a problem on the utility side**, and **a loose connection at the service or main panel**. The one you must not ignore is a **loose or failing neutral**, which makes some lights brighter while others dim.

> **Safety first:** Do not remove the panel cover, pull the meter, or touch the meter, the meter base or the overhead service wires. Parts of the panel and everything on the meter side stay live even with the main breaker off. Flipping a breaker handle is the only thing you should do at the panel. If you smell burning, hear crackling or buzzing at the panel or meter, or see sparks or a sagging wire, keep away and call the utility (and 911 if there is fire or smoke).

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Check if it is really the whole house.** Walk through every room while it happens. If only one room or circuit flickers, see cause 8 and the guide to [LED lights flickering on a dimmer](/electrical/led-lights-flicker-on-dimmer/).
2. **Watch for a pattern.** Note what starts at the moment of the flicker: AC or heat pump, furnace blower, well pump, dryer, microwave, EV charger.
3. **Compare lights.** If some lights get **brighter** while others get **dimmer** at the same moment, skip straight to cause 3 and call for help today.
4. **Ask a neighbor.** If houses on your street see the same thing, the problem is probably on the utility side (causes 2 and 7).
5. **Keep a log** for a few days: date, time, weather, what was running, and how long it lasted. Utilities and electricians find problems faster with it.

## 1. A large appliance starting up

Motors draw a big burst of current for a moment when they start, several times their running current. A central AC, heat pump, well pump, sump pump or refrigerator compressor can cause a short dip in every light, especially in an older home or one far from the transformer. (Curious about the size of that burst? This [motor starting current calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/motor-starting-current/) shows the idea.)

**Normal:** a quick dip, under about a second, only when a big motor starts, about the same each time.

**Not normal:** a deep dip, a dip that lasts several seconds, flicker that keeps going while the appliance runs, dimming from small loads like a toaster or microwave, or a problem that is getting worse.

**Fix:**

- Note which appliance triggers it and tell your electrician.
- Ask an electrician to check the service connections, the main breaker and the appliance's circuit. A loose connection makes normal startup dips much worse.
- For an AC or heat pump, an HVAC technician can check the outdoor unit. Some systems can have a soft start device added, but ask a pro whether it suits your unit.

## 2. A problem on the utility side

If neighbors see the same flicker, the cause is probably on the utility's equipment: a loose or corroded connection on the pole, a failing transformer, tree branches brushing the lines, or switching and equipment problems on the local grid.

**Fix:**

- Call your utility's outage or service line (on your bill or their website) and say that lights are flickering at several homes.
- Give them your log. Many utilities can check voltage at the meter or fit a recorder to catch an intermittent problem.
- Checking and repairing their own equipment is normally at no cost to you.

## 3. A loose or failing neutral

This is the serious one. US homes get two 120 V "legs" plus a neutral, giving 120 V for lights and outlets and 240 V for big appliances. The neutral keeps each leg near 120 V. When the neutral connection loosens or corrodes (at the transformer, the overhead drop, the meter or the main panel), the voltage on the two legs can drift apart.

**Warning signs:** some lights get brighter while others dim at the same moment, lights surge when a big appliance turns on, motors hum or run slowly, bulbs and electronics fail more often than they should.

**What to do right now:**

- **Call your utility immediately** and say you suspect a loose neutral. Many treat it as urgent.
- **Call a licensed electrician** to check the meter base, service conductors and main panel if the utility's side checks out.
- **Unplug computers, TVs and other sensitive electronics** until it is fixed.
- If lights are going very bright or something smells hot, turn off the main breaker if you can reach it safely, and touch nothing else.

## 4. Loose connections at the meter or main breaker

The large wires from the meter land on the main breaker or main lugs in your panel. If those connections loosen over time (heating and cooling cycles, aluminum wire, a poor original install), they can arc and heat up, and every light in the house flickers.

**Fix:** This is a job for a licensed electrician only, plus the utility if the meter has to be pulled. Signs that make it urgent: a warm or discolored panel cover, a buzzing or crackling sound, or a burning smell.

## 5. A loose breaker or bus connection in the panel

Breakers clip onto metal bars called the bus. A breaker that is loose on the bus, or a burned spot on the bus, can make the lights on that breaker flicker. If it is the main breaker or a whole section of the bus, it can look like whole-house flicker. Some older panel types are known for poor breaker connections.

**Fix:** Have an electrician inspect and repair the panel. Don't push on breakers or try to reseat them yourself. If a breaker also trips often, see [circuit breaker keeps tripping](/electrical/breaker-keeps-tripping/).

## 6. Wind or weather moving the overhead drop

If your power arrives on an overhead wire, wind can move it and expose a loose or corroded connection. That gives flicker that comes and goes with gusts. Ice, storms and tree contact can do the same.

**Fix:**

- From the ground, look at the wire from the pole to your house. Is it sagging, rubbing on branches, or pulling away from the house?
- Call the utility. They handle the drop and the pole connection. The mast and weatherhead on your house are often the owner's responsibility, so an electrician may be needed too. Ask the utility which parts are theirs.
- Never touch the wire or trim branches near it yourself.

## 7. A failing or overloaded utility transformer

The gray can on a pole or the green box on a pad usually serves several homes. If it is overloaded or failing, the whole street may see dimming at busy times, such as hot afternoons or cold evenings.

**Fix:** Call the utility and mention that neighbors see it too. Only the utility can test, upgrade or replace a transformer.

## 8. An overloaded circuit

If the flicker is really in one room or one circuit, a heavy load on that circuit (a space heater, a vacuum, a hair dryer) can dim the lights sharing it. That is not whole-house flicker, but it's often mistaken for it.

**Fix:**

- Move heavy appliances to a different circuit.
- If the lights dim badly even with modest loads, or the breaker trips, have an electrician check for a loose connection on that circuit.
- One fixture or one dimmed room flickering? See [LED lights flickering on a dimmer](/electrical/led-lights-flicker-on-dimmer/).

## Lights suddenly very bright or electronics dying?

Lights that glow brighter than normal, bulbs burning out quickly, or several electronics failing in a short time are strong signs of a voltage problem, often a lost neutral.

- Turn off the main breaker if you can reach it safely, and unplug sensitive electronics.
- Call the utility's emergency line right away, then a licensed electrician.
- If you smell burning, see smoke, or the panel or meter is hot or sparking, leave it alone, get everyone out, and call 911.

## When to call an electrician (and the utility)

Call the **utility** if:

- Neighbors see flicker too, or it lines up with wind, storms or busy times of day.
- The overhead wire is sagging, damaged or touching trees.
- You suspect a loose neutral (some lights brighter, others dimmer).

Call a **licensed electrician** if:

- The utility finds nothing on their side.
- Lights dim deeply or for more than a moment when appliances start.
- The panel is warm, buzzing, discolored or smells hot.
- Breakers trip often, or you have an older panel and new flicker.

Whole-house flicker rarely fixes itself. Loose connections get worse as they heat up, so a call now is much cheaper than repairs after a burned panel or a house full of damaged electronics.
