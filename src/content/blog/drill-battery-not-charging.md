When a cordless drill battery won't charge, the charger is usually telling you why with its light. The most common causes are **a pack that is too hot or too cold**, **dirty contacts**, a **deeply discharged lithium pack** and a **battery that has simply worn out**. A failed charger is less common, and easy to rule out with a second battery.

Most fixes take a few minutes. The one thing to avoid is forcing a damaged or locked-out battery to charge.

> **Safety first:** Never charge a battery that is **swollen, cracked, leaking, smells odd or got very hot** for no reason. Don't open the pack, and **don't try to bypass its protection circuit** or jump-start it from another battery. Lithium-ion cells can overheat and catch fire when abused. Charge on a hard surface away from anything flammable.

## How to tell what's wrong: quick test

1. **Look at the charger light** with the battery in place and compare it to the chart on the charger label or in the manual.
2. **Feel the pack.** If it's hot from use or cold from a truck or garage, let it reach room temperature first.
3. **Look at the contacts** on the pack and inside the charger slot for dirt, corrosion or bent tabs.
4. **Try a second battery** of the same type on the same charger.
    - **The second battery charges:** the charger works. Focus on the first pack (causes 3, 4 and 5).
    - **It doesn't charge either:** suspect the charger or the outlet (cause 6).

## 1. Read the charger's light pattern

Charger lights are **not standard across brands**. Solid red, blinking red, alternating red and green, or a fast flash can mean charging, waiting for the temperature to settle, or a fault, depending on the maker.

**Fix:**

- Find the **code chart**. It's usually printed on the charger's label, sometimes on the underside, and always in the manual or on the maker's support site.
- Note whether the pattern means "**hot or cold delay**", "**problem with the pack**" or "**problem with the charger**". That points you to the right section below.
- If the light shows a fault, remove the pack, unplug the charger for a minute, then try again once.

## 2. The battery is too hot or too cold

Lithium-ion chargers protect the pack by refusing to charge, or charging very slowly, when it's outside a safe temperature range. A pack straight off a hard-working drill can be too hot. A pack left in a truck or unheated garage in winter can be too cold. Many chargers show a special light for this "temperature delay".

**Fix:**

- Let the pack sit **indoors at room temperature** for 30 to 60 minutes, then try again.
- Don't heat a cold pack with a heater, hair dryer or oven, and don't put a hot pack in the freezer.
- Check the charging temperature range in your manual. Room temperature is ideal for most packs.
- Keep the charger out of direct sun and away from heaters. Some chargers have a fan, so keep the vents clear.

## 3. Dirty or damaged contacts

Sawdust, drywall dust, grease or light corrosion on the metal contacts can stop the charger from reading the pack.

**Fix:**

1. **Unplug the charger** and remove the battery.
2. Blow or brush dust out of the charger slot and the pack's terminals.
3. Wipe the contacts with a **cotton swab lightly dampened with isopropyl alcohol**. Let them dry fully.
4. Look for **bent, broken or burnt** contacts. Don't bend tabs back with metal tools. Burn marks or melted plastic mean the pack or charger should be replaced.
5. Slide the pack in until it **seats fully**. A pack that's only partly in can show no light at all.

## 4. The pack is deeply discharged

Lithium-ion packs have a built-in protection circuit. If a pack is run completely flat and then left for weeks or months, its voltage can drift below the level the charger will accept. The charger may show a fault light or nothing at all.

**Fix:**

- Leave it on the charger for **several minutes**. Some chargers try a slow recovery charge on low packs before the normal charge starts.
- Try a **different charger** made for the same battery platform, if you can borrow one.
- If it still won't take a charge, **replace the pack**. Contact the maker if it's under warranty.
- **Don't** connect it to another battery, a car battery or a power supply to "wake it up". That bypasses the protection that keeps the cells safe.

To avoid this next time, store packs partly charged in a cool, dry place and top them up every few months.

## 5. The battery is worn out

Every rechargeable battery loses capacity over time. Heat, deep discharges and heavy use speed it up. An aging pack may charge slowly, report full after a short charge, or die after a few minutes of work. Some chargers show a specific light for a pack at the end of its life.

**Fix:**

- Compare it with a newer pack on the same job. If the old one runs out far sooner, it's worn.
- Check the warranty. Many tool makers cover batteries for a set period, so keep your receipt.
- Buy a replacement made for **your tool's platform and voltage**. Non-genuine packs vary in quality and safety.

Older drills with **nickel (NiCd or NiMH) packs** behave differently and use their own chargers. If your drill is old enough to use nickel packs, replacement packs and chargers can be harder to find, and a new drill may be the better buy.

## 6. The charger or outlet is the problem

If two batteries won't charge on the same charger, the charger or its power supply is the likely culprit.

**Fix:**

- Plug a lamp or phone charger into the same outlet to confirm it has power. Check for a tripped GFCI or breaker. Some garage and outdoor outlets are GFCI protected.
- Plug the charger **directly into a wall outlet**, not a long extension cord or a power strip that's switched off.
- Look for a damaged cord, a cracked case or a burning smell. Stop using a charger with any of these.
- Unplug it for a few minutes to let it reset, then try again.
- If a known-good battery still won't charge, replace the charger with the model made for your battery platform.

## 7. Wrong charger or a poor fit

Battery platforms differ by brand, voltage and sometimes generation. A charger that physically accepts a pack isn't guaranteed to charge it.

**Fix:**

- Read the **label on the battery and the charger**. Check that the voltage and battery platform match.
- Don't use adapters to fit another brand's battery to your charger.
- Check the charger's latch or rails for cracks. A loose fit can break the connection.

## 8. Charger says full, but the drill won't run

If the charger shows a full charge but the drill does nothing, the problem may be the drill, not the battery.

**Fix:**

- Check the **forward/reverse selector**. In its middle (locked) position the trigger won't work on most drills.
- Look at the drill's battery contacts for dirt or damage and clean them the same way as in cause 3.
- Try the battery in another tool from the same platform. If it works there, the drill needs service.
- If the battery has a **charge gauge button**, press it to confirm it really is full.

## Swollen, leaking or damaged battery?

**Stop using it now.** Don't charge it, don't try to press the case back together and don't throw it in the trash.

- Move it outside or onto a nonflammable surface, away from other packs, until you can get rid of it.
- If it's hot, smoking or hissing, keep away from it and call 911 if it catches fire.
- Tape over the terminals with electrical tape.
- Take it to a **battery recycling drop-off**. Many home improvement stores and local waste programs accept lithium-ion tool batteries.

## When to call a pro

For tool batteries, "a pro" usually means the **manufacturer's service center** or support line. Contact them if:

- The battery or charger is still under warranty.
- The charger shows a fault light with every battery you try.
- A pack or charger got hot, smelled burnt or shows melted plastic.
- You're not sure whether a replacement battery is compatible with your tool.

Don't send a swollen or leaking pack by mail without asking first. Damaged lithium-ion batteries have special shipping rules, so the maker or a recycling drop-off will tell you what to do.
