A dripping faucet means something inside the valve no longer seals tightly when the handle is off. Water pressure pushes a little past the worn part, and it drips from the spout or seeps out around the handle.

The cause is almost always **a hardened rubber washer, a worn cartridge, or tired O-rings and seals**. The fix depends on which of the four faucet types you have, so start by identifying it.

## How to tell which type of faucet you have: quick test

1. **Count the handles.** Two separate hot and cold handles are usually compression, cartridge or ceramic disc. A single lever is usually cartridge, ball or ceramic disc.
2. **Feel how it shuts off.** If you have to turn a handle firmly until it stops, and it tightens harder as the washer wears, it is a **compression** faucet. A quarter turn or half turn to a hard stop usually means a **cartridge** or **ceramic disc** valve.
3. **Look under the lever.** A rounded dome cap under a single lever that swings in a wide arc is typical of a **ball** faucet. A wide, flat-bottomed cylinder under the lever usually means **ceramic disc**.
4. **Check for a brand or model.** Look on the faucet body, the escutcheon, under the sink or in the paperwork. The manufacturer's site usually has an exploded parts diagram, which removes the guesswork.

If you're still unsure, take the faucet apart (step 1 first) and compare what you find with the diagram above.

## 1. Shut off the water and prepare the sink

Every repair starts here, and skipping it is how screws end up in the drain.

**Fix:**

1. Turn the **shutoff valves under the sink** clockwise until they stop. If there are none, or they won't close, use the main water shutoff for the house.
2. Open the faucet to drain the water left in the line and confirm the water is really off.
3. **Plug the drain** with the stopper or a rag, and lay a towel in the sink to protect it and catch small parts.
4. Take a photo of each stage as you take it apart, and lay the parts out in order. That makes reassembly easy.

Old shutoff valves sometimes drip from the stem once you turn them. A gentle snug of the packing nut behind the handle usually stops it. If one won't close at all, that valve may need replacing, which is a good job to hand to a plumber.

## 2. Compression faucet: replace the washer

Compression faucets press a rubber washer down onto a brass valve seat to stop the water. Over time the washer hardens, cracks or flattens, and the faucet starts to drip, often after years of being cranked shut.

The washer and the seat below it are what wear.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

1. Pry off the decorative cap on the handle, remove the screw and lift off the handle.
2. Loosen the **packing nut** (or hex nut) with an adjustable wrench and unscrew the stem the same way you would open the faucet.
3. Remove the **brass screw** at the bottom of the stem and pry out the old washer.
4. Fit a new washer of the **same size and shape** (flat or beveled), and replace the brass screw if it is worn.
5. Replace the **O-ring** on the stem too, and coat it lightly with plumber's grease.
6. Reassemble, turn the water back on slowly and test.

If you have to crank the new washer very hard to stop the drip, the seat is probably damaged. See step 7.

## 3. Cartridge faucet: replace the cartridge

Cartridge faucets use a plastic or brass cartridge that moves up and down or rotates to control the flow. When the internal seals wear, the faucet drips from the spout and the handle may feel stiff or loose.

**Fix:**

1. Remove the handle. The screw is often under a decorative cap, or there is a small set screw that takes a hex key.
2. Remove the **retaining clip** or **retaining nut** that holds the cartridge in place. Keep the clip safe, it is easy to lose.
3. Note which way the cartridge faces (take a photo), then pull it **straight up**. Pliers help on a stuck one. Some brands need an inexpensive cartridge puller.
4. **Take the old cartridge to the store** and buy an exact match, or order by the faucet's model number.
5. Install the new one in the same orientation, refit the clip and handle, and test. If hot and cold are reversed, turn the cartridge 180 degrees.

## 4. Ball faucet: replace the seats and springs

A ball faucet has a rotating ball with holes that line up with the hot and cold inlets. Small **rubber seats sitting on springs** press against the ball. When they wear, the faucet drips from the spout.

**Fix:**

1. Buy a **ball faucet repair kit** for your brand. Kits usually include seats, springs, O-rings and a small wrench for the cap.
2. Loosen the set screw and remove the handle. Unscrew the dome cap.
3. Lift out the cam, the cam washer and the ball. Note how the ball sits.
4. Use a small screwdriver to lift out the old seats and springs from the faucet body.
5. Press new springs and seats into place, refit the ball, cam and cap, and snug the adjusting ring just enough to stop leaks without making the handle stiff.

If water leaks from around the spout collar instead, replace the O-rings on the faucet body at the same time (step 6).

## 5. Ceramic disc faucet: clean or replace the seals

Ceramic disc faucets use two polished discs that slide over each other. The discs themselves are very hard and rarely wear, but **rubber seals under the cylinder** can fail, and grit or mineral scale can stop the discs closing fully.

**Fix:**

1. Remove the handle and the escutcheon cap, then unscrew the screws or nut holding the disc cylinder and lift it out.
2. Check the **rubber seals** on the bottom. If they're cracked, flattened or missing, replace them with matching seals or replace the whole cylinder.
3. If there is scale, soak the seals and cylinder openings in white vinegar for a while, then rinse.
4. Reassemble and turn the water on **slowly**. A sudden blast of pressure can crack a ceramic disc.

If a disc is chipped or cracked, replace the whole cylinder.

## 6. Leaking at the handle or base: replace the O-rings

Water around the handle, or under the base of a swivel spout, is usually a worn **O-ring** or packing rather than the main valve seal.

**Fix:**

- On compression and cartridge faucets, replace the O-ring on the stem or cartridge.
- On kitchen faucets with a swivel spout, lift off the spout and replace the O-rings on the faucet body.
- Coat new O-rings with a thin film of **plumber's grease** (silicone type) so they seat smoothly.
- If an old compression faucet weeps at the stem, try snugging the packing nut a quarter turn first.

## 7. Worn valve seat (compression faucets)

The brass seat that the washer presses against can get pitted or grooved by years of water flow. Even a new washer won't seal on a rough seat.

**Fix:**

- Look into the faucet body with a flashlight. A smooth, round seat is fine. A rough, pitted or grooved one needs attention.
- Many seats unscrew with a **seat wrench**. Take the old one to the store to match it.
- Seats that don't unscrew can sometimes be smoothed with a **seat dresser** (reseating tool).
- If the seat can't be removed or dressed, replacing the faucet is often the simpler answer.

## Still dripping after a new part?

- **Wrong part.** A cartridge that is a few millimeters off, or a washer that is too thin, won't seal. Compare it with the old one again.
- **Debris in the valve.** Grit can lodge between new parts. Remove the part, flush the line briefly with the water on (into a bucket) and reinstall.
- **Damaged seat or body.** Pitting or corrosion inside the faucet body can't be fixed with new parts alone.
- **High water pressure.** Very high pressure wears parts faster and can push past seals. If several fixtures drip or bang, check your pressure. Our guide to [low water pressure](/plumbing/low-water-pressure/) explains how to test it with a simple gauge.

## When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if:

- The shutoff valves under the sink won't close or are leaking.
- A part is seized or corroded and won't come out without force.
- The faucet body is cracked or water is leaking under the sink or into the cabinet.
- You've replaced the worn part and it still drips, or you can't identify the faucet or find parts.

A plumber can also replace the whole faucet and the shutoff valves in one visit, which is often worth it on fixtures that are decades old.
