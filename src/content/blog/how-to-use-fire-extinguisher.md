A portable fire extinguisher can stop a **small, contained fire** before it spreads. It is not meant for a fire that's already filling a room. Using one well comes down to three things: **knowing when to fight and when to leave**, the **PASS method**, and having the **right type** within reach.

For most homes, the right type is a **multipurpose ABC extinguisher**. This guide covers how to use it, what the class letters mean, where to keep it and how to check it each month.

> **Safety first:** Your life matters more than your house. If the fire is bigger than a wastebasket, spreading, or the room is filling with smoke, **get out, close doors behind you and call 911 from outside.** Never use water on a grease or electrical fire. Never let the fire get between you and your way out.

## Fight it or get out? Quick check

Answer these before you pull the pin. If any answer is no, leave.

1. **Is everyone out or on their way out, and has someone called 911?**
2. **Is the fire small and not spreading?** Think of a wastebasket, a pan on the stove or a small appliance, not a burning couch or wall.
3. **Is the room clear of heavy smoke?** Smoke is what usually overcomes people, often before the flames reach them.
4. **Is your exit behind you?** You must be able to back straight out if it doesn't work.
5. **Do you have the right extinguisher, and do you know how to use it?** Check the label for the classes it covers.

## 1. Use the PASS method

Stand back from the fire, often given as **about 6 to 8 feet**, with your exit behind you. Hold the extinguisher upright.

1. **Pull** the pin. Twist it to break the plastic tamper seal and pull it straight out. Keep the nozzle pointed away from you.
2. **Aim** the nozzle or hose **low, at the base of the fire**. Spraying the flames themselves does little. You need to hit the fuel that's burning.
3. **Squeeze** the handle slowly and evenly. Letting go stops the spray.
4. **Sweep** from side to side across the base of the fire until it looks out. Move closer slowly as the fire shrinks.

**Watch it after.** Back away, keep an eye on the area and be ready to spray again. Fires can flare back up. If the extinguisher runs empty and the fire isn't out, **leave immediately**. Many home units empty in seconds, so there's no time to experiment.

## 2. Know the fire classes: A, B, C and K

Every extinguisher label shows which classes of fire it's approved for, usually as letters and small pictures. The main US classes:

| Class | What's burning | Common examples at home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Ordinary combustibles | Wood, paper, cloth, cardboard, many plastics, trash |
| **B** | Flammable liquids and gases | Gasoline, oil-based paint, solvents, lighter fluid |
| **C** | Energized electrical equipment | Appliances, wiring, outlets, power tools while plugged in |
| **D** | Combustible metals | Mostly industrial (magnesium, lithium metal). Rare at home |
| **K** | Cooking oils and fats | Commercial deep fryers and restaurant kitchens |

The numbers in a rating such as **2-A:10-B:C** show relative firefighting capacity. A bigger number in front of the A or B means it can handle a larger fire of that type. Class C has no number. It means the agent doesn't conduct electricity.

Using the wrong type can make a fire worse. A water extinguisher (class A only) on a grease or electrical fire is the classic mistake.

## 3. Choose an ABC extinguisher for your home

A **multipurpose ABC dry chemical** extinguisher covers the fires you're most likely to have at home in one unit. That's why it's the usual home pick.

**What to look for:**

- **The rating.** A common recommendation for the main floor and kitchen area is **2-A:10-B:C or higher**. Smaller 1-A:10-B:C units are handy for a car, boat or workshop bench.
- **A listing mark** from a recognized testing lab on the label.
- **A pressure gauge** you can read at a glance.
- **A weight you can handle.** Home units are often around 5 lb of agent, and some people find 10 lb units too heavy to aim well.
- **Rechargeable or disposable.** Rechargeable units often have a metal valve head and can be refilled by a fire equipment service. Disposable units are replaced after use. The label tells you which you have.

Expect roughly **$20 to $60** for a typical home unit, more for larger rechargeable models.

**About the residue:** ABC powder spreads widely and can corrode metal and electronics if it's left sitting. Clean it up soon after use (see "After the fire is out" below). That's a small price compared with a house fire.

## 4. Never use water on grease or electrical fires

**Grease fires:** Water sinks under burning oil, boils instantly and throws flaming oil up and out. For a small fire in a pan:

1. **Turn off the burner** if you can reach it safely.
2. **Slide a metal lid or a cookie sheet over the pan** to smother it. Leave it covered until it has cooled completely.
3. **Don't carry the pan** to the sink or outside.
4. For a fire inside the oven or microwave, **turn it off and keep the door closed.**
5. If that doesn't work, use your ABC (or a kitchen-rated) extinguisher from a safe distance, or get out and call 911.

**Electrical fires:** Water conducts electricity, so it can shock you. If you can do it safely, **unplug the appliance or switch off the breaker**, then use an extinguisher with a C rating. Once the power is off, it burns like an ordinary class A or B fire.

## 5. Keep extinguishers where you can reach them

An extinguisher only helps if you can grab it in a few seconds.

- **One on every level** of the home, including the basement.
- **In or near the kitchen**, along your path out of the room, not right next to the stove. You don't want to reach over flames to get it.
- **In the garage** and any workshop, where fuel, solvents and tools are stored.
- **Near the bedrooms**, so you can protect an escape route.
- **Mounted on its bracket**, at a height everyone who might use it can reach. Not on the floor behind clutter, and not locked in a cabinet.

Make sure every adult in the home knows where each one is and has read the instructions on the label.

## 6. Check the gauge every month

A quick look once a month is enough for most homes:

1. **Gauge:** the needle should sit in the **green** zone. Toward the "recharge" side means low pressure. Toward the "overcharged" side also needs attention.
2. **Pin and tamper seal:** both in place and unbroken.
3. **Body, hose and nozzle:** no dents, rust, cracks or blockages.
4. **Label:** still readable, with the instructions facing out.
5. **Manual:** some makers suggest turning dry chemical units upside down or tapping them now and then so the powder doesn't pack. Follow your label.

Some extinguisher types, such as CO2, have no gauge and are checked by weight. These are uncommon in homes.

## 7. Replace or recharge after any use

Even a one-second test spray can let pressure leak out over time. After **any** use:

- **Disposable unit:** replace it.
- **Rechargeable unit:** have a fire equipment service recharge and inspect it.

Also replace a unit if the gauge is out of the green, it's damaged, or it has passed the service life on its label. Check with your local fire department about how to dispose of an old one.

## After the fire is out

- **Get the fire department to check it** even if it looks out. Fire can keep burning inside walls, cabinets or appliances.
- **Air out the room** and keep children and pets away from the powder.
- **Clean up dry chemical residue** soon: vacuum or sweep loose powder, then wipe surfaces with a damp cloth. Check the label or safety sheet for specific advice.
- **Don't use a burned appliance** again until a qualified repair person has checked it, and don't turn power back on to a burned outlet or circuit. Have an electrician look first.
- **Check your smoke alarms** are working. If one keeps chirping, see [why a smoke detector chirps](/home-safety/smoke-detector-chirping/).

## When to call the fire department

Call **911** right away, before or while you fight the fire, if:

- The fire is **bigger than a wastebasket** or spreading, or you're not sure.
- There's **heavy smoke**, or anyone is hurt or still inside.
- The fire involves **gas**: get out, leave the door closed and call from outside.
- An extinguisher **didn't put it out** or it flared back up.
- You put it out but smell smoke, see scorching or feel heat in a wall, ceiling or outlet.

And call them after any fire you put out yourself. Firefighters would much rather check a fire that's already out than arrive at one that has spread.
