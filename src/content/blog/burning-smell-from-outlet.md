A burning smell near an outlet means something is getting hot enough to scorch plastic, insulation or dust. Sometimes it's a device or cord. Sometimes it's the wiring behind the cover plate, and that's the kind that can start a fire inside a wall.

The most common causes are **a loose wire connection**, **an overloaded outlet or power strip**, **a damaged plug or cheap adapter**, and **a worn-out receptacle**. Deal with the danger first, then find the cause.

## What to do right now

If the smell continues with the breaker off, call 911, then an electrician" width="640" height="452" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Decide in seconds: get out, or cut the power.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Smoke, flames, crackling or scorch marks?** Get everyone out, close doors behind you if you can, and **call 911 from outside**. Don't try to unplug anything, and don't go back in.
2. **None of those?** Unplug whatever is in the outlet, **but only if the plug and cover plate are safe to touch**. If they're hot, melted or discolored, leave them and go straight to the breaker.
3. **Turn off the breaker** for that outlet. If you're not sure which one it is, turn off the main breaker. Don't stand in water to do it.
4. **Don't use the outlet** again until the cause is found and fixed. Tape over it or put a note on it so nobody else plugs in.
5. **Smell still there, or getting stronger, with the power off?** Call 911. Heat can keep building inside a wall where you can't see it.

> **Safety first:** Never open an outlet with the power on. Turn off the breaker and confirm the outlet is dead with a non-contact voltage tester before you remove the cover plate. If you see any scorching, melted plastic or blackened wires, stop and call a licensed electrician.

## How to find the source: quick check

Do this only once you've ruled out an emergency and the breaker is off.

1. **Sniff and look, don't touch first.** Is the smell strongest at the outlet, the plug, a power strip, a cord or the device itself?
2. **Check the plug and device** you unplugged. Look for brown or melted plastic on the plug, bent or darkened blades, or a hot charger or heater body.
3. **Check the cover plate and slots** with a flashlight. Brown or black marks around the slots, a warped plate or a crack all point to heat at the receptacle.
4. **Think about what was running.** A space heater, hair dryer, microwave or several things on one power strip point to overload (cause 2). A smell with nothing plugged in points to wiring (cause 1 or 7).
5. **Note the age of the house.** Silver-colored wiring in a home from roughly the mid-1960s to the mid-1970s points to aluminum wiring (cause 5).

## 1. A loose wire or backstabbed connection

A loose connection is the classic cause. When a wire doesn't make firm contact, the small contact area has higher [electrical resistance](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/what-is-resistance/), and current flowing through it turns into heat. Push-in "backstab" connections, where the wire is pushed into a hole on the back of the receptacle instead of wrapped around a screw, are a common weak spot as they loosen over time. Current for other outlets on the circuit can pass through these connections, so this outlet can smell even with nothing plugged in.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/burning-smell-from-outlet-heat-sources.svg" alt="Chart of five places a burning smell can start, from the device to the wall: device or charger and cord or power strip are things you can check yourself; plug blades and receptacle contacts you can check, then call a pro if damaged; wire connections and wiring in the wall need an electrician" width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Work from the device back toward the wall.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

- With the breaker off and the outlet tested dead, remove the cover plate and the receptacle screws and look at the connections.
- **Any scorching, melted insulation or brittle, discolored wire:** stop and call an electrician. Damaged wire often needs to be cut back or the box repaired.
- **Clean wires, just loose or backstabbed:** an electrician (or an experienced homeowner) can move them to the screw terminals, wrapped clockwise and tightened firmly, or replace the receptacle.

## 2. An overloaded outlet, power strip or extension cord

Every outlet, cord and power strip has a limit. A space heater, hair dryer, toaster oven or window air conditioner can draw close to what a typical 15 amp circuit can handle. Add more devices on a power strip or a thin extension cord and parts can overheat before the breaker trips.

**Fix:**

- Plug high-wattage appliances directly into a wall outlet, one per outlet, never into a power strip or light-duty extension cord.
- Check the label on cords and strips for their amp or watt rating and stay under it.
- Never plug one power strip into another (daisy-chaining).
- Replace any cord or strip that got hot, smells burnt or has melted spots.
- If you need more outlets than the room has, have an electrician add a circuit.

## 3. A damaged plug, cheap adapter or loose fit

Bent, loose or corroded plug blades touch the receptacle contacts over a small area, which creates heat right at the slot. Cheap travel adapters, cube taps and off-brand chargers may not be built to handle the heat. Look for a safety listing mark from a recognized testing lab on anything you plug in.

**Fix:**

- Replace any plug or adapter with discolored or melted plastic. Don't straighten and reuse a scorched plug.
- Swap cheap adapters and multi-plug cubes for a listed power strip with a built-in breaker.
- If a known-good plug also fits loosely, the receptacle is the problem (cause 4).

## 4. A worn receptacle

The metal contacts inside a receptacle are springs that grip the plug blades. After years of use, or heavy plugs pulling on them, they lose their grip. A plug that falls out or wiggles is touching only part of the contact, and that spot heats up. Some heat damage at the slots, like browned plastic, shows from the front.

**Fix:**

- Replace the receptacle. It's a common DIY job, but only with the breaker off and the outlet **verified dead with a tester**.
- Match the amp rating (15 or 20 amp, shown on the receptacle) and use the screw terminals, not the push-in holes.
- Our guide to a [warm or hot outlet](/home-safety/outlet-warm-hot/) covers the same warning signs in more detail.
- If you find scorching on the wires or inside the box, stop and call an electrician.

## 5. Aluminum wiring

Some homes built from roughly the mid-1960s to the mid-1970s used aluminum branch wiring. Aluminum expands and contracts more than copper and can oxidize at connections, so terminals can loosen and overheat. A standard receptacle connected to aluminum wire is a known fire risk.

**Fix:**

- Look at the wire jacket or bare wire in the panel or an outlet (power off). Silver-colored solid wire or "AL" printed on the jacket suggests aluminum.
- Don't replace these receptacles yourself. Only devices rated for aluminum (marked CO/ALR) or approved repair connectors should be used, and an electrician experienced with aluminum wiring should do the work.

## 6. A failing device: charger, heater or appliance

Sometimes the outlet is fine and the device is the problem. A failing phone or laptop charger, a space heater with a worn switch or cord, or an appliance with a burnt motor can all give off a burning smell near the outlet.

**Fix:**

- If the device body, power brick or cord is hot or smells, unplug it and stop using it.
- Test the outlet with a known-good lamp. If there's no smell, heat or flicker, the device is the likely cause.
- Replace damaged chargers with the maker's own or a listed replacement. Have heaters and appliances repaired or replaced.
- If the outlet sparked when you plugged in, see our guide on [outlets that spark when plugging in](/electrical/outlet-sparks-when-plugging-in/).

## 7. Arcing inside the box or wall

Arcing is electricity jumping a gap, for example at a loose connection, a nail through a cable or a chewed wire. It can sound like crackling, sizzling or buzzing and make lights flicker. Arcing creates intense heat in a small spot and can ignite insulation, wood framing or dust nearby.

**Fix:**

- Keep the breaker off and call an electrician. This isn't a DIY repair.
- Ask whether your circuits have **AFCI (arc-fault circuit interrupter)** protection. AFCI breakers are designed to recognize dangerous arcing and cut power. Newer homes often have them on many circuits, and an electrician can tell you whether adding them makes sense in yours.

## 8. Lookalike smells (and one that isn't)

Not every burning smell near an outlet is electrical, but one well-known "strange" smell is.

- **Fishy or urine-like smell:** often overheating plastic in an outlet, switch, breaker or device. This is electrical. Treat it like a burning smell.
- **Dusty burning smell from a heater:** a space heater, baseboard heater or furnace often smells briefly the first time it runs each season as dust burns off. It should fade quickly. If it doesn't, or the plug or outlet gets hot, it's not just dust.
- **New appliance smell:** a new heater, oven or tool can smell for its first few uses. It shouldn't come from the plug or outlet.
- **Rotten egg smell:** that's likely gas, a different emergency. Leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.

## Smell won't go away with the breaker off?

If the smell continues, gets stronger, or you notice warm spots on the wall, discolored paint or smoke from a cover plate, the heat may be inside the wall or on another circuit.

- **Call 911.** Firefighters can check inside walls for hidden heat. Get everyone out while you wait if there's any sign of smoke.
- Don't cut into the wall or remove more cover plates yourself.
- Once the fire department says it's safe, leave the power off to that area and have an electrician inspect before anything is reset.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- You see **any** scorching, melting or blackened wires, even if the smell is gone.
- The smell came from the outlet with nothing plugged in.
- Your home has aluminum wiring.
- You heard crackling or buzzing, or lights flicker on that circuit.
- The breaker trips when you restore power, or more than one outlet is affected.
- You're not fully comfortable replacing a receptacle with the power off and verified dead.

A burning smell is one of the clearest warnings an electrical problem gives. An inspection costs far less than the damage a hidden wiring fire can do.
