The cost of charging an EV at home comes down to one sum: **kWh from the wall times your rate per kWh**. The kWh you need depends on **how far you drive**, **how efficient your car is** (miles per kWh), and a little extra for **charging losses**.

Most people get the rate wrong by using only the headline energy price. Below you'll find how to get the real figure from your bill, a worked example with clearly stated assumptions, and how **time-of-use rates** can cut the cost.

## How to work out your own cost: quick steps

1. **Find your monthly miles.** Check the odometer a month apart or estimate from your commute.
2. **Find your car's miles per kWh** from the trip computer or app (or convert from kWh per 100 miles).
3. **Find your rate per kWh** from your bill, including delivery charges (step 2 below).
4. **Run the formula** and compare with your bill once you've charged for a full month.

## 1. The basic formula: kWh times your rate

Electricity is billed in **kilowatt-hours (kWh)**. If you put 40 kWh into the car and your rate is 16 cents per kWh, the energy costs 40 × $0.16 = $6.40 (before charging losses).

For a monthly figure, start from miles:

**kWh needed = miles ÷ miles per kWh**

**Cost = kWh needed ÷ charging efficiency × rate**

## 2. Find your real rate per kWh

Your bill usually splits the price into **supply** (or generation) and **delivery** (or distribution), and sometimes adds riders and taxes per kWh. Add all the per-kWh charges together for your real rate.

- **Fixed monthly charges** (a customer or service charge) don't go up when you charge an EV, so leave them out.
- **Tiered rates:** if kWh above a set amount cost more, EV charging may push you into the higher tier. Use that higher rate for your estimate.
- **Shortcut:** dividing the total bill by total kWh gives a rough average, but it slightly overstates the cost of extra kWh because it includes fixed charges.

## 3. Add charging losses

Not every kWh from the wall ends up in the battery. Some is lost as heat in the charger, the car's onboard charger and the battery, and some powers the car's electronics and battery heating or cooling while it charges.

- A common planning figure is **about 10 to 15% lost**, so efficiency of roughly 85 to 90%.
- **Level 1** charging is often a bit less efficient, because the car's systems stay awake for many more hours. See [Level 1 vs Level 2 charging](/ev-charging/level-1-vs-level-2-charging/) for the other differences.
- **Cold weather** adds losses when the car warms the battery before or during charging.

That's why the kWh shown in your car's app is usually lower than what your charger or utility meter records.

## 4. Know your car's miles per kWh

Efficiency varies a lot between vehicles and with how you drive:

- Smaller, efficient EVs often get around **3.5 to 4+ miles per kWh** in mild weather.
- Large SUVs and pickups often get around **2 to 3 miles per kWh**.
- **Winter, highway speed and towing** all cut miles per kWh, sometimes a lot.

Use your own car's average over a few weeks if you can. If your car shows **kWh per 100 miles**, divide 100 by that number to get miles per kWh (30 kWh per 100 miles is about 3.3 miles per kWh).

## Worked example: cost per month

These are **example assumptions**, not averages. Swap in your own numbers.

| Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Miles driven per month | 1,000 |
| Car efficiency | 3.5 miles per kWh |
| Charging efficiency | 90% |
| Electricity rate (all per-kWh charges) | 16 cents per kWh |

1. **kWh into the battery:** 1,000 ÷ 3.5 = about **286 kWh**.
2. **kWh from the wall:** 286 ÷ 0.90 = about **318 kWh**.
3. **Monthly cost:** 318 × $0.16 = about **$51**.
4. **Cost per mile:** $51 ÷ 1,000 = about **5 cents per mile**.

Same assumptions, one session: topping up a 60 kWh battery from 20% to 80% adds 36 kWh. Divided by 0.90, that's 40 kWh from the wall, or about **$6.40**.

## 5. Use time-of-use rates

Many utilities offer **time-of-use (TOU)** or dedicated **EV rates**, where electricity costs less overnight or at other off-peak times and more during the afternoon and evening peak. Peak hours, prices and rules differ by utility, so check your utility's rate page.

Hours vary by utility.</figcaption></figure>

**How to make the most of it:**

1. **Check which plans your utility offers** and whether you need an approved charger or a separate meter.
2. **Set a charging schedule** in the car or the charger app so it starts after off-peak begins. Use one schedule, not both, so they don't conflict.
3. **Make sure it finishes in time.** Level 2 usually fits in an off-peak window. Level 1 may not.
4. **Watch the rest of your home.** A TOU plan also prices your AC, dryer and oven by time of day. Compare a few months of bills before and after.

## 6. Other things that change the cost

- **Solar panels:** charging during the day from your own solar can be much cheaper, depending on how your utility credits exported power.
- **Free or workplace charging** lowers your home cost.
- **Preconditioning** the cabin while plugged in uses home power, but saves battery range for the drive.
- **Battery charge limit:** charging to a lower everyday limit doesn't cost more per mile. It just means fewer kWh per session.

## Your bill went up more than expected?

- **Check your charger app's kWh** against your estimate. If the wall kWh are far higher than the car's, losses or battery heating may be higher than you assumed, which is common in winter.
- **Check the schedule** actually starts in off-peak hours, especially after a time change or a software update.
- **Look at the whole bill.** Heating, cooling and other loads also change from month to month. See [electric bill suddenly high](/solar-power/electric-bill-suddenly-high/) for what else to check.
