A power outage is usually just an inconvenience, but a few things turn it into a real danger: **carbon monoxide from a generator or improvised heater**, **spoiled food**, **fires from candles** and **people who depend on powered medical equipment**. A bit of preparation takes care of most of it.

This checklist runs in three parts: what to have **before** the lights go out, what to do **during** the outage and what to check **after** power comes back.

> **Safety first:** Never run a generator, grill, camp stove or charcoal indoors, in a garage or near open windows. Don't heat your home with a gas oven or stove. Carbon monoxide has no smell and can kill. Stay well away from downed power lines and anything touching them, and report them to your utility or 911.

## Quick check: the first 10 minutes

1. **Is it just you?** Look out the window for neighbors' lights and check your breaker panel. If only part of your home is dark, it's likely a tripped breaker, not an outage.
2. **Report it** to your utility by app, website or phone. Don't assume someone else has.
3. **Switch to flashlights**, not candles.
4. **Keep the fridge and freezer closed.**
5. **Unplug sensitive electronics** and leave one lamp switched on so you'll know when power is back.

## 1. Before: build an outage kit

Put these in one easy-to-find bin or shelf:

- **Flashlights or LED lanterns** for each person, plus **spare batteries**. Headlamps leave your hands free.
- **A charged power bank** (or two) for phones. Recharge them after each use.
- **Water:** the common FEMA guideline is **1 gallon per person per day for at least 3 days**. Store extra if you're on a well, since well pumps need electricity.
- **Medicines:** keep a supply on hand and a small cooler with ice packs for anything that needs refrigeration. Check the label or ask your pharmacist how long it's OK warm.
- **A battery or hand-crank radio** for updates if cell service is down.
- **Shelf-stable food** that needs no cooking, a manual can opener and a first aid kit.
- **Some cash**, since card readers and ATMs may be down.

## 2. Before: prepare the fridge and freezer

- **Put an appliance thermometer in both.** After an outage, it's the only way to know whether food stayed cold enough. The fridge should be at **40°F or below** and the freezer at **0°F or below**.
- **Freeze containers of water or ice packs** ahead of a storm. They help keep food cold longer and give you drinking water as they melt.
- **Group food together** in the freezer. A full, packed freezer stays cold longer than a half-empty one.
- **Know where to buy ice** or dry ice nearby.

## 3. Before: plan for medical devices and backup power

If anyone in the home uses an oxygen concentrator, CPAP, powered wheelchair, home dialysis or refrigerated medicine, plan now:

- **Ask the doctor or equipment supplier** how long the device's battery lasts and what to do in a long outage.
- **Contact your utility.** Many have a medical needs or priority program. Ask what yours offers.
- **Pick a backup power source.** A battery **portable power station** runs quietly indoors and suits CPAPs, phones, lights and a fridge for a while. Size one with [how big a power station you need](/solar-power/what-size-power-station-do-i-need/). A gas **portable generator** runs larger loads but must stay outside.
- **Test your generator** a few times a year so it starts when you need it. If it won't, see [why a portable generator won't start](/electrical/portable-generator-wont-start/).

## 4. During: keep the fridge and freezer closed

This is the single most useful thing you can do for your food. General **FDA and USDA guidance** says that with the doors kept closed:

- A **refrigerator** keeps food safe for **about 4 hours**.
- A **full freezer** holds its temperature for **about 48 hours**, a **half-full freezer** for **about 24 hours**.

Your appliance, the room temperature and how full it is all make a difference, so treat these as rough guides and rely on your thermometers.

**If the outage will run longer:**

1. Move milk, meat and leftovers into a cooler with ice after about 4 hours.
2. Add ice or dry ice to the freezer if you can get it. Handle dry ice with gloves and keep the space ventilated.
3. Open doors only when you must, and close them quickly.

## 5. During: run a generator safely

Portable generators produce **carbon monoxide** fast. It has no color or smell.

- **Outdoors only.** Never in a home, garage, basement, crawl space or shed, even with doors or windows open.
- **Keep it well away from the house.** The CDC advises **at least 20 feet** from doors, windows and vents, with the exhaust pointed away from the home.
- **Have battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms** working on every level and near bedrooms.
- **If anyone feels dizzy, sick, weak or headachy**, get everyone into fresh air right away and call 911.
- **Never plug a generator into a wall outlet** to power the house ("backfeeding"). It can electrocute utility workers and neighbors. Use heavy-duty outdoor extension cords to individual appliances, or have an electrician install a **transfer switch** or interlock.
- **Keep it dry.** Run it on a dry surface under an open canopy, not in the rain, and touch it with dry hands.
- **Let it cool before refueling.** Gasoline on a hot engine can ignite. Store fuel in approved containers outside the house.

## 6. During: unplug electronics and stay safe

- **Unplug** computers, TVs, consoles, microwaves and anything with a circuit board. Power can come back with a surge or flicker on and off.
- **Leave one lamp switched on** so you notice when power returns.
- **Use flashlights instead of candles.** If you use candles, keep them away from curtains, kids and pets and never leave them burning unattended.
- **Don't heat the home with a gas oven, stove or outdoor heater.** In cold weather, layer clothing, close off unused rooms and gather in one room.
- **In hot weather**, stay hydrated, keep blinds closed and check on older neighbors. Go to a cooling center if it gets dangerous.
- **Garage doors:** most openers have a manual release cord. Check your manual so you know how to use it safely.
- **Driving:** treat dark traffic signals as an all-way stop.

## 7. After: check what food is safe

1. **Check the thermometers** before you open anything else.
2. **Throw out perishable food** (meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, soft cheese, cooked leftovers) that has been **above 40°F for 2 hours or more**.
3. **Freezer food** that still has **ice crystals** or reads **40°F or below** can generally be refrozen. Quality may suffer, but it's safe.
4. **Never taste food** to decide. Spoiled food can look and smell fine. **When in doubt, throw it out.**
5. **Medicines** that needed refrigeration: ask your pharmacist or doctor before using them.

## 8. After: reset breakers and check appliances

- **Plug things back in gradually**, starting with the most important, once power has been steady for a few minutes. That avoids a big inrush all at once.
- **Check the fridge and freezer** are running and cooling back down. Reset clocks and timers.
- **Check heating and cooling.** Some systems have a short built-in delay before restarting. If yours doesn't come back, check its switch and breaker, then the manual.
- **Restart your internet** if it doesn't come back on its own: modem first, then router.
- **Refill and recharge** your kit: batteries, power banks, water and fuel.

## Power back but part of the house is still dead?

- **Check the breaker panel** for any breaker sitting in the middle or OFF position. Push it fully to OFF, then back ON.
- **Press RESET on GFCI outlets** in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, outdoors and basements. They can trip when power comes back.
- **If you have a transfer switch or interlock**, make sure it's set back to utility power.
- **Ask a neighbor.** If they're also missing some lights, the utility may still be working on one leg of the service. Report it.

## When to call an electrician or your utility

Call your **utility** (or 911 for danger) if:

- A **power line is down** or sparking. Stay far away and keep others back.
- Your home is still out when neighbors have power, or lights are **unusually dim or bright**. That can be a problem on the utility side.

Call a **licensed electrician** if:

- A **breaker trips again** as soon as you reset it.
- You smell burning, see scorch marks or an outlet feels warm.
- Your home **flooded** or water reached outlets, appliances or the panel. Don't turn anything on until it's checked.
- You want a **transfer switch or interlock** installed for a generator.
