A leaking water heater can be anything from harmless condensation to a tank that's rusting through. The first job is to keep yourself safe and limit the water damage. The second is to find exactly where the water is coming from, because that tells you whether it's a cheap fix or a replacement.

The most common sources are **loose pipe connections at the top, the temperature and pressure relief valve, the drain valve near the bottom and, on older heaters, the tank itself**. Condensation on gas models and leaks from nearby pipes can fool you too.

> **Safety first:** Water in a tank heater can be hot enough to scald. Turn off the breaker (electric) or set the gas control to OFF or PILOT (gas) before you touch anything, and keep power off while the tank is low or empty, since an electric element can burn out in seconds without water around it. If you smell gas, leave the house and call your gas company or 911 from outside. Keep water away from the heater's wiring and burner.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Shut the heater down.** Turn off the breaker or set the gas control to OFF or PILOT, then close the cold water shutoff valve above the heater.
2. **Dry everything** with towels, including the floor, the top of the tank and the pipes.
3. **Wait and watch** with a flashlight for 15 to 30 minutes. Put a dry paper towel under each fitting to see where drops form.
4. **Find the highest wet point.** Water runs down, so the highest wet spot is usually the source.
    - **Top of the heater?** Pipe connections or the relief valve (causes 2, 3).
    - **Side discharge pipe?** The relief valve is opening (cause 2).
    - **Bottom front?** The drain valve or an element gasket (causes 4, 5).
    - **Under the tank with dry fittings?** Condensation or a failing tank (causes 1, 6).

## 1. Condensation, not a leak

On gas water heaters, burning gas makes water vapor. When the tank is full of cold water, for example after a big hot water draw or a refill, that vapor can condense on the cold tank and drip onto the burner. You may hear a sizzle.

**Fix:**

- Wait an hour or two for the tank to heat fully. If the dripping stops, it was condensation.
- If the water heater is undersized for your use, it may happen often. That's worth discussing with a plumber, but it isn't a leak.
- If water keeps collecting under the tank once it's hot, look at cause 6.

## 2. The temperature and pressure relief valve

The **T&P relief valve** is a safety device near the top of the tank with a pipe running down the side. It opens to release water if the tank gets too hot or the pressure too high. Most are rated to open around 150 psi or 210°F, but check the tag on yours.

**Fix:**

- Check the thermostat. Many households are comfortable at about 120°F, which also lowers scald risk and energy use.
- Test your water pressure with a gauge that screws onto an outdoor hose spigot (about $10 to $20). Most homes run roughly 40 to 80 psi. Higher readings need a plumber to check or install a pressure reducing valve.
- If your home has a check valve or pressure reducing valve, heated water has nowhere to expand. An **expansion tank** on the cold line solves this. Plumbers install them often.
- If pressure and temperature are normal and it still drips, the valve itself is worn. Replace it with one of the same rating. This is a common plumber job.
- **Never cap, plug or remove the relief valve or its discharge pipe.** Without it, an overheated tank can fail violently.

## 3. Loose or corroded pipe connections

The cold inlet and hot outlet at the top of the heater can loosen over time. Steel nipples and dielectric fittings can also corrode, especially where copper meets steel. Look for **white or green crust, rust stains or drips** on the pipes above the tank.

**Fix:**

- With the water off, gently snug a loose nut with two wrenches, one to hold the fitting and one to turn. Don't over-tighten.
- Replace a leaking flexible water heater connector. They're inexpensive and sold at hardware stores.
- Heavily corroded nipples or fittings at the tank should be replaced by a plumber, since they can snap when you turn them.

## 4. A leaking drain valve

The drain valve near the bottom is used for flushing sediment. Plastic drain valves can crack, and any drain valve can drip if it isn't fully closed or its washer is worn, especially if debris is stuck in it.

**Fix:**

- Make sure it's closed fully, hand tight.
- Thread a **garden hose cap** (or drain valve cap) onto the outlet to stop a slow drip.
- If it still leaks, the valve can be replaced, but that means draining the tank. With the power or gas off and cold water off, connect a hose, run it to a drain, and open a hot faucet to let air in.
- Leave this to a plumber if the valve is corroded in place or the tank is very old.

## 5. A heating element gasket (electric heaters)

Electric heaters have one or two heating elements behind small covers on the side of the tank. If the gasket behind an element leaks, you may see rust or water behind the cover or running down the side.

**Fix:**

- Turn off the breaker and confirm the power is off with a non-contact voltage tester before you remove a cover. These heaters run on 240 V.
- If you see water around the element, it usually needs a new gasket or element. That means draining the tank below the element and using an element wrench.
- If you're not comfortable with 240 V wiring and draining a tank, call a plumber or an appliance technician.

## 6. The tank itself is leaking

Inside the jacket, a tank heater is a steel tank lined with glass and protected by an **anode rod** that corrodes instead of the tank. Over years the anode wears away and sediment builds up, and eventually the tank rusts through. Water then seeps out from under the jacket or bottom.

**Fix:**

- Check the fittings and valves first. If they're all dry and water still pools under the tank, the tank is very likely failing.
- A leaking tank can't be patched. Keep the water and power or gas off, and plan to replace the heater.
- Look at the rating label for the age and size of your heater to help price a replacement. Ask your plumber about the right size and fuel for your home.

## 7. The water is coming from somewhere else

Water heaters often sit next to furnaces, air conditioners, water softeners and laundry. A furnace or AC condensate drain, a sweating cold pipe or a leaking softener can drip onto the floor and look like a heater leak.

**Fix:**

- Dry the heater and its pipes completely and watch for where new water appears.
- Check nearby condensate lines and drain pans, the softener and the pipes in the ceiling above.
- If the heater stays dry, it isn't the source.

## Water pouring out or won't stop?

- **Close the cold water shutoff** on the pipe above the heater. If it won't turn or it isn't stopping the water, close your home's **main water shutoff**.
- **Turn off the power or gas** if you haven't already.
- **Open a hot faucet** nearby and connect a garden hose to the drain valve to drain the tank to a floor drain or outside, if you can do so safely. The water may be very hot.
- **Call a plumber.** Many offer emergency service.

## When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if:

- The tank itself is leaking, or the bottom of the jacket is wet with no other source.
- The relief valve drips often or discharges a lot of water.
- Pipes or fittings at the top are heavily corroded.
- You smell gas, see scorch marks, or the burner area is wet on a gas heater.
- Your water pressure reads high, or you need an expansion tank or pressure reducing valve.
