Hearing water running when every tap is off is unsettling. Sometimes it is harmless, like a drain carrying water from upstairs. Sometimes it is a leak quietly soaking a wall or the ground under your slab.

The most common causes are **a toilet that keeps refilling**, **the water heater or an appliance filling**, and **a leaking supply pipe in a wall or under the floor**. Noises from **water hammer** and **drains shared with a neighbor** sound similar but are not leaks. The water meter test sorts these out in about 15 minutes.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>Turn off all water use and watch the leak indicator on your water meter. If it moves, water is flowing: check toilets first, then the water heater, appliances and irrigation, and call a plumber if you can't find it. > **Safety first:** Water and electricity don't mix. If water is coming through a ceiling or wall near lights, outlets or a panel, stay out of standing water and don't touch wet electrical parts. Never stand under a ceiling that is sagging or bulging with water. Know where your main shutoff valve is before you need it.

## How to tell if water is really flowing: the meter test

Your water meter measures every drop entering the house, so it separates a real leak from a noise.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/running-water-sound-in-walls-meter-test.svg" alt="Flowchart: turn off every tap and appliance and watch the meter leak indicator for 15 minutes; if it does not move, the sound is not a supply leak; if it moves, close the main shutoff; if the meter still moves the leak is in the service line outside, otherwise it is inside the house" width="640" height="560" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Closing the main valve tells you whether the leak is outside or inside.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Stop all water use.** Turn off faucets, showers, the washer and dishwasher, irrigation timers and the ice maker. Ask everyone at home not to flush.
2. **Find the meter.** It is usually in a covered box near the curb or sidewalk, or in a basement, garage or utility room where the line enters. Some lids need a screwdriver or meter key.
3. **Watch the leak indicator for 15 minutes.** This is a small triangle, star or tiny dial that turns with very small flows. No indicator? Write down the reading, use no water for 1 to 2 hours, and compare. Some smart meters show a leak alert on the display or the utility's website.
4. **If it moves, close the main shutoff valve** inside the house and watch again.
    - **Still moving?** The leak is between the meter and your valve, on the service line outside (or the valve itself is leaking through).
    - **Stopped?** The leak or the running fixture is inside the house. Work through causes 1 to 6.
5. **If it never moved,** water is not flowing into your home. Jump to causes 7 and 8.

## 1. A running toilet

This is the number one culprit. A worn flapper lets water seep from the tank into the bowl. When the level drops, the fill valve tops it up for a few seconds, and you hear that refill through the pipe in the wall.

**Fix:**

1. **Dye test:** put a few drops of food coloring or a dye tablet in the tank (not the bowl). Don't flush for 15 to 30 minutes.
2. If color shows up in the bowl, the **flapper** is leaking. Clean the seat and replace the flapper. They are inexpensive, usually a few dollars to around $15.
3. If water is spilling into the **overflow tube**, the fill valve is set too high or not shutting off. Lower the float so the water stops at the line marked inside the tank, usually about 1 inch below the top of the overflow tube. Replace the fill valve if it still runs.
4. Flush afterward so the dye doesn't stain.

Our full guide on a [toilet that keeps running](/plumbing/toilet-keeps-running/) covers every part step by step.

## 2. Water heater refilling or a dripping relief valve

After anyone uses hot water, cold water flows into the tank to refill it. That is normal and stops once the tank is full. It is not normal to hear it refilling when no one has used hot water.

**Fix:**

- Check the **temperature and pressure (T&P) relief valve** discharge pipe, which runs down the side of the tank, often to near the floor or outside. If it is wet or dripping, the valve is opening or worn.
- Never cap or plug that pipe. A relief valve that keeps opening can point to excess pressure, thermal expansion or overheating, so have a plumber check it.
- Look at the **drain valve** near the bottom of the tank and the pipe connections on top for drips.
- A water heater that seems to run all the time with no hot water use can also be a sign of a leak on a hot water line, including a slab leak (cause 6).

If you find water around the tank, see our guide on a [water heater leaking](/appliances/water-heater-leaking/).

## 3. An ice maker, softener or filter system filling

These appliances use water on their own schedule:

- **Refrigerator ice maker:** fills for a few seconds each time it makes a batch.
- **Water softener:** regenerates every so often, often overnight, and sends water to the drain for a while. A stuck control valve can run water nonstop.
- **Reverse osmosis filter under the sink:** sends some water to the drain while it refills its tank. If its shutoff fails, it can trickle to the drain constantly.
- **Furnace humidifier:** some models run water through a pad while the heat is on.

**Fix:** Close the supply valve to one appliance at a time and repeat the meter test. If the flow stops, you have found it. Check that appliance's manual for its normal cycle before assuming it is faulty.

## 4. Irrigation, hose bibs and outdoor lines

A leaking sprinkler valve can let water weep into one zone around the clock, and a timer can run a zone when you're not expecting it.

**Fix:**

- Turn off the irrigation system's own shutoff valve and repeat the meter test.
- Walk the yard for soggy patches or one sprinkler head that drips constantly.
- Watch for a **frost-free outdoor faucet** that cracked during a freeze. Its valve sits inside the wall, so a split tube may leak only when the hose is turned on, and that water goes into the wall or basement.

## 5. A supply pipe leaking inside a wall

If the meter moves with every fixture and appliance ruled out, suspect a pressurized supply pipe. Pinholes in old copper, a failed fitting or a nail or screw through a pipe can all cause a steady hiss that never stops.

**Fix:**

- Listen along the wall to find where the sound is loudest. Look for stains, bubbling paint, soft or swollen drywall, warped baseboards or a musty smell.
- A moisture meter can confirm damp drywall without cutting into it.
- Close the main valve when you aren't using water until the leak is repaired.
- Call a plumber. Many use acoustic gear and thermal cameras to pinpoint the leak. Don't cut into a wall blindly: wiring may be behind it.

## 6. A slab leak under the floor

In homes built on a concrete slab, supply lines often run under the floor. A leak there can sound like faint running water under a room.

Signs include a **warm spot on a tile or wood floor** (when the hot line leaks), damp carpet, new floor cracks, a water heater that runs more than usual and a water bill that is higher than the same month last year.

**Fix:** This is a plumber's job. Options include repairing the pipe through the slab or rerouting the line through walls or the attic. Costs vary widely, so get more than one quote and check what your insurance covers.

## 7. Water hammer and noisy pipes (not a leak)

A sharp **bang, thud or knock** right after a faucet, washer, dishwasher or ice maker shuts off is water hammer. Fast-closing valves stop the moving water suddenly, and the pressure surge shakes the pipes. It is not a running sound, and the meter stays still afterward.

**Fix:**

- Install **water hammer arrestors** on the washer hoses or at the problem fixture.
- Secure loose pipes with straps or pipe clamps where you can reach them (basement, crawlspace).
- Check your pressure with a gauge on an outdoor faucet. Household pressure is commonly around 40 to 80 psi. If it is higher, a plumber may recommend a pressure reducing valve.
- Older homes may have air chambers that fill with water over time. Shutting the main and draining the house from the highest and lowest faucets can restore them.

## 8. Drain and vent sounds from upstairs or next door

Drain pipes carry a lot of water past your walls. A whoosh or gurgle that starts when someone upstairs flushes or showers, or that you hear in a condo, townhouse or duplex with shared walls, is usually a drain line. Rain running through interior downspouts and an air conditioner's condensate line can sound similar.

**Fix:** If your meter doesn't move, there is no supply leak. Note when the sound happens. If it matches a neighbor's routine, it's their drain. If you share a water meter with other units, ask building management to check for leaks instead.

## Water coming through a ceiling or wall?

Act fast:

1. **Close the main shutoff valve.** It is usually where the water line enters the house, often near the water heater, in a basement, crawlspace, garage or utility closet. In some warm-climate homes it is outside.
2. **Open a faucet at the lowest point** of the house to relieve pressure in the pipes.
3. **Turn off power** to the wet area at the breaker panel, but only if you can reach the panel while standing on a dry floor. If not, call an electrician or your utility.
4. **Stay out from under** a sagging or bulging ceiling.
5. Photograph the damage, move belongings, and call a plumber and your insurer.

## When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if:

- The meter moves with every fixture and appliance turned off, and you can't find the source.
- You see wet drywall, stains, mold or a warm patch on the floor.
- The T&P valve on the water heater is dripping or discharging.
- The meter keeps moving with the main closed. The service line from the meter to the house is often the owner's responsibility, but this varies, so call your water utility to confirm who handles it.
- Pipes bang loudly and your pressure reads higher than about 80 psi.
