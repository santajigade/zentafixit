A portable generator that won't start is almost always a small engine problem, not an electrical one. The engine needs **fuel**, **air** in the right mix, **a spark** and enough **oil** to satisfy its safety sensor.

The most common causes are **a closed fuel valve or empty tank**, **the choke in the wrong position**, **low oil tripping the low-oil shutoff**, and **stale fuel gumming up the carburetor**. Work through them in order: the easy checks fix most generators.

> **Safety first:** Carbon monoxide from a generator can kill in minutes, and you can't see or smell it. Run and test it **outdoors only**, never in a garage, basement or shed, even with the door open. Common guidance is to keep it **at least 20 feet from the house**, with the exhaust pointing away from windows, doors and vents. Check your manual, which may say more. Let the engine cool before refueling, and wipe up spilled gas.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Unplug every cord** from the generator and turn its main breaker off, if it has one. A load can bog the engine down as it tries to start.
2. **Do the basics:** fuel in the tank, fuel valve open, engine switch ON, oil at the full mark, generator on level ground.
3. **Set the choke** to CHOKE or START for a cold engine and pull the cord with long, steady pulls (or press the start button).
4. **Notice what happens:**
    - **No firing at all**, no sputter: suspect spark (cause 6), the oil sensor (cause 3) or no fuel reaching the engine (causes 1 and 5).
    - **Fires, sputters or runs a second, then dies:** suspect choke (cause 2) or stale fuel and a dirty carburetor (cause 5).
    - **Starter just clicks or turns slowly:** the battery (cause 7).

## 1. Out of fuel, fuel valve closed or the wrong fuel setting

It sounds obvious, but it's the top cause. Many generators have a **fuel valve** (or fuel shutoff) between the tank and the carburetor, and it's often turned off for storage. Some also have a **vent** on the gas cap that must be open, or the engine starves after a few seconds.

**Fix:**

- Check the tank level with your eyes, not just the gauge.
- Turn the **fuel valve to ON** or OPEN.
- Open the **gas cap vent** if your model has one.
- **Dual-fuel models:** make sure the fuel selector matches the fuel you're using. On propane, check that the tank isn't empty and its valve is fully open.

## 2. Choke in the wrong position or flooded engine

The choke richens the fuel mix so a cold engine can start. With the choke open, a cold engine may pop and die. With the choke left closed too long, or after many pulls, the engine floods with fuel and won't fire. A strong smell of gas is the clue.

**Fix:**

1. **Cold engine:** choke closed (CHOKE or START), pull until it fires, then move the choke gradually to RUN over the next 30 seconds or so as it warms.
2. **Warm engine:** start with the choke open (RUN).
3. **Flooded:** move the choke to RUN, wait about 10 to 15 minutes for the extra fuel to evaporate, then pull several times with the choke open.

## 3. Low oil level or not level ground

Most portable generators have a **low-oil shutoff**: a sensor that stops the engine from starting, or kills it, when oil is low. On some models an oil warning light blinks while you pull the cord. The sensor can also trip when the generator sits on a slope, even if the oil is fine.

**Fix:**

- Move the generator to **level ground**.
- Check the oil with the dipstick or fill cap, as your manual describes. Top up with the **oil type in your manual** to the full mark. Don't overfill.
- Small engines burn some oil, so check it before every use, not only when it won't start.

## 4. Engine switch off or a load plugged in

Many generators have an **engine switch** (ON/OFF, sometimes combined with the fuel valve or choke in one knob). If it's OFF, the ignition is grounded and you'll never get a spark. A heavy load plugged in at startup can also stall the engine.

**Fix:**

- Turn the engine switch or knob to **ON** or START.
- Unplug everything and switch off the generator's output breakers. Start it, let it warm up for a few minutes, then add loads one at a time, largest first.

## 5. Stale fuel and a gummed-up carburetor

This is the most common reason a generator that ran fine last season won't start now. Gasoline breaks down in storage, and ethanol blends attract moisture. Over a few months it can leave **varnish** in the carburetor's tiny jets, so the engine starves even with a full tank. Signs: it sputters, runs only with the choke on, or surges up and down.

**Fix:**

1. **Drain the old fuel** from the tank. Many carburetors have a **drain screw** on the float bowl: open it (engine cool, outdoors, a container underneath) to empty the bowl too.
2. **Refill with fresh gas** and add fuel stabilizer if you'll store it again.
3. Try to start it. Spraying **carburetor cleaner** into the air intake (follow the can's directions) can sometimes break up light deposits.
4. If it still won't run properly, the carburetor needs a **full cleaning or replacement**. That means taking it apart, which is a common job at small engine repair shops.

**Prevent it next time:** use stabilized fresh fuel, and before storage either run the generator dry or drain the tank and carburetor as your manual recommends. Start it every month or so and let it run with a light load for a while.

## 6. A fouled or worn spark plug

If the engine doesn't even try to fire, check for a spark. Plugs foul with carbon or oil, and their gap wears over time.

**Fix:**

1. With the engine off and cool, pull off the plug wire and remove the plug with a **spark plug socket**.
2. Look at the tip. **Black and sooty, oily, wet with fuel, or a cracked insulator** all mean replace it. A new plug usually costs just a few dollars.
3. Set the gap to the figure in your manual and fit the plug type it specifies. Thread it in by hand first so you don't cross-thread it.
4. To check the ignition, use an **inline spark tester** between the plug and its wire. Don't hold a plug against the engine by hand to look for a spark.
5. No spark with a new plug and the engine switch ON points to the ignition coil or the low-oil sensor circuit. That's a job for a repair shop.

## 7. Dead battery on an electric start model

Electric start models use a small battery that drains in storage. A click, a slow crank, or nothing at all when you press the button usually means a flat battery.

**Fix:**

- **Use the recoil cord.** Most electric start generators also have a pull start.
- Charge the battery with the charger that came with the generator, or one suited to that battery type (check your manual).
- Check the battery connections for corrosion or loose terminals.
- If it won't hold a charge, replace it. Many generators recharge the battery while running, but a battery that's fully dead may not recover.

## Starts, then dies right away?

- **Choke:** it may need the choke on a little longer, or it's flooding because the choke was left on (cause 2).
- **Low oil or a slope** (cause 3). The low-oil shutoff can let the engine start, then stop it.
- **Fuel starving:** a closed gas cap vent, a partly closed fuel valve, or varnish in the carburetor (causes 1 and 5).
- **Overload:** something plugged in. Start with nothing connected.
- **CO shutoff:** many newer generators have a **carbon monoxide safety shutoff**. If yours shuts down and shows a CO warning, move it farther from the building into open air, and check your manual before restarting.

## Never backfeed your house

Don't plug a generator into a wall outlet or dryer outlet with a double-male cord to power the house. This "backfeeding" can send power out onto the utility lines, where it can injure or kill line workers, and it can start a fire or damage your generator when grid power returns. The safe way is a **transfer switch** or **panel interlock kit** installed by a licensed electrician, usually with a permit. Otherwise, run heavy-duty extension cords from the generator to individual appliances. To work out how big a generator you need for those loads, try this [generator size calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/generator-size/).

Also fit **battery-powered CO alarms** inside your home. If anyone feels dizzy, sick or weak while the generator runs, get everyone into fresh air and call 911. For more outage planning, see the [power outage checklist](/home-safety/power-outage-checklist/).

## When to call a small engine repair shop

Take it to a small engine repair shop (or the brand's authorized service center if it's under warranty) if:

- There's **no spark** with a new plug and the engine switch ON.
- It runs only with the choke on after you've replaced the fuel, which usually means the carburetor needs a rebuild.
- The recoil cord is **stuck, won't retract or feels locked**. That can be a seized engine or hydraulic lock and shouldn't be forced.
- You see **fuel leaking**, or it makes knocking or grinding noises.

And call a **licensed electrician**, not a mechanic, to connect a generator to your home's wiring.
