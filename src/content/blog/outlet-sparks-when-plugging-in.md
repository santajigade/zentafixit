A spark when you push in a plug looks alarming, but it isn't always a problem. The moment the metal blades touch the outlet's contacts, current starts to flow. If the device is already switched on, a little electricity can jump the last tiny gap and make a quick blue flash.

When the spark is **large, yellow or orange, or happens again and again**, something is wrong. The usual causes are **a worn-out receptacle with loose contacts**, **a damaged plug**, **a loose wire behind the outlet** or **moisture**. Here's how to tell the difference and what to do.

> **Safety first:** Never touch a plug or outlet with wet hands, and don't pull a melted or smoking plug out with bare hands. Turn off the breaker first. Before removing a cover plate, confirm the outlet is dead with a non-contact voltage tester. If you're not comfortable working on outlets, stop after the visual checks and call a licensed electrician.

## How to tell if the spark is normal: quick test

1. **Think about the spark itself.** Was it a tiny blue flash for a split second, or a larger yellow or orange spark, a pop, or several sparks?
2. **Check for after-effects.** Sniff for a burning or hot plastic smell, listen for buzzing or crackling, and look at the outlet and plug blades with a flashlight for black or brown marks.
3. **Feel the cover plate** (dry hands, a light touch) after a few minutes of use. It should be about the same temperature as the wall.
4. **Test the fit.** Plug in a known-good lamp with its switch off. The plug should go in firmly and stay put. If it wobbles or slides out, the receptacle is worn.
5. **Repeat with the device off.** Switch the device off, plug it in, then switch it on. If the spark disappears, it was likely normal inrush (cause 1). If it still sparks, keep reading.

## 1. Normal arcing from a device that was switched on

Anything that starts drawing current the instant it gets power, like a vacuum, hair dryer, space heater or power tool left in the on position, can make a small spark as the blades meet the contacts. Chargers, laptop bricks and TVs can do it too, even without an on switch, because their internal capacitors charge up in a quick burst.

**Fix:**

- Switch devices off before you plug them in or unplug them.
- Push the plug in with one smooth, firm motion. Wiggling it in slowly lets it arc longer.
- For electronics you plug and unplug often, use a power strip with its own switch and connect things while the strip is off.
- If the spark is tiny, happens once and leaves no smell or mark, there is nothing to repair.

## 2. A worn-out receptacle with loose contacts

Inside each slot are spring metal contacts that should grip the plug blade tightly. After years of use, especially with heavy plugs or chargers that hang from the outlet, those springs lose tension. A loose grip leaves tiny gaps where electricity arcs, and that arcing produces heat.

**Fix:**

- Signs of a worn receptacle: plugs slide in with almost no resistance, droop or fall out, or the plug face is discolored.
- Replace the receptacle. A standard outlet costs only a few dollars. Here's [how to replace an outlet](/electrical/how-to-replace-an-outlet/) step by step, with the breaker off and the power verified dead.
- Match the replacement to the circuit (15 A or 20 A) and to the type you're removing, such as GFCI or tamper-resistant. Check the label on the old one and on the breaker.

## 3. A damaged plug or cord

Bent, loose or pitted blades make poor contact, so they arc in even a healthy outlet. Blackened tips are a sign that arcing has already happened. A cracked plug body or a cord that's frayed where it enters the plug can also spark or short.

**Fix:**

- Look at the blades. They should be straight, firm and clean. Dark pitting or melted plastic means stop using the device.
- Try the device in another outlet in a different room. If it sparks there too, the device or its cord is the problem.
- Replace damaged cords and plugs, or the whole device for anything with a molded plug. Don't tape over cracks.
- Never file down the wider blade of a polarized plug or force a three-prong plug into a two-slot outlet with a cheap adapter.

## 4. A loose wire connection behind the outlet

If an outlet sparks with every device you try, even ones that work fine elsewhere, the problem may be behind the cover plate. A loose screw terminal, or a wire pushed into a "backstab" hole that has loosened, can arc whenever current flows through it. You might also notice flickering, crackling or a warm cover plate. Heat is covered in more detail in [why an outlet feels warm or hot](/home-safety/outlet-warm-hot/).

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the breaker and confirm the outlet is dead with a non-contact voltage tester.
2. Remove the cover plate and the mounting screws, then gently pull the receptacle out.
3. Look for loose wires, darkened insulation, melted plastic or a burnt smell.
4. If you see damage, leave the breaker off and call an electrician. If everything looks clean and you're experienced, tighten the terminals or move backstabbed wires to the side screws.

## 5. Water or moisture in the outlet

Outlets in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, basements and outdoors can collect moisture from steam, splashes, rain or a nearby leak. Water gives electricity an easy path, which can mean sparks, a tripped GFCI or a shock.

**Fix:**

- Don't plug anything into an outlet that's wet or that sits in standing water.
- Turn off the breaker and let the outlet dry completely before using it again.
- Fit a weatherproof "in-use" cover on outdoor outlets so they stay covered even with a cord plugged in.
- If water keeps getting in, or the outlet has corrosion inside, have it replaced and the source of the water fixed.

## 6. A high-draw appliance on a worn or overloaded outlet

Space heaters, window air conditioners, microwaves and hair dryers pull a lot of current. A bigger current makes a bigger spark if the plug goes in while the appliance is on, and it stresses any weak contact. Adding them to an outlet already running several things, or to a power strip, makes it worse.

**Fix:**

- Plug high-draw appliances directly into a wall outlet, not a power strip or light-duty extension cord.
- Run only one heating appliance per outlet, and switch it off before plugging in.
- If an outlet sparks or warms up under these loads, replace it and check that the circuit isn't overloaded.

## 7. A short circuit in the device

A loud pop with a bright flash, often followed by a tripped breaker, usually means a short: hot and neutral (or ground) are touching somewhere in the device or cord. That's not normal inrush.

**Fix:**

- Leave the device unplugged. Don't keep resetting the breaker and trying again.
- Check the outlet for black marks. If it's damaged, leave the breaker off and replace the outlet.
- Have the device repaired or replace it. If the breaker trips with nothing plugged in, see an electrician.

## Big spark, smoke or a burning smell?

Act right away:

1. **If there are flames or smoke you can't stop, get everyone out and call 911.** Never use water on an electrical fire.
2. **Turn off the breaker** for that outlet, or the main breaker if you're not sure which one, as long as you can reach the panel safely.
3. **Unplug the device only after the power is off**, and use a dry cloth or glove if the plug is hot or melted.
4. **Leave the breaker off** until an electrician has checked the outlet and the wiring behind it.
5. **If the burning smell continues** after the power is off, or the wall feels warm, call the fire department. Wiring can smolder inside a wall.

If the outlet now seems dead after the spark, check for a tripped breaker or GFCI before assuming the worst. Our guide to an [outlet that stopped working](/electrical/outlet-not-working/) walks through it.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The outlet sparks with **more than one device**, or sparks again after you switch devices off first.
- You see **scorch marks, melting or discoloration** on the outlet, cover plate or plug blades.
- You hear **buzzing or crackling**, or the outlet feels **warm**.
- You smell **burning or hot plastic**, even once.
- The breaker trips when you plug something in, or several outlets have the same problem.
- You find loose or damaged wires behind the outlet, or you're not comfortable replacing it yourself.
