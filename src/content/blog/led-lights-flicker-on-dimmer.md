LED bulbs use a fraction of the power of the incandescent bulbs most dimmers were designed for. The electronics inside each LED have to make sense of the chopped-up power a dimmer sends, and when the bulb and dimmer don't agree, you get **flicker, buzzing, a faint glow when off, or lights that drop out at the low end**.

The cause is usually **non-dimmable bulbs**, an **old incandescent dimmer**, or **too little load** for the dimmer. Most fixes take a new bulb, a new dimmer, or a small trim adjustment.

> **Safety first:** Checking bulbs is safe with the light off and cool. Before removing a dimmer's wall plate, turn off the breaker and confirm the wires are dead with a non-contact voltage tester. A dimmer that's **hot, smells burnt or is discolored** should be switched off at the breaker and replaced.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Read the bulb base or box.** Does it say "dimmable"?
2. **Read the dimmer.** Remove the knob or look under the wall plate edge for markings like "LED", "CFL/LED" or an LED wattage rating.
3. **Slide or turn the dimmer to full.**
    - **Still flickers at full?** Suspect a loose bulb or connection (cause 7).
    - **Smooth at full, flickers lower down?** The problem is the dimmer and bulb match, the trim or the load (causes 2 to 6).
4. **Swap in one bulb of a different brand** that's marked dimmable. If it behaves, the original bulbs are the issue.

| What you see | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| Flicker or strobe at every setting | Non-dimmable bulbs (cause 1) |
| Buzz from the bulbs or the switch | Old incandescent dimmer (cause 2) |
| Faint glow when off | Load below the dimmer's minimum (cause 3) |
| Drops out or shimmers at the low end | Trim set too low (cause 4) |
| One bulb misbehaves, others fine | Mixed bulb models (cause 6) |
| Flicker at full brightness | Loose bulb or wiring (cause 7) |

## 1. The bulbs aren't dimmable

Many LED bulbs, especially low-cost multipacks, are **non-dimmable**. On a dimmer they flicker, strobe, buzz and often fail early. Their internal driver isn't built to handle dimmed power.

**Fix:**

- Look for "dimmable" on the bulb base or packaging. If it's not there, assume it isn't.
- Replace them with **dimmable** LEDs.
- If you don't need to dim that light, have the dimmer replaced with a regular switch.

## 2. An old incandescent dimmer

Older dimmers were made for incandescent bulbs drawing 40 to 100 watts each. Many use a dimming method that LED drivers handle badly, which causes **buzzing**, flicker and a narrow dimming range. The buzz may come from the bulbs, the dimmer, or both.

**Fix:**

- Replace the dimmer with one marked for **LED** use (often labeled LED, CFL/LED or LED+). Some are **trailing-edge** or **ELV** types, which many LEDs dim more smoothly on. Check the bulb box for which type it prefers.
- New dimmers typically cost about $20 to $60. Smart dimmers cost more.
- Wiring a dimmer is a common DIY job, but if you're not comfortable working in a switch box, an electrician can do it quickly.

## 3. Too little load for the dimmer

Dimmers have a **minimum load**. A dimmer that expects a few hundred watts of incandescent bulbs may not work properly with one or two LEDs drawing 8 or 9 watts each. Symptoms include flicker at low settings, a **faint glow when off**, or bulbs flashing now and then.

**Fix:**

- Check the dimmer's instructions for its minimum load.
- **Add bulbs** to the circuit if the fixture allows.
- Use a dimmer with a **low or no minimum load** for LEDs. Dimmers that use a **neutral wire** often handle very small loads better. Check whether your switch box has a neutral (a white wire bundle) before buying one.

## 4. The low-end trim is set wrong

Many LED dimmers have a small **trim dial** or a programming mode that sets the lowest brightness. If it's set too low for your bulbs, they flicker, shimmer or cut out at the bottom of the range, or won't turn back on from a low setting.

**Fix:**

1. Find the trim control. It's often a tiny dial on the side of the dimmer, behind the wall plate or under a removable faceplate.
2. Set the dimmer to its **lowest** position.
3. Turn the trim up slowly until the lights are **steady**, then a little more.
4. Some smart and digital dimmers set this in an app or with a button sequence instead. Check the dimmer's manual.

## 5. The bulb and dimmer aren't a tested match

Even dimmable LEDs and LED-rated dimmers don't always get along. The major dimmer makers publish **compatibility lists** showing which bulbs they've tested, often with notes about performance at low levels.

**Fix:**

- Look up your dimmer model on the maker's website and find its LED compatibility list or tool.
- Pick a bulb from the list, or a dimmer that lists your bulb.
- Buy one test bulb first before replacing a whole room.

## 6. Mixed bulbs or too much LED load

Different bulb brands on one dimmer can each dim differently, so one flickers while the others are fine. At the other end, dimmers have a **maximum LED load** that is usually much lower than their incandescent rating. Going over it can cause flicker and overheating.

**Fix:**

- Use **the same bulb model** in every socket on a dimmer.
- Add up the actual LED wattage and keep it under the dimmer's **LED rating**, which is printed on the dimmer or its packaging.

## 7. A loose bulb or connection

If a light flickers even at **full brightness**, or a tap on the fixture changes it, the problem is likely a connection, not dimming. A bulb that's not fully screwed in, a bent socket tab, or a loose wire at the dimmer or fixture can all do this.

**Fix:**

- With the light off and cool, **tighten each bulb** gently.
- With the breaker off, check that the brass tab in the bottom of the socket isn't flattened. Lift it slightly with a small screwdriver if needed.
- A loose wire at the dimmer or fixture can arc and heat up. If you're not experienced, have an electrician check and tighten the connections.

## Flickering in more than one room?

If lights flicker in **several rooms**, including ones without dimmers, or they dim when a large appliance starts, the problem isn't the dimmer. It could be a loose connection in the panel, a shared neutral issue, or a supply problem. Call an electrician, and your utility if the whole house is affected. (For the engineering side of how LEDs turn current into light, see this explainer on [how an LED works](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/led-working-principle/).)

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The dimmer is **hot**, discolored, smells burnt or buzzes loudly.
- Lights flicker across multiple rooms or the whole house.
- You need a dimmer that requires a **neutral wire** and there isn't one in the box.
- You're not comfortable replacing a dimmer or working on a three-way (two-switch) circuit.

In most homes, the fix is simply dimmable bulbs on an LED-rated dimmer with the trim set right.
