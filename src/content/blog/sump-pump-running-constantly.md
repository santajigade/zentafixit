A sump pump that runs all the time, or switches on and off every few minutes, is either working hard against a lot of water or stuck in a fault. Either way it's worth checking, because constant running wears out the motor, and a pump that fails during a storm can flood your basement.

The most common causes are **a float switch stuck in the on position, a failed check valve that lets water flow back into the pit, a blocked discharge line, or simply a lot of groundwater** after heavy rain. Here's how to tell which.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>Look in the pit. If it's empty and the pump still runs, the float switch is stuck or has failed. If the pump stops and then the water rushes back and it starts again, the check valve has failed. If it runs but the water doesn't go down, the discharge line or pump is blocked. If it only runs hard in wet weather and keeps up, that's normal.</p></div>

> **Safety first:** Water and electricity don't mix. Unplug the pump before you reach into the pit, and never stand in water while touching the pump, cord or outlet. If the outlet or plug is under water, stay out and call an electrician or your utility. Only unplug the pump briefly while the water level is low.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

<figure><img src="/images/posts/sump-pump-running-constantly-flow.svg" alt="Flowchart: look in the pit while the pump runs; if the pit is empty the float switch is stuck; if water is coming out outside and the pit refills fast after it stops, check for a failed check valve or high groundwater; if the water level doesn't drop, check the discharge line, the intake and the pump" width="640" height="430" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Watching one full cycle tells you most of what you need to know.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Check the weather.** If it has rained hard or snow is melting, frequent running is normal as long as the pump keeps up (cause 6).
2. **Look in the pit while the pump runs.** If the pit is empty or nearly empty and the pump keeps going, the float or switch is the problem (cause 1).
3. **Go outside** and see whether water is coming out of the discharge pipe. If the pump runs but no water comes out, the line is blocked or frozen, or the pump is clogged (causes 3, 5).
4. **Watch the pit after the pump shuts off.** If you hear water rush back down the pipe and the level jumps up, the check valve has failed (cause 2).
5. **Time the refill.** If the pit fills quickly with no rain, look for another source, such as discharge water flowing back toward the house (cause 4) or a plumbing leak (cause 7).

## 1. The float switch is stuck on

The float rises with the water and turns the pump on, then drops and turns it off. If it's caught on the pit wall, the pump's cord or the discharge pipe, it can stay up and keep the pump running even when the pit is empty. A switch can also fail in the on position.

**Fix:**

1. Unplug the pump.
2. Check that the float can move freely. Move cords and anything else touching it, and make sure the pump hasn't shifted so the float rubs the wall.
3. Look for a float filled with water, or one coated in sludge, which can stop it working properly.
4. Plug the pump back in and slowly pour a bucket of water into the pit. It should turn on, empty the pit and shut off.
5. If it still runs with the float down, the switch has failed. Many pumps use a replaceable switch; on others the whole pump is replaced.

## 2. A failed check valve

The check valve on the discharge pipe lets water out but stops it from flowing back. If it fails, the water in the pipe pours back into the pit each time the pump stops, so the pump turns on again within a short time and keeps cycling.

**Fix:**

- Listen for a rush of water and watch for the level to rise right after the pump stops.
- Look for an arrow on the valve body. It should point up, away from the pump.
- Replacing a check valve is a common DIY job on PVC lines that use rubber couplings with hose clamps. Unplug the pump first and have a bucket and towels ready, because the water in the pipe will drain out.
- If there's no check valve at all, ask a plumber whether one should be added for your setup.

## 3. A blocked or frozen discharge line

If water can't leave through the discharge line, the pump runs but the pit stays full. In cold weather the outside end of the pipe can freeze, and in any weather it can be blocked by debris, ice, a crushed drain hose or a clogged buried line.

**Fix:**

- Go outside and check the end of the pipe. Clear leaves, dirt, ice or snow.
- If the line freezes, a plumber can fit a freeze guard or an air gap fitting so water can escape if the outside pipe blocks.
- If it drains into a buried pipe or the yard, make sure that pipe isn't crushed or blocked.

## 4. Discharge water flowing back toward the house

If the discharge pipe ends right next to the foundation, the water you just pumped out can soak back into the soil and return to the pit. The pump keeps moving the same water in a loop.

**Fix:**

- Make sure the discharge ends well away from the house and drains downhill. An extension hose or a buried line can carry it farther.
- Point it away from the foundation, your neighbor's property and walkways that could ice over.
- Check local rules before connecting a sump pump to a sewer line. Many places don't allow it.

## 5. A worn or clogged pump

A worn motor, a damaged impeller or a clogged intake screen means the pump moves less water than it should. It has to run longer to empty the pit, or can't empty it at all. Grinding, rattling or humming are warning signs.

**Fix:**

1. Unplug the pump and lift it out if you can do so safely.
2. Clean the intake screen and remove gravel, sludge and debris from the pit bottom. A wet/dry shop vacuum helps.
3. Set the pump on a solid, flat base such as a brick or paver so it doesn't suck up debris.
4. If it still struggles after cleaning, or makes grinding noises, the pump is likely worn out and should be replaced.

A pump that's too small for the amount of water coming in can also run constantly. If yours ran fine for years but now can't keep up, a plumber can check whether you need a larger pump or a second one.

## 6. A high water table or heavy rain

After heavy rain, spring snowmelt or in areas with a high water table, groundwater keeps flowing into the pit. A pump that runs a lot but keeps the pit from overflowing is working as designed.

**Fix:**

- Keep gutters clean and downspouts directed several feet away from the house to reduce how much water reaches the pit.
- Make sure soil around the foundation slopes away from the house.
- Consider a battery backup pump and a high water alarm. If the main pump fails or the power goes out during a storm, they give you a safety net. See our [power outage checklist](/home-safety/power-outage-checklist/) for storm prep.

## 7. Water from a plumbing leak

If the pit keeps filling in dry weather and the water looks clear, it may be coming from a leaking water line, a water softener or another appliance draining into the pit.

**Fix:**

- Check what drains into the pit. Some homes route a softener, dehumidifier or washer drain there by design.
- Turn off all water fixtures and watch the water meter for 30 minutes. If it moves, you may have a leak.
- Call a plumber to track down a hidden supply line leak.

## Basement water rising right now?

- **Don't step into standing water** if there's any chance the outlet, cord or an appliance is wet. Call an electrician or your utility to cut power safely.
- If the pump runs but can't keep up, check the discharge line outside for a blockage first. It's the fastest fix.
- A second submersible utility pump with a hose run outside can buy time.
- Move valuables and electronics up and out of the basement.

## When to call a plumber

Call a plumber if:

- The pump runs nonstop and you can't find a stuck float or blockage.
- The check valve or discharge line needs replacing and you're not comfortable cutting pipe.
- The pump can't keep up even in moderate rain, or the pit overflows.
- The pump is old, noisy or overheating and you want it replaced before the next storm.
