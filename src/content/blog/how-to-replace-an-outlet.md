Replacing a worn, cracked or loose outlet is one of the most common home electrical jobs. The wiring itself is simple: **black to brass, white to silver, bare or green to green**. What makes it safe is everything around that: **turning off the right breaker**, **proving every wire in the box is dead**, and **buying an outlet that matches the circuit**.

This guide covers a standard 120 V duplex outlet, plus the cases that trip people up: **switched outlets**, **GFCI outlets** and **two-prong outlets with no ground**.

> **Safety first:** You'll be handling wires that carry 120 V. Always turn off the breaker and **prove the wires are dead** before you touch them. A box can hold wires from more than one circuit, so test every wire, not just the outlet. Work with dry hands and a dry floor. If you find **aluminum wiring**, **scorched or melted wires**, **brittle or crumbling insulation** or a **loose box**, stop and call a licensed electrician.

## Before you start: quick checklist

1. **Find the breaker** for the outlet. Plug in a lamp or radio you can hear, then switch breakers off until it goes out.
2. **Have a working tester.** A non-contact voltage tester plus a plug-in outlet tester or multimeter.
3. **Buy the right outlet** (step 3). Bring the old one or a photo to the store if you're unsure.

## 1. Turn off the breaker

Panel labels are often wrong or out of date, so confirm with a lamp.

**How:**

1. Plug a lamp into the outlet and switch off the breaker you think feeds it.
2. Move the lamp to the other half. Both halves should be dead.
3. Tape over the breaker or leave a note on the panel so nobody switches it back on.

## 2. Prove every wire in the box is dead

A dark lamp isn't proof. The box may hold wires from **another circuit**. Electricians use a **live-dead-live** check: test on a known-live outlet, test your work, then test the known-live outlet again.

**How:**

1. Check your **non-contact voltage tester** on a known-live outlet. It should beep or light.
2. Hold it to **both slots of both halves** of the outlet you're replacing. It should stay quiet.
3. Remove the cover plate and the two mounting screws, and gently pull the outlet out of the box by its mounting strap. Don't touch the terminals or bare wire.
4. Hold the tester to **every wire in the box**, including any that don't connect to the outlet.
5. Confirm with a **plug-in outlet tester** or a **multimeter** reading of 0 V at both halves. Our guide to [testing an outlet with a multimeter](/tools/how-to-test-an-outlet-with-a-multimeter/) shows the readings.
6. Check the tester on the known-live outlet **again**. If it still works, the box is dead.

If any wire still shows power, find and switch off that circuit too before going on.

## 3. Match the new outlet to the circuit

The new outlet must suit the circuit it's on. Getting this wrong can overheat wiring.

**What to check:**

- **Amp rating.** A 15 amp outlet has two straight slots. A 20 amp outlet has a T-shaped neutral slot. **Never** put a 20 amp outlet on a 15 amp circuit. Look at the number on the breaker handle.
- **Wire size.** As a general guide, 14 gauge copper wire goes with 15 amp breakers, and thicker 12 gauge wire goes with 20 amp breakers. If the wire seems too thin for the breaker, or you can't tell, ask an electrician before going further.
- **Tamper-resistant (TR).** TR outlets have internal shutters and are typical for homes today. Look for "TR" on the face.
- **Location.** Outdoor and damp locations generally call for weather-resistant (WR) outlets, and bathrooms, kitchens, garages, basements and outdoors generally need GFCI protection. Some areas also require arc-fault (AFCI) protection when outlets are replaced. Check with your local building department or an electrician.
- **Like for like.** If the old outlet was a GFCI, replace it with a GFCI.

Standard outlets usually cost a few dollars; GFCIs cost roughly $15 to $30.

## 4. Photograph and label the wires

Before you disconnect anything, take a clear photo of each side of the outlet. Note which wires go to which screws and whether the **small metal tab** between the screws on each side is intact or broken off.

**How:**

- If there are **two sets of wires**, mark one set with tape (for example "top") so the pairs stay together.
- If a white wire is connected to a brass screw, wrap black or red tape on it. That wire is being used as a hot.
- If the wiring looks different from the diagram above, stop and call an electrician.

## 5. Remove the old outlet

**How:**

1. Loosen each side screw and unhook the wire.
2. For **push-in (backstab)** wires, push a small flat screwdriver into the release slot next to the hole while pulling the wire. If there's no slot, cut the wire close to the outlet and strip a fresh end, as long as enough wire remains.
3. Inspect every wire end. Snip off nicked, burnt or badly bent ends and strip a fresh end, using the **strip gauge** printed on the back of the new outlet (often about 3/4 inch).
4. If the wires are too short to work with after trimming, you'll need **pigtails** (step 6).

## 6. Connect the wires to the new outlet

<figure><img src="/images/posts/how-to-replace-an-outlet-hook.svg" alt="Three steps for a screw terminal: strip the wire to the length on the strip gauge, bend a hook with needle-nose pliers, then wrap the hook clockwise around the screw. Right: the hook turns clockwise so tightening pulls the wire in. Wrong: counterclockwise, which pushes the wire out as you tighten" width="640" height="380" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A clockwise hook tightens with the screw instead of pushing out.</figcaption></figure>

**How:**

1. **Ground first.** Connect the bare copper or green wire to the **green screw**. In a metal box, keep any ground connection to the box itself.
2. **Neutral.** Connect the white wire to a **silver** screw.
3. **Hot.** Connect the black (or taped) wire to a **brass** screw.
4. **Wrap clockwise.** Bend a hook in each wire and wrap it **clockwise** about three-quarters of the way around the screw, so tightening pulls it in. Tighten firmly. Only bare copper should sit under the screw, with the insulation close to it.
5. **One wire per screw.** If there are two sets of wires, use the second brass and silver screws, or join them with a **pigtail**: a short 6-inch piece of matching wire joined to the circuit wires with a wire connector, with its other end on the outlet.
6. **Skip the push-in holes.** Clamp-style back-wire terminals that tighten with the side screw are fine. Plain push-in holes are not recommended.
7. Tug each wire gently; none should move.

## 7. Mount the outlet and fit the cover

**How:**

1. Fold the wires neatly into the box in a zigzag, ground at the back.
2. Push the outlet in with the **ground hole at the bottom** (or as the old one sat) and fit the mounting screws. It should sit straight and firm.
3. Fit the cover plate snugly; overtightening cracks it.

## 8. Restore power and test it

**How:**

1. Remove the tape and switch the breaker back on.
2. Plug a **plug-in outlet tester** into **both halves**. The lights should show "correct" on its chart.
3. Plug in a lamp to confirm.
4. On a GFCI, press **TEST** (power should cut off) and then **RESET**.
5. If the tester shows a fault, the outlet sparks, or the breaker trips, switch the breaker off and recheck your work against the photo.

## Split or switched outlets

On some outlets, often in bedrooms and living rooms, **one half is controlled by a wall switch**. You'll see two hot wires on the brass side and a **broken-off tab** between the brass screws.

**Fix:**

- Snap off the brass-side tab on the new outlet the same way, using needle-nose pliers to bend it back and forth until it breaks off.
- Connect each hot wire to the same half as before (your photo).
- If the **silver-side tab** was also broken, or the box has two breakers feeding it, the outlet may be fed by two circuits. Match the old wiring exactly, and if that's unclear, call an electrician.

## Replacing a GFCI outlet

A GFCI has **LINE** terminals for incoming power and **LOAD** terminals that feed other outlets after it. Swap them and most modern GFCIs won't reset.

**Fix:**

- Label the LINE and LOAD wires before removing the old GFCI.
- If only one set of wires is present, it goes on **LINE**. Leave the tape over LOAD.
- If you're not sure which set is LINE, test each set with power on, which is best left to an electrician.
- See how [LINE and LOAD work on a GFCI](/electrical/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/) if it trips after the swap.

## Two-prong outlet with no ground?

Many older homes have two-slot outlets with no ground wire in the box. **Don't just fit a three-prong outlet** in its place: the round hole would look grounded but wouldn't be.

**Options:**

- Replace it with **another two-prong outlet**.
- Fit a **GFCI outlet**, which protects people from shock without a ground. Label it **"No equipment ground"** (stickers usually come in the box). Outlets it protects downstream also get labels.
- Have an electrician check whether the box is grounded through metal conduit or armored cable, or **run a new ground**.

Rules vary, so confirm with an electrician or your local building department.

## Stop and call an electrician if you see this

- **Aluminum wiring:** dull silver wires, often marked "AL" on the sheathing. It needs special connectors and techniques.
- **Scorched, melted or discolored** wires, outlet or box.
- **Brittle, cracked or crumbling** insulation.
- A **loose box** that moves in the wall, or a cracked box.
- Wires **too short** to strip and connect safely.
- A wire that stays live after you've switched off every breaker you can find.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- You can't prove the box is dead, or you're not comfortable working inside it.
- You're unsure of the circuit's amp rating, wire size or what outlet type it needs.
- The new outlet tests wrong, sparks or trips the breaker.
- Your area requires a permit or a licensed electrician for this work.

A good replacement feels firm, grips plugs tightly and tests correct on both halves.
