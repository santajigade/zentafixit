Every load of laundry leaves lint behind. The lint screen catches most of it, but some always slips past and builds up in the **vent duct**, the **outdoor vent hood** and the **lint trap housing** inside the dryer. Lint is very flammable, and a clogged vent traps hot air right where that lint sits. That's why dryer vent cleaning is a fire safety job, not just a way to dry clothes faster.

The good news: most vents take under an hour to clean with a brush kit and a vacuum. The keys are **cutting power (and gas) first**, cleaning **the whole path from the dryer to the outside**, and making sure the duct itself is the **right kind of metal**.

> **Safety first:** Unplug an electric dryer before you touch the duct. Most US electric dryers run on **240 V**, so if you can't reach the plug, turn off the dryer's breaker. On a **gas dryer**, close the gas shutoff valve and move the dryer gently so you don't kink or strain the gas line. If you ever **smell gas**, don't touch any switches: leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Wear gloves, since duct edges are sharp.

## Signs your dryer vent is clogged: quick check

1. **Time a normal load.** If a regular load now needs more than one cycle, or a lot longer than it used to, airflow is probably restricted.
2. **Feel the dryer and the room.** A dryer top or front that's unusually hot, or a laundry room that gets hot and humid, points to air that can't escape.
3. **Go outside while the dryer runs.** The flap on the outdoor hood should open and you should feel a strong stream of warm air. A weak puff, or a flap that doesn't move, means a blockage.
4. **Look for lint.** Lint piled around the outdoor hood, behind the dryer or stuck in the flap is a clear sign.
5. **Notice any smell.** A **burning smell** while drying means stop using the dryer until you've cleaned the vent and lint trap housing. Some newer dryers also show a vent or airflow warning: check your manual for what yours means.

## 1. Clean the lint screen every load

The screen is your first line of defense. A full screen chokes airflow before air even reaches the duct.

**How:**

1. Roll the lint off the screen after **every load**.
2. Every few months, run water over it. If water pools instead of passing through, dryer sheet residue is clogging the mesh: scrub it with warm, soapy water and let it **dry fully**.

## 2. Unplug the dryer or shut off the gas

Do this every time before you pull the dryer out or touch the duct.

**How:**

1. **Electric dryer:** unplug the power cord from the wall outlet. If the plug is out of reach, switch off the dryer's double-pole breaker at the panel.
2. **Gas dryer:** unplug the cord (gas dryers use a regular 120 V plug), then turn the handle on the gas shutoff valve so it sits **across** the pipe, which is closed.
3. Slide the dryer out slowly, watching the cord, gas line and duct so nothing gets pinched or pulled off.

## 3. Disconnect the transition duct

The **transition duct** is the short, bendable piece that links the back of the dryer to the vent in the wall or floor.

**How:**

1. Loosen the clamp at the dryer's exhaust outlet and slide the duct off.
2. Loosen the clamp at the wall end and remove the duct completely.
3. Look inside it with a flashlight. If it's **crushed, torn, plastic or thin foil**, plan to replace it (see the duct section below).
4. Shake or vacuum out the lint, or brush it if it's metal and in good shape.

## 4. Brush out the vent run

This is the main job. A **dryer vent brush kit** has a round brush and flexible rods that screw together, so you can reach well into the duct. Kits are widely sold for about $20 to $40.

**How:**

1. Start at the **wall opening** inside. Vacuum out the loose lint you can reach.
2. Feed the brush in, adding rods as you go, and twist it while you push and pull. Work in short sections.
3. If your kit is made for a drill, run the drill **clockwise only** and at low speed. Running it in reverse can unscrew the rods and leave the brush stuck inside the duct.
4. Pull the brush back often and vacuum the lint it brings out.
5. Go outside and brush in from the **outdoor hood** too. Brushing from both ends pushes lint out instead of packing it deeper.
6. Keep going until the brush comes back clean.

## 5. Clean the outdoor vent hood and flap

The outdoor end clogs often, and you can see it, so it's easy to check.

**How:**

1. Pull lint, leaves and any nests out of the hood by hand (wear gloves).
2. Make sure the **flap or damper swings open and closed freely**. Clean or replace a hood whose flap sticks.
3. Remove any **screen or mesh** over the opening. Screens catch lint and should not be used on a dryer exhaust. A hood with a flap keeps pests out without trapping lint.
4. Keep plants, snow and stored items clear of the hood.

## 6. Vacuum the lint trap housing

Lint also falls past the screen into the slot it sits in, and some builds up inside the cabinet.

**How:**

1. With the screen out, vacuum the **lint screen slot** with a long, narrow crevice tool, or use a flexible lint trap brush.
2. Vacuum the **exhaust outlet** on the back of the dryer as far as you can reach.
3. Lint deep inside the cabinet, around the drum, blower or heater, means opening panels. That's a job for an appliance technician, or follow your service manual only if you're comfortable doing it.

## 7. Reconnect with the right duct and test airflow

**How:**

1. Reattach the transition duct with clamps at both ends. Keep it as **short and straight** as you can, with no sags or kinks when you push the dryer back.
2. Seal joints in metal duct with **metal foil tape**, not cloth duct tape, which dries out and peels.
3. Don't join duct sections with **screws** that poke into the airflow. They snag lint and start new clogs.
4. Plug the dryer back in (and reopen the gas valve on a gas dryer, then check the connection the way your manual says).
5. Run the dryer on an air-only or heat setting for a few minutes, then go outside. The flap should open and you should feel **strong, steady airflow**.

## Foil, plastic or metal: which duct is safe?

| Duct type | Where it fits | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rigid metal** (smooth wall) | Runs inside walls, floors and ceilings | Best: smooth inside, holds the least lint |
| **Semi-rigid metal** (aluminum, bendable) | The short transition behind the dryer | Good: holds its shape, look for a listed transition duct |
| **Foil accordion** (thin, very flexible) | Often sold as a transition duct | Avoid: crushes and sags easily, ridges trap lint, many makers and codes don't allow it |
| **Plastic or vinyl** (white, coiled wire) | Nowhere | Never use: it can melt or burn and traps lint |

A few more rules for a safe vent:

- Most US dryers use **4-inch** diameter duct. Don't step down to a smaller size.
- The vent must end **outdoors**. Never vent a dryer into an attic, crawl space, wall cavity or garage.
- Every bend adds resistance. Your dryer's **installation instructions** list the maximum vent length and how much each elbow reduces it, and local code may add its own limits. Check both before changing a long run.
- Concealed duct inside a wall should be rigid metal, not flexible duct.

## How often should you clean a dryer vent?

- **Lint screen:** after every load, and wash it every few months.
- **Full vent and outdoor hood:** at least **once a year**.
- **More often** if you do a lot of laundry, have pets or have a long vent with several bends, and any time you notice the signs above.

If your dryer's manual gives a stricter schedule, follow it.

## Still drying slowly after cleaning?

If airflow outside is strong now but clothes still take too long:

- **Check the load and settings.** Overloading, very wet loads and low heat cycles all slow drying.
- **Check the heat.** If the drum tumbles but the air stays cool, the issue is in the dryer, not the vent. See [why a dryer is not heating](/appliances/dryer-not-heating/) for the breaker, thermal fuse, heating element and gas igniter checks.
- **Weak airflow even with a clean duct** can mean a hidden crush in the wall, a bird nest high in the run, or a run that's simply too long. That needs a closer look.

## When to call a pro

Call a dryer vent cleaning service or an appliance technician if:

- The vent exits through the **roof**, or the outdoor end is high up and needs a ladder you're not comfortable on.
- The run is **long, has many bends** or goes through walls and ceilings you can't access.
- The brush gets **stuck**, or airflow stays weak after cleaning.
- You find **scorched lint**, melted duct or burn marks, or smell burning even with a clean vent.
- Lint has built up **inside the dryer cabinet**.
- You need a **new vent run**, or you have any doubt about a **gas connection**.

If a dryer ever shows flames or smoke, don't open the door. If it's safe, turn it off or unplug it, get everyone out and call 911 from outside. Keep a working smoke alarm near the laundry area and, if you have a gas dryer, a carbon monoxide alarm too.
