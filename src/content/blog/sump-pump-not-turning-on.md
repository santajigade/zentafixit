A sump pump sits in a pit in the lowest part of the basement and switches on when groundwater fills the pit. When it won't turn on, water keeps rising and can overflow onto the floor, often during the heavy rain when you need it most.

Most of the time the cause is simple: **no power reaching the pump, a float switch that is stuck, or debris jamming the pit**. A quick bucket test tells you which.

> **Safety first:** Water and electricity are a dangerous mix. Never step into standing water near a pump, outlet or extension cord. Unplug the pump before you put your hands in the pit, and keep the plug and outlet dry. If the basement floor is already flooded around outlets or appliances, stay out and call an electrician or your utility to cut power first.

## How to tell what's wrong: quick test

<figure><img src="/images/posts/sump-pump-not-turning-on-flow.svg" alt="Flowchart: confirm the outlet has power, then pour water into the pit; if the pump does not start, the float or switch is stuck or failed; if it hums or runs but water stays, check the intake screen, check valve and discharge line; if it runs and empties the pit, it is working" width="640" height="430" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>The bucket test sorts most sump pump problems in a few minutes.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Check for power.** Plug a lamp or an outlet tester into the pump's outlet. If it's dead, start with cause 1.
2. **Look into the pit.** Is the float free to move, or is it caught on the pipe, the wall or the pump cord? Is there mud, gravel or debris around the pump?
3. **Pour water in.** Slowly pour a bucket or two of water into the pit until the float rises.
    - **Nothing happens:** the switch or motor isn't getting the signal (causes 1, 2, 6).
    - **Hums but no water moves:** the impeller is jammed or the intake is blocked (cause 3).
    - **Runs but the level barely drops:** the check valve or discharge line is the problem (causes 4, 5).
4. **Listen and watch.** A working pump empties the pit to a low level and shuts off by itself.

## 1. No power: tripped GFCI or breaker

Sump pumps are often on a GFCI outlet, and basements are damp. A tripped GFCI or breaker leaves the pump silent with no warning.

**Fix:**

- Press **RESET** on the outlet, and check for another GFCI upstream (in a garage, bathroom or the basement wall) that may protect this outlet.
- Check the **breaker panel**. If a breaker tripped, push it fully to OFF, then back to ON.
- Make sure the pump cord is fully plugged in, and if it has a **piggyback plug** (the switch plug with the pump plug behind it), that both are seated.
- If the GFCI trips again, the pump motor or cord may have an insulation fault. Our guide to a [GFCI outlet that keeps tripping](/electrical/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/) walks through finding the cause.

Don't run a sump pump on a long extension cord or replace a GFCI with a regular outlet to stop the trips. Ask an electrician about the right setup for your basement.

## 2. The float switch is stuck

The float switch tells the pump when to start. If the float gets caught on the pit wall, the discharge pipe or its own cord, or the pump has shifted in the pit, the float can't rise and the pump never starts.

**Fix:**

1. **Unplug the pump.**
2. Check that the float can move freely through its full range. A **tethered float** (on a cord) needs room to swing up. A **vertical float** slides up and down a rod and can stick on dirt.
3. Move the pump so the float clears the pit wall, and tidy the cords so they can't snag it.
4. Clean any mud or scale off the float and rod.
5. Plug back in and repeat the bucket test.

If the float moves freely but the pump still doesn't start, the switch itself may have failed (cause 6).

## 3. Debris in the pit or intake

Gravel, silt, sand and odds and ends that fall into the pit can clog the pump's intake screen or jam the impeller. You may hear a hum with no water moving, or the pump may trip the outlet.

**Fix:**

1. **Unplug the pump.**
2. Remove standing water with a shop vacuum or bucket so you can see.
3. Lift the pump out by its handle, not the cord or float (you may need to loosen a union on the discharge pipe).
4. Clean the **intake screen** and clear any debris around the impeller opening.
5. Scoop out silt and gravel from the bottom of the pit. Setting the pump on a flat, solid base such as a paver keeps it above the worst of the sediment.
6. Reinstall, reconnect the pipe snugly and run the bucket test.

## 4. A stuck or failed check valve

The **check valve** on the discharge pipe stops pumped water from running back into the pit. If it sticks closed, the pump runs but can't push water out. If it fails open, water flows back down after each cycle and the pump short-cycles.

**Fix:**

- Listen for a firm thump when the pump shuts off. That's normal. Constant running with no drop in level is not.
- Check the arrow on the valve body. It must point **up and away from the pump**, in the direction of flow.
- If the valve is clogged or stuck, unplug the pump, loosen the rubber couplings or unions, and clean or replace the valve.
- Many installs have a small **air relief hole** in the discharge pipe between the pump and the check valve to prevent air lock. If yours has one, make sure it's clear.

## 5. Frozen or blocked discharge line

In cold weather, the discharge pipe outside can freeze, especially where it runs along the ground or sits in a low spot. Leaves, ice or a crushed extension hose can block it too. The pump runs against a blockage and may overheat.

**Fix:**

- Follow the discharge line outside and check the end for ice, leaves, dirt or a kinked or crushed hose.
- Thaw a frozen section with warm water or a hair dryer (never an open flame) while the pump is unplugged.
- Make sure the outside pipe slopes away from the house so water drains out instead of sitting in it.
- Ask about a **freeze guard** fitting for the outdoor end, which lets water escape if the line ices up.

## 6. A failed switch or burned-out motor

Switches and motors wear out. A switch can fail with the float moving perfectly, and a motor can seize or burn out after years of cycling.

**Fix:**

1. With a **piggyback plug**, unplug both plugs, then plug **only the pump plug** straight into the outlet.
2. If the motor starts, the **switch is bad**. Unplug it within a few seconds if the pit is dry. Replace the switch, or the pump if the switch is built in.
3. If the motor still doesn't run, hums, or smells hot, the **motor has failed**. Replace the pump.
4. Pumps with a built-in switch (no piggyback plug) can't be tested this way. Check the manual or replace the pump.

## 7. Battery backup and the pump's age

A **battery backup pump** sits beside the main pump and runs when the power is out or the main pump fails. It has its own float, set a little higher. If it isn't working, you may have no backup when you need one.

**Fix:**

- Most backup systems have a **test button** and an alarm. Press the test button regularly and follow the maker's schedule for checking or replacing the battery.
- Keep the battery charger plugged in and the terminals clean.
- If the **main pump is old**, short-cycles, runs noisily or struggles to keep up, plan to replace it before the wet season. Replacing an aging pump on your schedule beats an emergency call during a storm.

## Basement flooding right now?

- **Stay out of standing water** if any outlets, cords or appliances are near it. Call an electrician or your utility to shut off power safely.
- If it's safe, **unplug the pump** and try the quick fixes above: reset the GFCI, free the float, clear the intake.
- Use a **portable utility pump** or wet/dry vacuum to lower the water if you can do so safely.
- Call a plumber or water damage company if the water keeps rising.

## When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if:

- The pump hums, smells hot or won't run even when plugged straight into the outlet.
- The pump keeps tripping the GFCI or breaker.
- The check valve or discharge line needs cutting or replacing.
- Water rises faster than the pump can keep up, or the pit runs constantly.
- You want a battery backup or a high water alarm installed.

Call an electrician for repeated GFCI or breaker trips, damaged cords, or an outlet that is wet or scorched.
