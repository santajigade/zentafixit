A breaker panel with blank, faded or wrong labels turns a simple job into guesswork. When a circuit trips, when you want to replace a light switch, or when water is spraying from a burst pipe near an outlet, you need to know **which breaker kills which circuit**, fast.

Mapping your panel is a safe, no-tools-inside job. You **switch off one breaker at a time**, **test every outlet, light and hardwired appliance**, and **write down exactly what went dead**. It takes an afternoon and pays off for as long as you live there.

> **Safety first:** You only touch the **breaker handles**. Keep the inner metal cover (the **dead front**) on at all times, and never remove it or reach inside the panel. Work with **dry hands** on a **dry floor**, and stand to one side of the panel as you switch. If the panel is wet, rusty, scorched, warm, buzzing or smells burnt, stop and call a licensed electrician.

## Why accurate labels matter

- **Outages:** when a breaker trips, a good label tells you what was on it, which helps you work out why.
- **Safe repairs:** before anyone changes an outlet, switch or fixture, the right breaker has to be off. A wrong label can leave a live circuit you think is dead.
- **Emergencies:** with water, smoke or sparks, you or a family member need to cut the right circuit without hunting.
- **Code:** the National Electrical Code asks that each circuit be **legibly identified** by its purpose. Inspectors and home buyers notice a blank or vague directory.

## What you need

1. **A plug-in outlet tester** (about $10 to $25). Its lights show at a glance whether an outlet has power.
2. **A lamp or a radio.** A radio turned up loud is handy: you can hear from the panel when it goes quiet.
3. **A non-contact voltage tester.** Hold its tip at an outlet's short (hot) slot. It beeps or lights when power is present. Check it on a known-live outlet first.
4. **A helper, or your phone.** A video call between the panel and the room saves dozens of trips up and down stairs.
5. **Pencil, paper, painter's tape and a flashlight.** Tape lets you mark outlets with circuit numbers as you go.

## Before you start: what not to switch off

Turning off a breaker cuts everything on it, so plan for these first:

- **Refrigerators and freezers:** keep the doors shut. A short outage is fine, but don't leave them off longer than you need.
- **Sump pumps:** don't switch one off during rain or if water is rising. Map it on a dry day and turn it straight back on.
- **Medical devices:** oxygen concentrators, CPAP machines and any home medical equipment must stay powered. Know their circuit before you begin, or run them from a battery backup the device maker approves.
- **Computers and network gear:** save your work and shut down desktop PCs, and expect the Wi-Fi and internet to drop. Tell everyone at home before you start.
- **Central AC or heat pump:** if you switch its breaker off, wait several minutes before turning it back on, or follow the unit's manual.
- **The main breaker:** leave it on. You're mapping branch circuits one at a time.

Clocks, garage door openers and some smart devices may need resetting afterwards. Hardwired smoke alarms switch to battery and may chirp, which is normal.

## 1. Sketch every room

Draw a simple floor plan of each level, plus the garage, basement, attic and outside walls. Mark every **outlet**, **light**, **ceiling fan**, **switch** and **hardwired appliance** (furnace, water heater, range, dryer, dishwasher, disposal, garage door opener, bath fans).

Don't forget outdoor outlets, porch lights, the doorbell and smoke alarms.

## 2. Number every breaker space

Use the **numbers your panel already has**. Many panels have numbers stamped or printed beside each space on the dead front. If yours has none, a common scheme in US panels is **odd numbers down the left** (1, 3, 5) and **even numbers down the right** (2, 4, 6), counting from the top. Write the numbers on small pieces of painter's tape next to each handle if needed, and draw the panel layout on your sheet.

Some slim breakers fit two circuits in one space. Give each handle its own entry.

## 3. Turn on every light and plug in a tester

**How:**

1. Turn on **every light and fan** in the house, including closets, porch and garage.
2. Flip every wall switch that might control an outlet to **ON**.
3. Plug a tester, lamp or radio into a few outlets in the area you're mapping first.
4. Start a video call with your helper, or set a loud radio where you can hear it from the panel.

## 4. Turn off one breaker and test everything

**How:**

1. Switch **one breaker** firmly to **OFF**.
2. Walk the whole house. Check **every outlet** with the plug-in tester or lamp, every light, and every hardwired appliance. Don't stop at the first room that went dark: one circuit often serves parts of two or three rooms.
3. For appliances, look for a sign of power: dishwasher or range display lights, the garage door opener's light, the furnace blower when the thermostat calls for heat.
4. Write the breaker number on your sketch beside each dead item, and on tape on the outlet if you like.
5. Switch the breaker back **ON**, then move to the next one.

If a breaker won't switch back on or trips straight away, leave it off and see [what to do when a breaker won't reset](/electrical/breaker-wont-reset/).

## 5. Check both halves of every outlet

A standard duplex outlet has two halves, and they're **not always on the same circuit**. A switched outlet may have one half controlled by a wall switch. Some kitchen outlets are **split**, with the top half on one breaker and the bottom on another.

**How:** test the top and the bottom of every outlet each time. If the halves are on different breakers, write both numbers, such as "2 and 4". Breakers that feed a split outlet are often joined by a handle tie so they switch off together. Note that on your directory.

## 6. Note 240 V double-pole breakers

A **double-pole breaker** is twice as wide, takes **two spaces** and has one wide handle or two handles tied together. It feeds **240 V** loads such as an electric dryer, range, water heater, central AC, heat pump, EV charger or well pump.

**How:** label it with **both space numbers** and one name, such as "1 and 3: Water heater (240 V)". An electric water heater shows no obvious sign of power, so it's often found by elimination once the other 240 V loads are matched. If you're not sure, write "probably" and confirm with an electrician.

## 7. Mark GFCI and AFCI breakers

Breakers with a **TEST button** are usually **GFCI**, **AFCI** or **dual-function** breakers. They protect the whole circuit, so an outlet on them may have no buttons of its own.

**How:** add "GFCI", "AFCI" or "dual" to the label. That tells anyone resetting a dead bathroom or bedroom outlet to look at the panel, not just the outlet. Read the breaker's own label to see which type it is. For where each kind of protection is usually expected, see [where GFCI and AFCI are required](/electrical/gfci-afci-requirements/).

## 8. Write a clear panel directory

The directory card usually sits inside the panel door. Fill it in neatly, or print a typed one and tape it there.

**Good labels name the room and the specific items:**

| Vague | Clear |
| --- | --- |
| Kitchen | Kitchen counter outlets, sink side |
| Plugs | Bedroom 2 outlets and ceiling light |
| Bath | Hall bath outlets and fan (GFCI breaker) |
| Dryer | 1 and 3: Electric dryer (240 V) |
| Spare | Not found (ask electrician) |

**Tips:**

- Use names that won't change, like "front bedroom" or "Bedroom 2 (north)", not a person's name.
- Write **"Not found"** for a breaker you couldn't match. Only call it "spare" if you know it has nothing connected.
- Add the date, keep a photo of the card on your phone, and update it after any electrical work.

## Breakers you can't match?

- **Recheck the easy-to-miss items:** outdoor and garage outlets, attic and crawl space lights, the doorbell, bath fans, smoke alarms, under-cabinet lights and outlets behind furniture.
- **Recheck outlet halves and switched outlets** with the switch on.
- **Check for a second panel.** Some homes have a subpanel in the garage or basement. Its breakers need their own directory.
- **Don't open anything to find out.** Tracing wires means working inside the panel or boxes, which is electrician work.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- A breaker is **hot, buzzing, scorched** or won't stay on.
- The panel shows **rust, water marks or a burning smell**.
- A breaker controls items you can't find, or a circuit seems to feed **far too many rooms**.
- An outlet or light stays live with **every branch breaker off**.
- You need anything done **inside the panel**. The dead front stays on for homeowners, every time.

Many electricians will also trace and label a panel as part of a service visit, which is worth asking about if your home has had years of add-on wiring.
