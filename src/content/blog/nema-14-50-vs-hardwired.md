A Level 2 home charger connects to a 240 V circuit in one of two ways: it **plugs into a 240 V outlet** (most often a **NEMA 14-50**, the same outlet many RVs use), or an electrician **hardwires** it straight to the circuit with no plug at all.

Both work well. The real differences are **maximum charging speed, how well the outlet holds up, GFCI rules, flexibility and cost**. Here's how they compare so you can pick the right one before the electrician arrives.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>A NEMA 14-50 outlet is a good, flexible choice if 40 A charging is enough and you will leave the charger plugged in. Hardwire if you want up to 48 A, are installing outdoors, or want to avoid plug wear and costly GFCI breaker trips. Either way, use a licensed electrician and get a permit.</p></div>

> **Safety first:** A 240 V, 50 or 60 A circuit carries enough energy to cause serious injury and fires. Installing the outlet or charger means working in your electrical panel, so it is a job for a licensed electrician in most homes. Never use adapters, extension cords or a dryer outlet "for now" with an EV charger.

## How to decide: quick checklist

1. **Check your charger's specs.** Does it come as a plug-in model, a hardwired model, or both? What's its maximum current? Many plug-in chargers top out at 32 or 40 A.
2. **Check your car's onboard charger.** The car limits the speed too. If your car accepts only 32 A or so, a 48 A hardwired charger won't make it charge faster.
3. **Decide where it goes.** Indoors in a garage, an outlet works well. Outdoors, a hardwired unit avoids an exposed plug.
4. **Think about how long you'll stay.** If you rent or may move soon, a plug-in charger comes with you.
5. **Ask your electrician about panel capacity and GFCI rules** before choosing. They can change the answer.

## 1. Charging speed: the biggest difference

EV charging counts as a **continuous load**, so the charger may only use **80% of the circuit's rating**. That sets the ceiling:

| Setup | Breaker | Max charging current | Power at 240 V |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plug-in, NEMA 14-50 | 50 A | 40 A | about 9.6 kW |
| Hardwired | 60 A | 48 A | about 11.5 kW |
| Hardwired, smaller circuit | 40 A | 32 A | about 7.7 kW |

**What it means for you:**

- A 40 A plug-in charger adds a lot of range overnight, which covers most daily commutes. To estimate your own times, try this [EV charging time calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/ecology/ev-charging-time/).
- 48 A is useful if you have a long commute, two EVs sharing one charger, or a car with a large battery and a fast onboard charger.
- If your car can't accept more than about 32 to 40 A, the hardwired speed advantage disappears.

## 2. Outlet quality, heat and wear

An EV pulls high current for **hours at a time**, which is much harder on an outlet than an occasional RV hookup or a welder. Most problems with 14-50 charging come from the receptacle and its connections, not the charger.

**Where the heat comes from:**

- **Cheap receptacles** with weak contacts that don't grip the plug blades firmly.
- **Loose terminal screws** that weren't tightened to the maker's torque spec.
- **Repeated plugging and unplugging.** Each insertion wears the contacts a little, and outlets are not designed for daily cycles.

**How to avoid it:**

- Ask for an **industrial or commercial grade** 14-50 receptacle, not the cheapest one on the shelf.
- Have the electrician **torque the terminals** to the receptacle maker's spec.
- **Leave the charger plugged in.** Coil the car cable on its holster instead.
- Every few months, carefully feel the plug and cover plate after a long session. Warm can be normal. **Hot, discolored, melted or smelling of burning** is not. Stop charging and call an electrician.

A hardwired charger has no plug or receptacle, so these failure points simply don't exist.

## 3. GFCI rules for 240 V outlets

Recent editions of the **National Electrical Code (NEC)** expanded GFCI protection to cover **240 V outlets** in places like garages and outdoors, and they also require GFCI protection for **outlets installed for EV charging**. In areas that have adopted these editions, a new 14-50 for an EV generally needs a **GFCI breaker**.

**Why this matters:**

- A 50 A two-pole GFCI breaker costs **much more** than a standard breaker.
- Most EV chargers already have **their own built-in ground-fault protection**. A GFCI breaker ahead of it can cause **nuisance trips**, where charging stops overnight for no obvious reason. Some charger makers address this in their manuals, so check yours.
- A **hardwired** charger doesn't use a receptacle, so the receptacle GFCI rule doesn't apply in the same way. The charger's own protection covers it. Your electrician will confirm what your local code requires.

Which NEC edition applies depends on your city or county, so ask your electrician or local building department rather than relying on a general rule.

## 4. Flexibility and moving

**The outlet wins here:**

- **Take it with you.** A plug-in charger unplugs and moves to your next home. Renters often prefer this.
- **Swap chargers easily.** If the charger fails or you change cars, you plug in a new one without an electrician.
- **Other uses.** A 14-50 can also run an RV or some shop equipment, if your local rules allow it.
- **Portable chargers.** Many EVs come with or offer a mobile connector that plugs into a 14-50.

**Hardwired advantages:**

- **Outdoor installs** are simpler without an exposed plug and in-use cover.
- **Power sharing.** Some hardwired chargers can link together and split one circuit between two cars.
- **Some utility rebates** require a specific type of charger or installation. Check your utility's rules before buying.

## 5. Cost to install

The two cost about the same in labor because the expensive part is the same: running heavy wire from the panel to the garage.

**What changes the price:**

- **Distance** from the panel to the charger, and whether walls or ceilings need opening.
- **Wire size.** A 60 A circuit for a 48 A hardwired charger needs heavier wire than a 50 A circuit. Your electrician sizes it for the run.
- **The GFCI breaker** for an outlet can add a noticeable amount.
- **Panel upgrades** if your panel lacks capacity, which can cost far more than the charger itself.

Total costs vary widely by region and house, from a few hundred dollars for a short run to several thousand if a panel upgrade is needed. Get two or three written quotes. For more on the whole process, see [Level 2 charger installation](/ev-charging/level-2-charger-installation/).

## 6. Electrician, permit and panel capacity

Whichever you choose, the steps are the same:

1. **Load calculation.** The electrician checks your panel can handle a 40 to 60 A continuous load on top of your AC, dryer, range and other big loads. If not, options include a smaller circuit, a load management device or a panel upgrade.
2. **Permit.** Most US jurisdictions require a permit for a new 240 V circuit. It means an inspector checks the work, which protects you and can matter to your home insurance.
3. **Install to the charger manual.** Breaker size, wire size and settings must match what the charger's manual says. Hardwired chargers usually have a setting (switch or app) for the circuit size.
4. **Inspection** and then your first charge.

## Already have a 14-50 outlet?

- **Check its age and condition.** An old RV outlet with a worn or discolored face should be replaced with a good quality one before daily EV use.
- **Check the breaker size.** It should be 50 A for a 14-50. Set the charger to 40 A or less.
- **Ask an electrician** whether it needs GFCI protection under your local code, and whether the wiring is sized correctly.
- If charging keeps stopping, see [home EV charger not charging](/ev-charging/home-ev-charger-not-charging/).

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician to:

- Install any new 240 V outlet or hardwired charger.
- Check an existing outlet before using it for daily EV charging.
- Look at any **warm plug, discolored outlet, burning smell or tripping breaker**. Stop charging until it's checked.
- Work out panel capacity, GFCI needs and permits for your area.

The choice between outlet and hardwired matters less than doing either one properly. A well-installed outlet with the charger left plugged in is safe. A badly installed hardwired charger is not.
