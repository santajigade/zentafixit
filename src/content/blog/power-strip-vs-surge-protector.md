A **power strip** and a **surge protector** look almost the same: a block of outlets on a cord. The difference is inside. A plain power strip just **splits one outlet into several**. A surge protector does that too, but it also has parts that **absorb brief voltage spikes** before they reach your TV, computer or router.

You can usually tell which one you have in a minute by reading the **label** and checking the **indicator lights**. Both share the same safety rules: **no daisy chaining**, **no space heaters**, and **stay under the amp rating**.

> **Safety first:** Power strips and surge protectors are a common cause of home fires when they're overloaded, damaged or covered. Never plug a **space heater** into either one. If a strip, cord or plug feels **hot**, looks **melted or discolored**, or smells like burning, unplug it (by the plug, with dry hands) and replace it.

## How to tell them apart: quick check

1. **Read the label** on the back or bottom. Look for the words "surge protector" or "surge suppressor" and a **UL 1449** listing.
2. **Look for a joule rating and a VPR** (voltage protection rating). A plain power strip won't list either.
3. **Look for indicator lights** marked "protected" and "grounded" (or similar). A plain strip usually only has a lit on/off switch.
4. **Check the amp rating**, often 15 A for household strips. You'll need this for safe use either way.

If the label says only something like "power tap" or "relocatable power tap" with no surge ratings, it's a power strip with no surge protection.

## 1. What a power strip does

A power strip is an extension cord with several outlets. Most have an **on/off switch** and a small **circuit breaker** that trips if you draw too much current. That breaker protects the strip from overload, not your devices from surges.

Power strips are generally listed to a different UL standard for outlet strips (often **UL 1363**). That listing means it was tested for basic electrical safety, nothing more.

**Good for:** lamps, phone chargers, fans, holiday lights and other low-cost, low-wattage items where surge damage wouldn't matter much.

## 2. What a surge protector does

A surge protector (sometimes called a surge suppressor) has the same outlets and breaker, plus a **surge protection circuit**. A surge is a voltage spike that lasts a tiny fraction of a second. It can come from lightning near power lines, utility switching, or large motors in your own home switching on and off.

The surge circuit sits quietly at normal 120 V and only reacts when voltage jumps well above it. Many models also add **coax, Ethernet or phone** jacks so surges can't sneak in through those lines.

## 3. How MOVs absorb a surge

Most plug-in surge protectors use **metal oxide varistors (MOVs)**. An MOV is a small disc that blocks current at normal voltage. When voltage spikes past its threshold, it suddenly conducts and diverts the excess energy, usually to the ground wire, so less of it reaches your equipment.

A spike gets diverted to ground.</figcaption></figure>

Every surge an MOV absorbs wears it down a little, and one large surge can use it up. That's why surge protectors don't last forever (see section 7). Many designs include a **thermal fuse** that disconnects worn-out MOVs. Depending on the model, the strip then either **cuts power** or **keeps passing power with no protection**, which is why the indicator light matters.

## 4. Read the label: UL 1449, joules and VPR

| Label item | What it means | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **UL 1449** | The US safety standard for surge protective devices | Must be listed. Skip unlisted strips |
| **Joules** | How much surge energy it can absorb before it wears out | Higher is generally better |
| **VPR** | Voltage let through during a standard UL test surge | Lower is better: 330 V is the lowest rating |
| **Amps / watts** | Maximum continuous load for the whole strip | Often 15 A; stay well under it |

A few notes:

- **Joules** have no single standard test behind them, so compare within a brand rather than across brands. Budget strips often list a few hundred joules; models for computers and home theater commonly list somewhere around 1,000 to 3,000 or more.
- **VPR** comes from UL 1449 testing. For 120 V strips you'll typically see **330 V, 400 V or 500 V**. Marketing may call it "clamping voltage", but VPR is the standardized number.
- **Connected equipment warranties** vary a lot. Read the terms before relying on one.

## 5. Protected and grounded lights

- **Protected** (or "surge protection active"): the surge circuit is still working. If it's **off**, the protection is used up, even if the outlets still work.
- **Grounded** (or "ground OK"): the strip detects a ground connection at the wall outlet. MOVs that divert surges to ground can't do that job without one.

If the grounded light stays off, the outlet may have no working ground. Don't use a two-prong "cheater" adapter to plug in a surge protector. Check the outlet with a plug-in tester, and have an electrician fix a missing ground.

## 6. Which one does each device need?

| Device | Use | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TV, soundbar, streaming box, game console | Surge protector | Costly electronics with sensitive circuit boards |
| Desktop PC, monitor, laptop charger | Surge protector (or a UPS with surge protection) | Protects hardware; a UPS also rides through outages |
| Router, modem, mesh units | Surge protector with coax or Ethernet jacks if possible | Surges can arrive through the cable line too |
| Lamps, phone chargers, fans | Either | Low cost and low draw |
| Space heaters, portable AC, microwaves, hair dryers | Neither: wall outlet only | High wattage can overload a strip |
| Refrigerators, medical equipment | Check the manual | Some makers have specific instructions |

If you're unsure how much a device draws, the label lists watts or amps. This [amps to watts calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/amps-to-watts/) helps you add up a strip's total load against its rating.

## 7. When to replace a surge protector

There's no fixed lifespan, because wear depends on how many surges it has absorbed. Replace it:

- When the **protected light is out** or flickering.
- **After a major surge**, a nearby lightning strike or a power event that damaged other things in the house.
- If the **case is cracked**, the **cord is frayed or pinched**, or any part feels **warm** or looks **discolored**.
- If it's **old with no indicator light**, since you can't tell how much protection is left. Many people replace older strips every few years as a precaution.

A worn-out surge protector often keeps working as a plain power strip, so "it still turns on" doesn't mean it still protects.

## 8. Safe use rules for both

1. **Don't daisy chain.** Never plug a strip into another strip or into an extension cord. It's easy to overload the first one and the wall outlet behind it.
2. **No space heaters or other high-wattage heaters.** Plug space heaters, portable heaters, toaster ovens and similar loads **directly into a wall outlet**.
3. **Don't run cords under rugs**, carpets or furniture, or through doorways where they get pinched. Heat can't escape and damage goes unseen.
4. **Check the amp rating** and add up what you plug in. Many household strips are rated **15 A**, but the wall circuit is shared with other outlets too.
5. **Keep them dry and uncovered.** Don't use indoor strips outside, in bathrooms near water, or buried under bedding or piles of clutter.
6. **Don't use them as permanent wiring.** If you need strips everywhere, that's a sign the room needs more outlets.

A warm strip or plug is a warning sign; see [why an outlet feels warm or hot](/home-safety/outlet-warm-hot/) for what can cause it.

## 9. How it fits with whole-house surge protection

A plug-in surge protector only protects what's plugged into it. A **whole-house surge protector** mounts at your electrical panel and handles larger surges coming in from the utility, including for hardwired loads like HVAC equipment that can't use a strip.

The two work best together: the panel device takes the big hits, and plug-in units catch what gets past it plus smaller surges created inside the home. Neither one can promise protection from a **direct lightning strike**, and neither keeps power on during an outage (that's a UPS). For the panel side, see [whole-house surge protectors explained](/electrical/whole-house-surge-protector/).

## Already had a surge or a hot strip?

- **Unplug the strip** if it's hot, smells burnt or looks melted, and don't reuse it.
- **Check the protected light** on every surge protector in the house after a storm. Replace any that are out.
- **Reset tripped breakers** and GFCIs, then test any outlets that went dead.
- **Note what failed.** Contact your insurer, and the strip maker if it has an equipment warranty, before throwing damaged gear away.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- A wall **outlet** (not just the strip) is **warm, scorched or sparking**, or a breaker keeps tripping.
- The **grounded light** stays off or a tester shows an **open ground** or reversed wiring.
- You rely on **strips in every room** because there aren't enough outlets.
- You want a **whole-house surge protector** installed at the panel.
