A GFCI (ground-fault circuit interrupter) outlet shuts off power when it senses current leaking where it shouldn't, such as through water or a person. Once it trips, it stays off until someone presses **RESET**. That's often why a bathroom, kitchen or outdoor outlet suddenly goes dead.

Resetting takes seconds once you find the right GFCI. The tricky parts are that **one GFCI can protect several other outlets**, so the dead outlet may have no buttons at all, and that **a GFCI won't reset without power** or while a fault is still there.

> **Safety first:** Only touch a GFCI with **dry hands** while standing on a **dry floor**. If the outlet or the area around it is wet, scorched, warm or smells burnt, don't reset it. Turn off its breaker and call a licensed electrician. Pressing TEST and RESET is safe; removing the cover plate or outlet is not part of this job.

## How to reset a GFCI: quick version

1. **Find the GFCI** that controls the dead outlet. It has TEST and RESET buttons.
2. **Unplug everything** on that GFCI and on the outlets it protects.
3. **Press RESET firmly** until it clicks and stays in.
4. **Plug in a lamp** to confirm power is back.
5. **Still dead?** Check the breaker panel, then see the "won't reset" section below.

## 1. Find the GFCI that protects the dead outlet

If the dead outlet has TEST and RESET buttons, that's your GFCI. If it doesn't, it's probably wired after a GFCI somewhere else on the same circuit. A GFCI protects everything connected to its **LOAD** terminals, which can include several ordinary outlets, sometimes in a different room.

**Where to look:**

- **Bathrooms.** In many homes, one bathroom GFCI protects the outlets in another bathroom.
- **Kitchen.** Countertop outlets are often protected by one or two GFCIs along the counter.
- **Garage.** A garage GFCI often protects the outdoor outlets, and sometimes a freezer or opener outlet.
- **Outdoors.** Check patio, deck and porch outlets, including ones behind furniture or grills.
- **Basement and laundry areas.** Unfinished basements, utility sinks and laundry outlets are common spots.

Also check behind appliances, under bathroom sinks and inside cabinets. Look for small "GFCI Protected" stickers on outlets, which point to a GFCI upstream. If you can't find one anywhere, the circuit may be protected by a **GFCI breaker** in the panel (step 5).

## 2. Unplug what's on the circuit

A GFCI may refuse to reset, or trip again right away, if the device that caused the trip is still plugged in. A hair dryer, a wet extension cord or an outdoor light can keep the fault present.

**How:**

- Unplug everything from the GFCI and from the outlets it protects.
- If water got into an outdoor outlet or cord, let it dry fully before resetting. Don't reset a wet outlet.

## 3. Press TEST, then RESET firmly

The two buttons sit between the receptacles. **TEST** trips the outlet on purpose. **RESET** restores power.

**How:**

1. Look at the RESET button. If it's sticking out, the GFCI has tripped.
2. If you're not sure whether it tripped, press **TEST** first. You may hear a click and RESET may pop out. That puts it in a known state.
3. Press **RESET** firmly and fully with your fingertip until you hear or feel a **click** and the button stays in. A light tap often isn't enough.
4. If RESET pops right back out, stop and go to the "won't reset" section. Don't keep pressing it over and over.

## 4. Check the indicator light

Many GFCIs have a small status light, but what it means **varies by brand and model**. On some, a green light means power and protection are on. On others, a red or amber light means the outlet has tripped, and a flashing or steady red light can mean the GFCI has failed and needs replacing. Some have no light at all.

**How:**

- Read the label or the printing on the outlet face, or look up the instructions for your model.
- Don't rely on the light alone. Plug in a lamp or phone charger to confirm there's power.

## 5. Check the breaker, including GFCI breakers

If the GFCI won't reset, there may be no power reaching it. Most modern GFCIs can't reset without power.

**How:**

1. Open the breaker panel and look for a breaker that's in the **OFF** position or sitting in the **middle**, between ON and OFF. A tripped breaker often sits in the middle.
2. Push it **firmly to OFF**, then back to **ON**.
3. Look for **GFCI breakers**. They have their own TEST button on the breaker and protect a whole circuit, often a bathroom, outdoor circuit, hot tub or pool equipment. Reset them the same way: fully OFF, then ON. Some breakers also combine GFCI and AFCI protection and may have an indicator; check the label on the breaker or panel door.
4. Go back and press RESET on the GFCI outlet again.

If the breaker trips again right away or won't stay on, don't keep forcing it. See [what to do when a breaker won't reset](/electrical/breaker-wont-reset/).

## 6. Confirm power is back and plug things in

**How:**

1. Plug a lamp or charger into the GFCI and into each outlet it protects.
2. Plug your devices back in **one at a time**. If the GFCI trips when one goes in, that device likely has a fault.
3. Keep anything that trips it unplugged until it's repaired or replaced.

## Why won't my GFCI reset?

Work through these in order:

- **No power reaching it.** A tripped breaker, a GFCI breaker, or another GFCI further back on the same circuit can leave this one without power. Check the panel and nearby GFCIs first.
- **The ground fault is still there.** Many GFCIs won't latch while current is still leaking. Unplug everything, dry any wet outlets or covers, and try again. If it still won't reset with nothing plugged in, the fault may be in the wiring or an outdoor fixture.
- **The GFCI has failed.** GFCIs wear out. Many newer models test themselves and are designed to refuse to reset, or show a warning light, when they can no longer protect you. If there's power at the outlet but it won't reset, replace it.
- **Line and load are reversed (new installs).** If the GFCI was just installed and has never worked, the incoming power may be on the **LOAD** terminals instead of **LINE**. Most current GFCIs are designed not to reset when wired this way. Also note that many new GFCIs ship tripped, so press RESET once power is on.
- **Something is physically wrong.** A cracked face, a stuck button or signs of heat mean the GFCI should be replaced.

Replacing a GFCI means working inside the box with the breaker off and the wires proven dead. If you're not comfortable with that, it's a quick job for an electrician.

## Resets but trips again?

If the GFCI resets fine but trips again later, or every time you use a certain appliance, the reset procedure isn't the problem. Common causes are moisture, a faulty appliance, a fault on a downstream outlet or a worn-out unit. Our guide to [why a GFCI outlet keeps tripping](/electrical/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/) walks through each one.

A single trip during a storm or after a power outage can happen. If it resets and holds, there's usually nothing to fix.

## Test your GFCI every month

A GFCI can fail without you noticing and keep supplying power without protecting you. Most manufacturers recommend testing **once a month**; check the instructions for your model.

1. Plug in a lamp and turn it on.
2. Press **TEST**. The lamp should go off and RESET should pop out.
3. Press **RESET**. The lamp should come back on.
4. If the lamp stays on after TEST, or RESET won't restore power, **replace the GFCI**.

Test GFCI breakers too, with the TEST button on the breaker, following the manufacturer's instructions. Expect anything on that circuit to switch off during the test.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The GFCI won't reset even with power on, nothing plugged in and everything dry.
- The breaker trips again as soon as you reset it.
- You see **scorch marks**, feel **heat**, or smell **burning** at the outlet or panel.
- A newly installed GFCI has never worked, or you suspect it's wired wrong.
- Outlets are dead and you can't find any tripped GFCI or breaker.

Never replace a GFCI with a regular outlet to stop it from tripping. In places like bathrooms, kitchens, garages, outdoors and basements it's there to protect people from shock.
