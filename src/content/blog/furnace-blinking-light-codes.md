A blinking light on your furnace is the control board talking to you. Most modern gas furnaces have a small **LED on the control board** that flashes a pattern when something has gone wrong, and many also blink a simple pattern when everything is fine.

The meaning of each pattern depends on the furnace maker and model, so there's no universal chart. What you can always do is **count the blinks**, **match them to the chart printed inside your furnace door**, and work out which general area the fault is in. The usual suspects are the **pressure switch, flame sensing, the limit switch and ignition**.

> **Safety first:** If you smell gas, don't touch any switches or the furnace. Leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Turn off power at the furnace service switch before removing any panel, and never bypass a safety switch to keep the furnace running. Make sure you have working carbon monoxide alarms near sleeping areas.

## How to read the code: quick steps

1. **Leave the doors on.** Find the small viewing window or opening in the lower furnace door.
2. **Watch the light for at least two full cycles.** Count the flashes between the longer pauses.
3. **Record it.** Write down the count and the color, or take a short phone video so you can count again later.
4. **Find the chart.** It's usually a label on the inside of the furnace door or in the installation manual.
5. **Read the meaning** for your exact pattern, fix the simple causes in this guide, then reset the furnace once.

## 1. Find the control board light

The control board usually sits behind the **lower door**, the one covering the blower. Many furnaces have a small **clear window or peephole** in that door so you can see the LED without opening it. Some show the code on a small numeric display instead of a single light.

**How:**

- Turn off any nearby lights or use a flashlight at an angle so the LED isn't washed out.
- If there's no window, check the furnace manual. Some models need the door removed to see the board.
- Before you remove a door, record what you can see. Many furnaces have a **door safety switch** that cuts power the moment the blower door comes off, and the code may disappear with it.

## 2. Count the blinks correctly

Most boards flash a **number of times, pause, then repeat**. Some use two digits, such as a few flashes, a short pause, more flashes, then a long pause. Others use different colors or long and short flashes.

**How:**

- Start counting right after a **long pause**, then count again on the next cycle to confirm.
- Note **steady on, steady off, rapid flicker or a slow regular blink**. On many furnaces these mean normal, no power or standby, but it varies.
- Note the **color** if there's more than one LED or the LED changes color.
- A **phone video** is the easiest way to get it right. You can play it back and count slowly.

## 3. Match the pattern to the chart on the door

Furnace makers print a **diagnostic code chart** on a label, usually on the **inside of the blower door** or the burner door, sometimes on the side of the cabinet. It lists each blink pattern and what the board has detected.

**How:**

1. With the code already recorded, turn the furnace off at its **service switch**, then remove the door and read the label.
2. If the label is missing or faded, find the **model number** on the rating plate inside the cabinet, and download the installation or service manual from the maker's website.
3. Write the meaning on a piece of tape near the furnace so you have it next time.

Don't rely on a chart for a different brand or model. Two furnaces can use the same flash count for completely different faults.

## 4. Pressure switch codes

A **pressure switch** confirms the draft inducer fan is pulling combustion gases out through the vent before the furnace lights. If it doesn't close (or stays closed when it shouldn't), the board stops the sequence and flashes a code.

Common causes are a **blocked vent or intake pipe** (snow, ice, leaves or a bird's nest at the outside termination), a **clogged condensate drain** on high-efficiency furnaces, a **cracked or disconnected rubber tube** to the switch, or a failing inducer motor.

**Fix:**

- Outside, check the **plastic vent and intake pipes** where they leave the house. Clear snow, ice or debris by hand.
- On a high-efficiency (condensing) furnace, look for **water backed up** in the drain trap or hose, or a kinked drain line.
- Listen for the **inducer fan** starting before ignition. Loud rattling or no sound at all needs a technician.
- Don't replace the switch just because the code mentions it. The switch is often reporting a venting problem correctly.

## 5. Flame sense and ignition codes

After the burners light, a **flame sensor** (a thin metal rod in the flame) proves to the board that there's a flame. If the board doesn't see it, it shuts off the gas within seconds. Ignition codes mean the furnace tried to light and failed.

Common causes are a **dirty flame sensor**, the **gas supply turned off**, the gas valve control in the OFF position, a worn **hot surface igniter**, or a poor ground connection.

**Fix:**

- Check the **gas shutoff valve** on the pipe to the furnace. The handle is usually in line with the pipe when open.
- Make sure other gas appliances in the house work, which rules out a supply problem.
- Cleaning a flame sensor is a common maintenance job, but it means removing the burner door with the power and gas off. If you're not comfortable working near gas burners, leave it to a technician.
- Many furnaces **lock out** after several failed ignition tries. Some retry on their own after a waiting period. Check your manual for how yours behaves.

## 6. Limit switch codes

The **high limit switch** shuts the burners off if the furnace gets too hot. That usually means air isn't moving through the furnace fast enough to carry the heat away.

**Fix:**

- **Replace the air filter.** This is the most common cause. See [how often to change your furnace filter](/hvac/how-often-change-furnace-filter/).
- Open closed supply vents and clear furniture or rugs off return grilles.
- Make sure the blower door is fully seated.

If the limit code keeps coming back with a clean filter and open vents, the blower motor, its capacitor or the heat exchanger may need checking. A limit switch that trips repeatedly is a reason to call a pro, not to keep resetting.

Some furnaces also have a separate **flame rollout switch** near the burners. A rollout code means flames came out of the burner area, which can point to a blocked heat exchanger or vent. Don't reset it yourself. Call a technician.

## 7. Other codes you may see

Charts often include codes for **reversed polarity or a missing ground**, a **low flame signal**, **blower or motor faults**, **communication errors** with a smart or communicating thermostat, and a **stored fault history**. Treat these the same way: read the chart, check the simple things (power, filter, vents, thermostat wiring), then call a technician.

## Why you shouldn't keep resetting it

A reset clears the code, but not the cause. Repeated resets can be unsafe:

- **Ignition and flame faults:** each failed attempt can let a small amount of unburned gas into the burner area.
- **Limit and rollout faults:** the furnace is overheating or flames are where they shouldn't be.
- **Pressure switch faults:** combustion gases may not be venting properly, which is a carbon monoxide risk.

**Reset once:** turn the service switch or breaker off, wait about 30 seconds, turn it back on and watch what the light does. If the same code returns, stop there.

## No light at all?

- **Check the service switch.** It looks like a light switch on the furnace or a nearby wall. Make sure it's ON.
- **Check the breaker** for the furnace. If it has tripped, push it fully to OFF, then ON. If it trips again, call a technician.
- **Check the blower door** is pushed fully closed so the door switch is pressed in.
- **Check the thermostat** has power and is set to HEAT, a few degrees above room temperature. See [why a furnace won't turn on](/hvac/furnace-not-turning-on/) for more.
- **Look for a blown fuse** on the control board (usually a small automotive-style fuse). A blown fuse often means a wiring short, so a technician should find the cause.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a licensed HVAC technician if:

- You smell gas (leave first and call the gas utility from outside).
- Your carbon monoxide alarm sounds.
- The same code comes back after one reset and a fresh filter.
- The chart points to rollout, a gas valve, a heat exchanger or a control board fault.
- You can't find the chart or the meaning for your model.

Give them the **model number** and the **exact blink pattern**, or show them your video. It helps them bring the right parts on the first visit.
