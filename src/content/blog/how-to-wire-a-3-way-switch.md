A 3-way switch lets **two switches control one light**, like at the top and bottom of a staircase or at both ends of a hallway. Each switch can turn the light on or off no matter where the other one is set, which is why 3-way switches have **no ON or OFF markings**.

Wiring one comes down to three things: knowing **which screw is the common**, connecting **the two traveler wires** between the switches, and keeping track of the wires when you **replace an old switch**.

Codes and permit rules vary by city and county. If you find aluminum wiring, no ground wire, or anything that does not match this guide, stop and call a licensed electrician.

## How a 3-way switch works

A regular single-pole switch simply opens or closes one wire. A 3-way switch works differently: it connects its **common** terminal to one of two **traveler** terminals, flipping between them each time you move the toggle.

Two travelers run between the switches. When both switches connect to the **same traveler**, the circuit is complete and the light is on. Flip either switch and the path breaks, so the light goes off.

## What you need

1. **A replacement 3-way switch** with the same amp rating as the old one (printed on the switch).
2. **A non-contact voltage tester** to confirm the power is off. Our guide to [using a non-contact voltage tester](/tools/non-contact-voltage-tester/) covers how to check it on a live outlet first.
3. **Screwdrivers, wire strippers and needle-nose pliers.**
4. **Tape or wire labels** to mark the common wire.

## 1. Identify the terminals

Every standard 3-way switch has four screws:

- **Common:** one **dark screw**, usually black or dark bronze, sometimes marked COM. This is the one that matters most.
- **Travelers:** two **lighter brass or gold screws**. They are interchangeable.
- **Ground:** a **green screw**, usually on the metal mounting strap.

Screw positions differ between brands, so always go by the **color of the screw**, not its position.

## 2. Know the wires and cable

In a typical setup you will see:

- **Black:** hot, or one of the travelers.
- **Red:** the other traveler, found in 3-wire cable.
- **White:** neutral, normally spliced together in the back of the box with a wire nut and not connected to a standard switch.
- **Bare copper or green:** ground, connected to every switch's green screw and to the fixture.

Between the two switches you need a **3-wire cable with ground**, written as **14/3** or **12/3**. Cable from the panel and to the light is usually **14/2** or **12/2** with ground. Match the wire size to the breaker: **14 AWG on a 15 A breaker** and **12 AWG on a 20 A breaker**. Never put 14 AWG wire on a 20 A circuit.

**White wires used as hot:** sometimes a white wire is used as a traveler or as the hot leg to a switch, especially in older work. When it is, it should be **re-identified with black or red tape** wrapped around it at both ends. If you find an untaped white wire on a switch screw, mark it now.

## 3. Recognize your layout

The two most common layouts are:

- **Power to the first switch, then to the light.** The cable from the panel enters the first switch box. A 3-wire cable runs to the second switch box, and a 2-wire cable runs from there to the light. The diagram below shows this layout.
- **Power at the light.** The cable from the panel goes to the light fixture box first, and cables run down to the switches from there. The wire colors in this layout vary a lot, white wires are often used as hot, and older homes may not have a neutral at the switches.

If you are only replacing a switch, you do not need to work out the whole layout. You just need to move each wire to the matching screw on the new switch.

## 4. Turn off the breaker and test

1. **Turn off the breaker** for the light and put a note on the panel so nobody turns it back on.
2. **Check your tester on a known-live outlet** so you know it works.
3. **Remove the cover plate and the mounting screws**, then gently pull the switch out of the box without touching the terminals.
4. **Test every wire and terminal** in the box, not just the ones on the switch. Boxes often hold wires from more than one circuit.
5. **Recheck the tester on the live outlet.** If the box reads dead and the tester still works, you can start.

## 5. Label the common wire

Before you remove anything, find the **dark common screw** on the old switch and **wrap tape around the wire attached to it**. This one step prevents the most common 3-way wiring mistake.

The wires on the brass screws are the travelers. They are interchangeable, so they do not need labels, though a photo never hurts.

## 6. Move the wires one at a time

1. **Hold the new switch next to the old one** and find its dark common screw.
2. **Move the labeled common wire first**, from the old switch's common screw to the new switch's common screw.
3. **Move each traveler** to one of the new switch's brass screws.
4. **Connect the ground** to the green screw. If the box has a bare ground wire that was not connected, attach it now.
5. Wrap each wire **clockwise** around its screw so tightening pulls it in, then tighten firmly. Many push-in holes on the back of switches accept only 14 AWG copper, so check the switch before using them.
6. **Fold the wires back**, mount the switch, and fit the cover plate.

## 7. Test the switches

Turn the breaker back on and try all four combinations: flip switch 1, then switch 2, then each again. The light should change state **every time you flip either switch**, whatever position the other one is in. If it does not, turn the breaker off again and see the troubleshooting section below.

## Installing a smart 3-way switch

Smart 3-way switches wire differently from standard ones, and models vary a lot. Before you buy:

- **Check for a neutral.** Many smart switches need a **neutral wire** in the box to power their electronics. Look for a bundle of white wires spliced in the back of the box. If there is none, look for a model that states it works without a neutral.
- **Check how the second location works.** Some smart switches replace only one switch and keep the other mechanical 3-way. Many use a **companion switch** (sometimes called an add-on, accessory or remote) at the second location, and some wire the travelers in a non-standard way.
- **Follow the product manual exactly.** Terminal names and wire roles differ between brands, so do not assume the old switch's wiring carries over.

If a smart switch never worked after installation, see our guide to [light switch problems](/electrical/light-switch-not-working/), which covers neutral and companion switch issues.

## Light works only in certain switch positions?

If the light works with one switch only when the other is in a certain position, or only works in one or two of the four combinations, the most likely cause is **a common wire swapped with a traveler** on one of the switches. This often happens after a replacement where the wires were copied by position rather than by screw color.

**Fix:**

1. **Turn off the breaker** and test the box dead.
2. **Check the dark screw** on each switch. In the power-to-first-switch layout, the common at switch 1 should be the hot from the panel, and the common at switch 2 should be the wire going to the light.
3. If you labeled the common, check that it is on the dark screw. If it is on a brass screw, swap it with the wire that is on the dark screw.
4. Also check for a **loose wire** or a cracked switch. A loose traveler causes similar symptoms.

If you did not label the wires and cannot tell which is the common, stop and call an electrician. Finding it usually means testing with the power on, which is not a job for guesswork.

## What about 4-way switches?

To control one light from **three or more places**, you add a **4-way switch** between the two 3-way switches. A 4-way switch has **four traveler terminals** and no common. The two 3-ways still sit at the ends of the run, and each extra location adds another 4-way in the middle. Screw pairing varies by brand, so check its instructions.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The wires are **dull silver aluminum** rather than copper. Standard switches are not rated for aluminum wire, and only devices marked for it (such as CO/ALR) should be used.
- There is **no ground wire** in the box, or the box is metal with no clear ground path.
- You find **scorch marks**, melted insulation, or a switch that feels warm.
- The wire colors do not match what you expect, there are **more wires than this guide shows**, or you cannot identify the common.
- You need **new cable runs**, want to add a switch location, or a permit is required where you live.

If you are not sure you can prove a box dead every time, leave the job to an electrician.
