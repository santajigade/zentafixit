When you flip a light switch and nothing happens, the switch is often not the problem. A burned-out bulb, a tripped breaker or a GFCI somewhere else on the circuit causes many "dead switch" calls. But switches do wear out, and wires can work loose behind the cover.

The most common causes are **a burned-out or loose bulb**, **a tripped breaker or GFCI**, **a loose wire at the switch**, **a worn-out switch**, and, where two switches control one light, **a failed 3-way switch**. Start with the easy checks, then open the switch box only with the power off.

> **Safety first:** Turn off the breaker for the circuit before you remove a switch cover, and confirm the wires are dead with a non-contact voltage tester. Switch boxes can hold wires from more than one circuit. If a switch is warm, buzzing, smells hot or shows scorch marks, leave the breaker off and call an electrician.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Try a known-good bulb** in the fixture. Make sure it screws in snugly.
2. **Check other lights and outlets** in the same room and nearby rooms. Are any of them dead too?
3. **Look at the breaker panel** for a tripped breaker, and press RESET on any GFCI outlets in bathrooms, the kitchen, the garage or outside.
4. **Feel the switch.** Does it click firmly, or feel loose, soft or gritty?
5. **Count the switches.** If two switches control the light, note which positions you've tried.

## 1. A burned-out or loose bulb

It sounds too simple, but a bulb that failed at the same moment you flipped the switch is the most common cause. LED bulbs can also fail early if they overheat in an enclosed fixture that isn't rated for them.

**Fix:**

- Swap in a bulb you know works from another lamp.
- With the power off, look in the socket. If the small brass tab at the bottom is pressed flat, it may not touch the bulb. Once you've confirmed the power is off, gently lift it a little with a screwdriver.
- In multi-bulb fixtures, check every bulb, and make sure the bulbs suit the fixture (enclosed-rated bulbs for closed globes).

## 2. A tripped breaker

If the light and other things nearby went dead at once, the circuit is probably off at the panel. A tripped breaker usually sits in the middle position or looks out of line with the others.

**Fix:**

- Push the breaker fully to **OFF**, then back to **ON**.
- If it trips again right away, or trips often, don't keep resetting it. See [why a breaker keeps tripping](/electrical/breaker-keeps-tripping/).

## 3. A tripped GFCI on the same circuit

Bathroom, garage, basement and outdoor lights are sometimes wired after a GFCI outlet. When that GFCI trips, the light goes out even though the breaker looks fine.

**Fix:**

- Press **RESET** on every GFCI outlet near the room, including ones in the garage, outside walls and other bathrooms.
- If a GFCI won't reset or keeps tripping, see our guide to a [GFCI outlet that keeps tripping](/electrical/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/).

## 4. A loose wire at the switch

Wires can loosen at the screw terminals, or slip out of the push-in holes on the back of the switch (backstab connections). A loose wire gives a light that flickers, works only when you jiggle the switch, or quits completely.

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the breaker and confirm with a non-contact voltage tester that the switch wires are dead.
2. Remove the cover plate and the two screws holding the switch, and gently pull it out of the box.
3. Look for a wire that's loose, burnt or broken. If wires are in the backstab holes, move them to the side screws: wrap each wire clockwise around its screw and tighten firmly.
4. Check any wire nuts in the box. Tug each wire gently. If one slips out, redo the connection.
5. Put the switch back, restore power and test.

## 5. A worn-out switch

Switches have small metal contacts inside that wear out after years of use. A failing switch may feel loose or mushy, stop clicking, or need to be pushed hard to work. Sometimes it fails with no warning at all.

**Fix:**

- Buy a replacement of the **same type**: single-pole (marked ON and OFF, one switch for the light) or 3-way (no ON or OFF markings, two switches for one light). Match the amp rating printed on the old switch.
- Take a photo of the wiring before you disconnect anything, then move one wire at a time to the same position on the new switch.
- If you see dull silver-colored wires instead of copper, the home may have **aluminum wiring**. Don't connect it to a standard switch. Call an electrician.

## 6. A 3-way switch problem

When two switches control one light (at the top and bottom of stairs, for example), they are 3-way switches. If one fails, or a wire is loose at either one, the light may only work with one switch in a certain position, or not at all.

**Fix:**

- Try every combination: switch A up and down with switch B up, then with switch B down. If the light works in some combinations only, one switch or its wiring is the problem.
- 3-way switches have a darker **common** screw plus two **traveler** screws. When replacing one, move the common wire to the common screw of the new switch first. The common is not always the same color wire, so label it before you remove it.
- If you can't tell which wire is which, call an electrician.

## 7. A dimmer, timer or motion switch issue

Dimmers, timers and motion sensor switches have electronics that fail more often than a plain switch. A dimmer may also have a separate on and off control or a low-end trim set so low the light doesn't come on.

**Fix:**

- Check the slider or dial isn't turned all the way down. Some dimmers have a separate small on/off switch.
- For motion switches, check the mode setting (auto, manual or off) and the sensitivity or time delay, as described in the instructions.
- If LED bulbs flicker or won't turn on with a dimmer, the bulb and dimmer may not be compatible. See [LED lights flickering on a dimmer](/electrical/led-lights-flicker-on-dimmer/).
- If the dimmer still doesn't work with a known-good bulb, replace it.

## 8. A smart switch that's offline or miswired

Smart switches need a constant small amount of power to stay connected. Most need a **neutral wire** in the box, which many older homes don't have at switch locations. They also stop responding to the app if Wi-Fi or the hub goes down.

**Fix:**

- Check whether the switch works by pressing the paddle itself. If it does, the problem is the app, Wi-Fi or hub, not the wiring.
- Restart your router and look for an offline status in the app.
- Many smart switches have a small air-gap tab or reset button. Pull the air gap out, wait a few seconds, and push it back in to restart it. Check the instructions for yours.
- If it never worked after installation, recheck the wiring against the instructions, especially the neutral and, for 3-way setups, the companion switch.

## Switch warm, buzzing or smells hot?

- **A slightly warm dimmer** can be normal under load, but a standard switch shouldn't feel warm.
- **Buzzing, crackling, a hot or burning smell, or brown marks** around the switch point to a loose connection that's arcing. Turn off the breaker and leave it off.
- **Don't keep using it.** Arcing connections can start a fire inside the wall. Call an electrician.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The switch or cover plate is hot, scorched, buzzing or smells of burning.
- You find aluminum wiring, cloth-covered wires that crumble, or wires that aren't long enough to work with.
- The breaker trips again after you reset it.
- You've replaced the bulb and switch and the light still doesn't work.
- The box has wiring you can't identify, or more than one circuit.
- You're not comfortable working inside the switch box, or local rules require a licensed electrician.
