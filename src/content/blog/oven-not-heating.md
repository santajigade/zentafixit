An oven that won't heat is usually one of a handful of things. On an **electric** oven, the most common causes are a **burned-out bake element** or **power missing on one side of the 240 V breaker**. On a **gas** oven, it's most often a **weak igniter** that glows but never lets gas flow, or a **closed gas valve** after the range was moved.

Rule out the simple stuff first: a **delay-start timer, a control lock or a demo mode** can make a perfectly good oven look broken. Here's how to work through it in order.

> **Safety first:** Unplug the range or turn off its breaker before you touch an element, remove a panel or use a multimeter. Do resistance checks only with the power off. **If you smell gas, don't touch any switches, don't use the oven, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.** If you're not comfortable working behind panels, stop after the quick test and call an appliance technician.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Check the display** for a lock icon, a timer or delay message, or a demo or showroom notice (cause 1).
2. **Set the oven to BAKE** at a normal temperature and open the door after a few minutes.
3. **Electric:** look at the bake element on the oven floor. If it glows red, the element and power are fine. If it stays dark, try BROIL. If both stay dark, suspect the breaker, a thermal fuse or the control board (causes 2, 7, 8). If only one stays dark, that element is likely bad (cause 3).
4. **Gas:** look through the slots in the oven floor. If the igniter glows but no flame appears after a few minutes, suspect the igniter (cause 4). If the igniter never glows, check the gas valve and power (causes 5, 7, 8).
5. **Heats, but to the wrong temperature?** Go to cause 6.

## 1. A setting is blocking heat (delay start, lock or demo mode)

Many ovens have features that stop them from heating on purpose. A **delay start** (timed bake) waits until a set time. A **control lock** ignores the keypad. **Sabbath mode** changes how the controls respond. **Demo or showroom mode**, used in stores, lights the display without heating, and new ranges sometimes arrive with it switched on.

**Fix:**

- Cancel any timer or delayed cook and start a plain BAKE.
- Look for a lock symbol and unlock the controls.
- If you suspect Sabbath or demo mode, check your manual for the exact way to turn it off. The button combinations differ between brands and models.
- If there's an error code on the display, look it up in your manual. Our guide to [reading appliance error codes](/appliances/how-to-read-appliance-error-codes/) explains how to find the right meaning.

## 2. One side of the 240 V breaker is off (electric)

Electric ranges run on a **240 V double-pole breaker**, which is really two connected breakers feeding two hot wires. The heating elements need both. If only one side has power, the display, clock and oven light may still work and some burners may seem to work or heat weakly, but the oven won't heat. This can happen when a breaker fails internally, or when one of two fuses blows in an older fuse panel.

**Fix:**

1. Find the range breaker in the panel (often labeled RANGE, STOVE or OVEN).
2. Push it firmly to **OFF**, then back to **ON**. A tripped breaker can sit in a middle position that looks almost on.
3. In an older fuse panel, have the range fuses checked and replaced with the correct type and rating.
4. If the breaker trips again or won't reset, stop and see our guide on a [breaker that won't reset](/electrical/breaker-wont-reset/). A loose connection at the range's terminal block or a failing breaker is a job for an electrician.

## 3. A burned-out bake or broil element (electric)

The **bake element** (bottom) does most of the work, and the **broil element** (top) helps during preheat on many ovens. Elements fail with age, often with a bright flash or a pop. A failed element usually shows a **visible break, a bulge or a blistered, bubbled spot**. Some ovens have a hidden bake element under a smooth floor panel, so you can't see it.

Fuses and boards hide behind panels.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the breaker (or unplug the range) and let the oven cool completely.
2. Inspect both elements with a flashlight for breaks, blisters or burn marks.
3. If you want to confirm, remove the screws holding the element to the back wall, pull it forward gently and disconnect its two wires. Set a multimeter to resistance (ohms) and touch the probes to the two terminals. An open reading (OL on most meters) means the element is broken. Otherwise, compare your reading with the value in your service sheet or manual. If ohms are new to you, this explainer on [what electrical resistance is](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/what-is-resistance/) is a good primer.
4. Replace the element with the part number for your model. Most simply screw in and plug onto the same wires.

## 4. A weak igniter (gas)

Most gas ovens use a **glow bar (hot surface) igniter**. The igniter is wired in line with the gas valve, and the valve only opens once enough current flows through the igniter. As igniters age, they can still **glow orange but draw too little current**, so the valve stays shut and the burner never lights.

**Fix:**

- Watch the igniter through the oven floor slots. Glowing for several minutes with no flame points strongly to a weak igniter.
- If it never glows at all, the igniter may be broken, or the problem is power or the control board (causes 7, 8).
- With the power off, an igniter usually comes out with two screws and a plug-in connector. Use the exact part for your model; the igniter and valve are matched.

## 5. The gas supply valve is closed (gas)

Behind most gas ranges there is a **shutoff valve** on the supply line. It's often closed when a range is installed, moved or serviced. On a propane system, an empty tank has the same effect.

**Fix:**

- If your gas cooktop burners light, gas is reaching the range, so skip to cause 4.
- If nothing lights, check the shutoff valve. On a typical lever valve, the handle runs **in line with the pipe when open** and across it when closed.
- Check that the propane tank isn't empty.
- If the gas was off at the meter or the range was just moved, and anything seems wrong with the connection, call your gas utility or a licensed plumber. **Any gas smell means leave and call from outside.**

## 6. A faulty oven temperature sensor

Most modern ovens use a thin **temperature sensor probe** on the back wall to tell the control board how hot it is. If the sensor fails or its wiring is damaged, the oven may heat too much, too little or not at all, and many ovens show an error code.

**Fix:**

- Check that the sensor isn't bent or touching the oven wall. Straighten it gently if your manual allows.
- With the power off, the sensor can be checked with a multimeter on resistance. Compare the reading with the value listed in your service sheet or manual for room temperature.
- A sensor is usually held by two screws and is a common DIY swap.

## 7. A blown thermal fuse or high-limit switch

A **thermal fuse** or **high-limit switch** cuts power to the heat if the oven gets too hot. It most often trips **during or right after a self-clean cycle**, because self-clean runs far hotter than normal baking. Some are one-time fuses, others reset automatically once cool.

**Fix:**

- If the oven quit during or after self-clean, this is a strong suspect.
- The fuse usually sits behind the rear panel or the control panel, so it means pulling the range out and removing panels with the power off.
- A fuse that blew once may be telling you something else is overheating. If you replace it and it blows again, call a technician.

## 8. A failed control board or relay

The **control board** switches the elements or the igniter on through small relays. A relay can stick open or burn out, so the oven clicks or shows that it's preheating but never heats. A board can also fail after a power surge or self-clean.

**Fix:**

- Rule out causes 1 to 7 first.
- Look up any error code in your manual.
- Board replacement is possible for confident DIYers with the exact part number, but diagnosing a board usually takes live voltage tests, which are best left to a technician.

## Oven completely dead?

If the display is blank and nothing works:

- **Check power first.** Reset the range breaker fully (OFF, then ON). For a gas range, check that the 120 V cord is plugged in and the outlet works.
- **Check for a power outage or a tripped GFCI** if the gas range shares a kitchen circuit.
- A dead display with power confirmed usually means the control board, a thermal fuse or the range's wiring. That's a technician job.

## When to call an appliance technician

Call a qualified appliance technician (or an electrician for panel problems) if:

- You smell gas at any point. Leave first, then call your gas utility from outside.
- The breaker trips again after you reset it, or you see scorching at the terminal block or outlet.
- The igniter is new and the burner still won't light. The gas valve may have failed.
- The thermal fuse blows a second time, or you suspect the control board.
