When an air conditioner freezes up, ice forms on the indoor coil and often on the larger copper line running to the outdoor unit. The system keeps running, but the air from your vents gets weak and warm, and you may find water around the indoor unit as the ice melts.

Ice forms when the coil gets too cold. The most common causes are **a dirty air filter**, **closed vents or blocked returns**, **a dirty evaporator coil**, **a weak blower**, and **low refrigerant from a leak**. The first three you can fix yourself.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>Turn cooling off and run the fan on ON until all the ice melts, which can take several hours. Then replace the air filter, open every supply vent and clear the return grilles. If it freezes again with a clean filter and good airflow, the coil may be dirty or the system may be low on refrigerant, so call an HVAC technician.</p></div>

> **Safety first:** Don't chip, scrape or pry ice off the coil or lines. The coil fins and copper tubing damage easily, and a punctured line releases refrigerant. Turn off power at the breaker before opening any panel on the indoor unit.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Turn cooling off** and set the fan to ON. Leave the ice to melt.
2. **Pull out the air filter** and hold it up to a light. If you can't see light through it, it's clogged.
3. **Walk the house.** Count closed or blocked supply vents and look for furniture or rugs over return grilles.
4. **Think about the timing.** Did it freeze on a cool night or right after a long run on a hot day?
5. **Once it's thawed**, run cooling with a new filter for a day. If ice returns, look at causes 3, 4 and 6.

## How to thaw a frozen AC safely

Thaw the ice before you test anything else. Most causes can't be checked properly while the coil is frozen.

**Fix:**

1. Set the thermostat to **OFF** for cooling and the fan to **ON**. The blower pushes room air over the coil to melt the ice.
2. Put towels around the indoor unit and keep an eye on the drain pan. Melting ice makes a lot of water.
3. Wait until the coil and the larger copper line are completely clear of ice. This often takes a few hours, and a heavily frozen coil can take most of a day.
4. If the indoor unit is in a hot attic or the fan won't run, turn the whole system off and let it thaw on its own. That's slower but safe.
5. Replace the filter before you turn cooling back on.

## 1. A dirty air filter

A clogged filter is the most common cause of a frozen AC. It chokes the airflow, so the coil gets colder than it should and the moisture on it turns to ice.

**Fix:**

- Replace the filter with the size printed on the old filter's frame. Point the airflow arrow toward the blower.
- Check it monthly in cooling season. Many households need a new filter every 1 to 3 months, more often with pets or dust. See [how often to change your furnace filter](/hvac/how-often-change-furnace-filter/).
- A very restrictive high-MERV filter can also cut airflow on some systems. If your system freezes with a brand new filter, try the rating your equipment manual recommends.

## 2. Closed vents or blocked returns

Closing vents in unused rooms seems like it saves energy, but it reduces the total airflow over the coil. Blocked return grilles do the same.

**Fix:**

- Open every supply vent in the house.
- Move furniture, rugs and curtains away from return grilles.
- Check for a closed damper lever on the ductwork near the indoor unit, if you have one, and ask a technician before changing a damper that was set on purpose.

## 3. A dirty evaporator coil

Dust that slips past a filter builds up on the indoor coil over time. A matted coil blocks air just like a dirty filter does, and it's often the reason a system that used to run fine now freezes.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/ac-unit-freezing-up-causes.svg" alt="Comparison chart of AC freezing causes: dirty filter, blocked vents and cool weather are do it yourself fixes; dirty coil and weak blower may be do it yourself or pro; low refrigerant and refrigerant restrictions are pro only" width="640" height="400" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Start with the fixes you can do, then call a pro if the ice comes back.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

- With the power off, look at the coil through the access panel if it's easy to reach. A gray, felt-like layer means it needs cleaning.
- A no-rinse evaporator coil cleaner made for AC coils can help with light dust, if your coil is accessible. Follow the label.
- Many coils are sealed inside the cabinet. Cleaning those properly is part of a technician's maintenance visit.

## 4. A weak or failing blower

The indoor blower moves all the air across the coil. A blower wheel caked with dust, a slipping belt on older units, a failing motor or a weak capacitor slows it down, and the coil freezes.

**Fix:**

- Feel the airflow at a vent near the indoor unit. If it's weak even with a clean filter and the coil thawed, suspect the blower.
- Listen for a hum, grinding or squeal from the indoor unit.
- Cleaning the blower wheel, replacing a motor or capacitor, and setting the fan speed are jobs for an HVAC technician.

## 5. Running the AC when it's cool outside

Central AC is designed for warm weather. On cool nights the pressures in the system drop and the indoor coil can get cold enough to freeze. Many manufacturers advise against running cooling when it's cool outside, often below about 60 °F, but check your manual.

**Fix:**

- On cool days, open windows or just run the fan instead of cooling.
- Avoid setting the thermostat very low for long periods. Pick a sensible setting and let the system cycle.
- If you have a heat pump and see frost on the **outdoor** unit in winter, that's normal. It runs a defrost cycle on its own. See our guide to a [heat pump blowing cold air](/hvac/heat-pump-blowing-cold-air/) if it never clears.

## 6. Low refrigerant or a leak (pro only)

Refrigerant isn't used up like fuel, so if the system is low, it has a leak. Low refrigerant drops the pressure in the indoor coil, which makes it colder, and ice forms. You'll often see frost on the larger insulated copper line at the outdoor unit, and sometimes hear hissing or bubbling.

**Fix:** Call a licensed HVAC technician. Handling refrigerant requires EPA certification, and simply adding refrigerant without fixing the leak only delays the next freeze-up. Ask the technician to find the leak and explain the repair options.

## 7. A restriction in the refrigerant system (pro only)

A clogged filter drier, a kinked line or a failing metering device (the part that controls refrigerant flow into the coil) can make the coil run too cold even with a full charge. These look a lot like low refrigerant from the outside.

**Fix:** A technician needs to measure system pressures and temperatures to tell these apart. Don't try to bend lines or open the refrigerant circuit yourself.

## Water leaking while it thaws?

- **Some water is normal.** A thawing coil drips into the drain pan and out through the condensate line.
- **Pan overflowing:** the drain line may be clogged. With the power off, use a wet/dry vacuum on the outdoor end of the condensate line to pull out the clog. Many systems have a float switch that shuts the AC off when the pan fills, and it resets once the water drains.
- **Water near ceilings or electrical parts:** turn the system off at the breaker and call a technician.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a licensed HVAC technician if:

- The AC freezes again after you've thawed it, replaced the filter and opened every vent.
- You see frost on the outdoor copper line, hear hissing, or suspect a leak.
- Airflow is weak even with a clean filter, or the blower makes a grinding, squealing or humming noise.
- The coil is dirty and sealed inside the cabinet.
- You see oil stains on the copper lines or fittings, which can mark a leak.

Once it's fixed, keep the filter fresh, the vents open and the yearly maintenance visit on the calendar. If it thaws but still won't cool, see [AC running but not cooling](/hvac/ac-running-not-cooling/).
