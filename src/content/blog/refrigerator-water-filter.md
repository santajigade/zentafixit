The water filter in your refrigerator cleans the water that goes to the door dispenser and the ice maker. Over time it fills up with what it traps, the flow slows down, and it stops doing its job. Changing it is one of the easiest jobs in the house, and most people can do it in about 10 minutes.

What matters is getting the **timing** right, buying a filter that **matches your model** and is **properly certified**, and **flushing the new filter** before you drink from it.

> **Before you start:** Most refrigerator filters are made for water that is already safe to drink, such as treated city water. They improve taste and reduce certain contaminants, but they are not designed to make unsafe water safe. If you're on a private well or have a water quality concern, test your water first.

## How to change it: overview

1. **Find your filter and the right replacement** using your refrigerator's model number.
2. **Put a towel down** to catch drips.
3. **Remove the old filter** with a twist or a push of its release button.
4. **Install the new filter** until it seats or clicks.
5. **Flush it** by running water through the dispenser, then **reset the filter light**.

## 1. Know when it's due

Most refrigerator makers recommend changing the filter about **every 6 months**. Each filter is also rated for a certain number of gallons. Whichever comes first is when it's due. A large household that uses a lot of water and ice may reach the gallon limit before 6 months.

Signs it's time:

- The **filter light** or message on the fridge comes on. Many fridges base this on time or water use, not on the filter's actual condition, so treat it as a reminder.
- **Water flow from the dispenser slows** to a trickle.
- **Water or ice tastes or smells** like chlorine, metal or the freezer.
- **Ice cubes are smaller** or the ice maker makes less ice than usual.

Write the date on the new filter with a marker, or set a phone reminder, so you don't have to guess next time.

## 2. Find the filter and the right replacement

Filters come in different shapes, and the location depends on the fridge. Look for the model number on a sticker inside the fresh food section, on the side wall or ceiling, or behind the bottom grille, then use the manual or the maker's website to find the filter part number.

Common locations:

- **Inside the fresh food section,** in the upper right corner, the ceiling, or a cover near the crisper drawers.
- **Behind the grille at the bottom front** (the base grille or kick plate), usually with a push button or a twist cap.
- **On the water line behind the fridge,** as an in-line filter, mostly on older or basic models.

Write down the part number from the old filter before you remove it. It's the surest way to buy the right one.

## 3. Change the filter step by step

The steps vary a little by style, so follow the label on the filter cover or your manual if it differs.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/refrigerator-water-filter-steps.svg" alt="Four steps to change a refrigerator water filter: remove the old filter with a quarter turn or the release button, take the cap off the new filter, push and turn it in until it locks, then run water through the dispenser to flush it" width="640" height="300" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Most filters lock in with a quarter turn or a push.</figcaption></figure>

**Twist-in filters (most inside and base grille filters):**

1. Put a towel under the filter. A little water will spill.
2. Turn the old filter about a quarter turn counterclockwise, or as the arrows show, and pull it straight out.
3. Remove the protective cap from the new filter. Compare it to the old one to be sure the shape matches.
4. Push the new filter in and turn it clockwise until it stops or lines up with the mark. Don't force it.

**Push-in filters:**

1. Press the release button or push the filter in to unlatch it, then pull it out.
2. Slide the new filter in until it clicks into place.

**In-line filters behind the fridge:**

1. Turn off the water supply valve to the fridge.
2. Disconnect the filter from the tubing using the fittings, with a towel underneath.
3. Connect the new filter with the flow arrow pointing toward the fridge, then turn the water back on and check for drips.

If a twist-in filter is stuck, make sure you are turning it the right way. Don't use tools on the filter housing, which can crack it.

## 4. Flush the new filter

A new filter contains air and some loose carbon dust. Flushing clears both. Most makers say to run somewhere around 2 to 4 gallons through the dispenser, but follow the amount in your manual or on the filter box.

**How to flush:**

- Hold a large pitcher under the dispenser and press for short bursts, then longer runs. Pour the water down the drain or onto plants.
- Expect sputtering and spitting at first. That's air in the line, and it stops as you keep going.
- Expect the water to look cloudy or have tiny black specks at first. That's air bubbles and carbon from the new filter, and it clears as you flush.
- If your fridge has an ice maker, throw out the first batch or two of ice made after the change.

Check around the filter for drips after flushing. If it leaks, take it out and reinstall it, making sure it's fully seated. For a fridge that keeps leaking, see our guide to a [refrigerator leaking water](/appliances/refrigerator-leaking-water/).

## 5. Reset the filter light

Most fridges don't know you changed the filter until you reset the reminder. On many models you press and hold a button labeled something like **Filter**, **Reset Filter** or **Water Filter** for a few seconds until the light changes. Some models use a combination of buttons or a menu on a display.

The exact method varies by brand and model, so check your manual or the inside of the door if this doesn't work.

## 6. Choose a certified filter

The filter that fits your fridge is not always the one that filters well. Brand-name and generic filters can both be good, but only certified ones have been independently tested.

Look for certification from an accredited body such as **NSF**, **IAPMO** or **WQA** to these NSF/ANSI standards:

| Standard | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| NSF/ANSI 42 | Taste and odor, such as chlorine |
| NSF/ANSI 53 | Specific health related contaminants, such as lead, as listed on the filter |
| NSF/ANSI 401 | Certain emerging contaminants, as listed on the filter |
| NSF/ANSI 372 | Lead content of the filter's own materials |

Read the list of contaminants each filter is certified to reduce. Two filters can both say "NSF certified" and cover very different things. Prices range widely, roughly $15 to $60 or more, depending on brand and certifications.

## 7. Get the water and ice right after the change

A new filter can take a little settling in. Most small issues after a change are temporary.

- **Slow flow right after:** keep flushing. If it stays slow, the filter may not be fully seated, or the water supply valve may be partly closed.
- **No water at all:** the filter may be the wrong model or not locked in. Remove it and reinstall it carefully.
- **Ice tastes old:** empty the ice bin, wash it, and let the ice maker refill it with fresh ice from the new filter.

If the ice maker still won't work after the change, see our guide to an [ice maker that's not working](/appliances/ice-maker-not-working/).

## Water still slow or not dispensing?

- **Check the supply valve** behind the fridge or under the sink is fully open.
- **Check the tubing** behind the fridge isn't kinked after pushing the fridge back.
- **Check your home's water pressure.** Many fridges need a minimum pressure to dispense properly, listed in the manual.
- **Check for a frozen line** in the door if the fridge is set very cold. Set the fresh food section to around 37°F and give it time.

## When to call a pro

Call an appliance repair technician or a plumber if:

- Water leaks from the filter housing even with a new filter correctly installed.
- The dispenser doesn't work even with the filter seated and the supply valve open.
- The water line behind the fridge is cracked, leaking or corroded at the valve.
- You want a new water line run to a refrigerator that doesn't have one.

A leaking line behind a fridge can damage floors for weeks before you notice, so fix drips promptly.
