**Phantom power** (also called standby or vampire power) is the electricity devices use when they look switched off. A TV waiting for its remote, a cable box keeping its clock and guide updated, a game console ready to download updates: each draws a little power, all day, every day.

One device rarely matters much. But a home can have dozens, and **a few bad ones** (cable and satellite boxes, game consoles in instant-on mode, older electronics) do most of the damage. The way to find them is to **measure**, then cut power only where it's worth it.

## How to find standby power: the short version

1. **Get a plug-in watt meter** or a smart plug with energy monitoring.
2. **Measure each device switched off**, starting with your TV area and home office.
3. **Write down the watts** and work out the yearly cost.
4. **Cut the big ones** with a smart strip, a timer, a switch or a settings change.
5. **Leave alone** anything that must stay on, and anything using under about 1 W.

## 1. Understand what standby power is

Most electronics never fully turn off when you press the power button. They keep a small circuit running so they can respond to a remote, keep a clock, stay connected to Wi-Fi or download updates. Power adapters (the boxes on cords) can also draw a little even with nothing attached.

The US Department of Energy has estimated that standby power can account for roughly 5 to 10 percent of home electricity use. Your share depends on what you own and how it's set up, which is why measuring beats guessing.

## 2. Get a plug-in watt meter

A **plug-in watt meter** (sometimes called an electricity usage monitor) sits between the device and the wall outlet and shows how many watts flow through it. Basic ones cost roughly $15 to $30 at hardware stores and online. Many also total up kWh over time.

A **smart plug with energy monitoring** does the same job and shows readings in an app. Note that the smart plug itself uses a little standby power.

Limits to know:

- They work on standard **120 V plug-in devices** only. They can't measure hardwired or 240 V appliances like the water heater, furnace or electric dryer.
- Check the meter's **maximum load** before using it with a space heater or anything that draws a lot of power.

## 3. Measure each device in its off state

**How:**

1. Plug the device into the watt meter, and the meter into the wall.
2. Turn the device **off the normal way** (remote, power button or sleep), the way it usually sits.
3. Wait a minute or two for the reading to settle, then note the **watts**.
4. For devices whose draw rises and falls, such as a cable box downloading guide data, use the meter's **kWh mode for 24 hours** and divide by 24 to get average watts. (kWh ÷ 24 × 1,000 gives watts.)
5. For a group on one power strip, plug the whole strip into the meter to measure them together, then split them up to find the worst one.

## 4. Know the usual culprits

Standby use varies widely between models and settings. These rough ranges show where to look first. **Always measure your own.**

| Device | Typical standby range (rough) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cable or satellite box with DVR | about 10 to 30 W | Often near its full power even "off" |
| Game console in instant-on or rest mode | about 1 to 15 W | Depends heavily on power settings |
| Desktop PC (sleep or off) | about 1 to 5 W | Sleep uses more than shutdown |
| Older TVs, receivers, sound systems | about 1 to 10 W | Newer models usually lower |
| Newer TV in standby | about 1 W or less | Quick start and voice features can raise it |
| Smart speakers and displays | about 1 to 5 W | Always listening by design |
| Printers (sleep) | about 1 to 5 W | Inkjets may need power for maintenance |
| Microwave or coffee maker with clock | about 1 to 3 W | Small but always on |
| Phone charger, nothing attached | well under 1 W | Not worth worrying about |

Your **modem and router** also run 24 hours a day, but they're working, not idling. Most people leave them on.

## 5. Work out what it costs

The formula:

**Watts × hours ÷ 1,000 × rate per kWh = cost**

A device that's always plugged in runs about **8,760 hours a year** (24 × 365).

**Example (assumed rate of 15 cents per kWh, check your bill):**

- A cable box at **20 W**: 20 × 8,760 ÷ 1,000 = 175 kWh a year, × $0.15 = **about $26 a year**.
- A console left in instant-on at **10 W**: 88 kWh, **about $13 a year**.
- Add up everything you measured and you have your total standby cost.

Your electric bill shows your actual rate (look for the price per kWh, including delivery charges if listed separately). This [electricity cost calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/everyday-life/electricity-cost/) does the math for any device.

## 6. Use smart power strips and timers

Once you know which groups waste power, cut it without remembering to unplug things.

- **Smart power strips (load-sensing):** one "control" outlet for the main device, such as a TV or computer. When it turns off, the strip cuts power to the "switched" outlets for the soundbar, console, speakers and printer. Some also have "always on" outlets.
- **Advanced power strips** may also turn things off after a period of no activity, using a motion sensor or remote control signals.
- **Plug-in timers or smart plugs** can cut power on a schedule, for example to a home office setup or a coffee maker overnight.
- A **regular power strip with a switch** works too, if you'll actually use it.

Don't put devices that need constant power on a switched outlet: the router, a DVR that records shows, or anything that needs overnight updates.

## 7. Change settings like instant-on and quick start

Many devices let you trade a few seconds of startup time for lower standby power. Look in the power or system settings for options with names like:

- **Instant-on, quick start, fast start or rest mode** (TVs and game consoles). Choose the energy-saving or full shutdown option.
- **Network standby, wake on LAN or remote start** (TVs, receivers, soundbars, PCs).
- **Auto power off or sleep timers** (printers, monitors, speakers, PCs).

Names and menus differ between brands and models, so check your manual or the maker's support page.

## 8. Know what to leave plugged in

Some things should stay on even though they draw standby power:

- Refrigerators, freezers, sump pumps and well pumps
- Medical equipment and anything with an alarm or monitoring function
- Security systems, smoke and CO alarms, and doorbell transformers
- Modems and routers (unless you're fine with them going offline)
- DVRs that record, and devices set to install updates overnight
- Inkjet printers, if the manual warns that cutting power stops maintenance cycles

## Want to check the whole house?

Look at your home's **baseline**: the power it draws in the middle of the night when nothing major is running. Many utilities with smart meters show hourly use online. Find the lowest hours overnight, ignore the fridge and HVAC cycling, and that floor is roughly your always-on load. A high floor that you can't explain with your measurements is worth chasing. For bigger jumps in your bill, see [why your electric bill is suddenly high](/solar-power/electric-bill-suddenly-high/).

## When to call an electrician

Standby power is a DIY job. Call a licensed electrician if:

- An outlet, plug or power strip feels **warm or smells hot**, even with little plugged in.
- Your baseline is high and you suspect a **hardwired load** you can't measure, such as heat tape, a pump that runs constantly or an old electric heater.
- You want a **whole-home energy monitor** installed in your electrical panel. That work happens inside the panel and isn't DIY.
