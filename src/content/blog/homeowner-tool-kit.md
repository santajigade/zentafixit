You don't need a garage full of tools to handle most jobs around the house. A **small set of good hand tools**, a **cordless drill** and a couple of **electrical testers** cover hanging pictures, tightening loose hinges, assembling furniture, swapping a light fixture and most of the fixes on this site.

The trick is to **buy the everyday tools first**, choose decent quality for the ones you'll use most and add specialty tools only when a real job calls for them.

## How to build your kit: quick plan

1. **Buy the first column** of the chart above. These tools handle the majority of small jobs.
2. **Add a cordless drill** as soon as you start hanging shelves, curtain rods or anything heavy.
3. **Add a level and a stud finder** before you mount anything on a wall.
4. **Add electrical testers** before you change an outlet, switch or light fixture.
5. **Keep it all in one place**, such as a toolbox or tool bag, so you're not hunting for a screwdriver mid-job.

## 1. Screwdrivers: the tool you'll use most

Most household screws are **Phillips**, with **slotted** (flathead) close behind. You'll also run into square-drive screws and star-shaped Torx screws on appliances and furniture.

**What to buy:**

- A **multi-bit screwdriver** that stores Phillips, slotted, square and Torx bits in the handle covers a lot in one tool.
- Add a few **dedicated screwdrivers**: #1 and #2 Phillips, and a small and medium slotted. The #2 Phillips is the size you'll reach for most.
- A **precision screwdriver set** helps with eyeglasses, toys, battery covers and electronics.
- Look for comfortable handles and hardened tips that don't round off.

## 2. Tape measure and level

Measuring and leveling are part of nearly every hanging, mounting and furniture job.

**What to buy:**

- A **25-foot tape measure** with a wide, stiff blade that stays straight when extended. Shorter tapes are handy in a drawer but run out fast on room measurements.
- A **24-inch level** for shelves, pictures and TV mounts, plus a small **torpedo level** for tight spaces.
- Many phones have a level app. It's fine for a quick check, but a real level is more reliable along a long shelf.

## 3. Hammer and utility knife

**What to buy:**

- A **16-ounce curved claw hammer** is the standard all-purpose size. The curved claw pulls nails well. A fiberglass or steel handle with a rubber grip is easy to control.
- A **retractable utility knife** for opening boxes, cutting carpet, scoring drywall and trimming caulk. Buy **spare blades**. A dull blade slips and is more dangerous than a sharp one.

Always cut away from your body and retract the blade when you put the knife down.

## 4. Pliers and an adjustable wrench

**What to buy:**

- **Slip-joint pliers** for general gripping.
- **Needle-nose pliers** for small parts, bending wire and reaching into tight spots.
- **Tongue-and-groove pliers** (adjustable, also called groove-joint pliers) for gripping pipes, drain nuts and larger fittings under sinks.
- An **8 or 10-inch adjustable wrench** for nuts and bolts on furniture, appliances and plumbing fittings. Tighten the jaws snugly to avoid rounding the nut.

Later, a **wire stripper** is worth adding if you plan to replace outlets, switches or fixtures.

## 5. A cordless drill

A cordless drill/driver drills holes and drives screws, and it saves a lot of time and wrist strain. It's the one power tool most homeowners should own.

**What to look for:**

- **Size:** compact **12V class** drills are light and handle most household jobs. **18V or 20V max class** drills have more power for hardwood, masonry and large holes. Both are good choices.
- **Adjustable clutch:** lets you drive screws without stripping them or sinking them too deep.
- **Two batteries** if you can, so one charges while you work.
- **Bits:** a basic set of drill bits for wood and metal, a few masonry bits and a set of driver bits.

If you expect to add more cordless tools later, pick a battery platform you're happy to stay with, since batteries usually only fit one brand's tools.

## 6. A stud finder

To hang heavy shelves, a TV or a large mirror, you need to screw into the wood studs behind drywall, not just the drywall.

**What to buy:**

- An **electronic stud finder** that marks the edges or the center of a stud.
- A simple **magnetic stud finder**, which finds the screws or nails holding the drywall, is a cheap backup and works through some surfaces that confuse electronic ones.

Many electronic stud finders also have an AC wire warning. Treat it as a hint, not a guarantee. Before drilling, avoid areas directly above or below outlets and switches, where wires usually run.

## 7. A non-contact voltage tester and a multimeter

If you'll ever replace an outlet, a switch or a light fixture, you need a way to check for power. These two testers do different jobs.

**What to buy:**

- A **non-contact voltage tester** beeps or lights up near a live AC wire. It's fast and easy for a first check. See [how to use a non-contact voltage tester](/tools/non-contact-voltage-tester/) for the right way to use one and its limits.
- A **digital multimeter** with AC volts, DC volts, resistance and continuity. It shows the actual voltage, tests batteries and fuses, and gives a more reliable answer on whether a circuit is dead. Here's [how to test an outlet with a multimeter](/tools/how-to-test-an-outlet-with-a-multimeter/).
- Optional: a **plug-in outlet tester** that shows common wiring faults with a pattern of lights.

> **Safety first:** Choose testers with a **CAT II or higher** safety rating for household outlets, and **CAT III** if you might work in a breaker panel. The rating is printed on the tester. Always check a tester on a known-live outlet before trusting it to tell you something is dead.

## 8. Flashlight and safety glasses

**What to buy:**

- A bright **LED flashlight** and a **headlamp**. The headlamp keeps both hands free under sinks, in attics and at the breaker panel during an outage.
- **Safety glasses** marked **ANSI Z87.1**. Wear them when drilling, hammering, cutting, or working overhead where dust can fall.

Good additions: **work gloves**, **hearing protection** for loud power tools and **dust masks** for sanding or drilling drywall.

## Where to spend and where to save

- **Spend more** on the tools you'll use most and that affect safety or results: screwdrivers, the tape measure, the cordless drill and your electrical testers.
- **Save** on tools you'll use rarely, such as a torpedo level, a magnetic stud finder or a precision screwdriver set.
- **Pre-packaged kits** can be good value for a first home, but check that they include full-size tools. Many pad the piece count with small bits.
- **Read the warranty.** Many hand tools carry long warranties, which is a good sign of quality.

## What can wait

Skip these until a real job needs them, then decide whether to buy or rent:

- Circular saws, miter saws and other large power tools.
- Specialty plumbing tools such as a basin wrench or a drain snake.
- Ladders taller than a step stool, unless you already need one for gutters or ceiling fixtures.

## When to call a pro

The right tools make small jobs easy, but they don't replace training. Call a licensed professional for:

- **Electrical work** beyond swapping like-for-like devices, such as new circuits, panel work or anything involving aluminum wiring.
- **Gas** lines and gas appliances.
- **Plumbing** that involves cutting into supply lines or moving drains.
- **Roof and high ladder work.**

When in doubt, check your local permit rules. Some jobs need a permit even when you do the work yourself.
