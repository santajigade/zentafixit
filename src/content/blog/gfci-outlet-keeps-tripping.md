A GFCI (ground-fault circuit interrupter) outlet cuts power in a fraction of a second when it senses electricity leaking to ground, for example through water or a person. It trips at a tiny imbalance of around 5 milliamps, so it's sensitive by design.

When one keeps tripping, the cause is usually **moisture, a faulty appliance, or something wired downstream**. Here's how to find which.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/gfci-anatomy.svg" alt="Diagram of a GFCI outlet showing the TEST and RESET buttons, status light, hot slot, neutral slot and ground hole" width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Tap the diagram to enlarge. The TEST and RESET buttons sit between the two receptacles.</figcaption></figure>

It works by comparing the current going out on the hot wire with the current coming back on the neutral. If they differ, some current is escaping, and the GFCI cuts power. (For the engineering side of grounding, see this explainer on [earthing systems and how fault current returns](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/tn-tt-it-earthing-systems/).)

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>Most tripping GFCIs are caused by water in an outlet box, an appliance with a small insulation fault, or a problem on an outlet wired after the GFCI. A GFCI that trips with nothing connected and no moisture is often simply worn out.</p></div>

> **Safety first:** Work only with dry hands and dry floors. Before removing any cover plate or outlet, turn off the circuit at the breaker and confirm it's dead with a non-contact voltage tester. If you're not comfortable working inside a box, stop at Step 4 and call a licensed electrician.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

<figure><img src="/images/posts/gfci-diagnosis-flow.svg" alt="Flowchart: unplug everything, press reset and wait 10 minutes; if it trips again the problem is wiring, moisture or the GFCI, otherwise plug devices back one at a time" width="640" height="430" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>The 10-minute test splits the problem in two.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Unplug everything** from the GFCI and from any outlets it might protect (often other outlets in the same bathroom, kitchen, garage or outside wall).
2. **Press RESET.** If it won't reset at all, skip to the "Won't reset" section below.
3. **Wait 10 minutes.**
    - **Trips with nothing plugged in?** The problem is in the wiring, moisture, or the GFCI itself (causes 1, 3, 4, 5).
    - **Stays on?** Plug devices back in **one at a time**, waiting a few minutes after each. The one that makes it trip is your culprit (cause 2).

## 1. Moisture in an outlet or box

Water is the most common trigger, especially for **outdoor, bathroom, garage and basement** outlets. Rain, condensation, a leaking pipe or steam from a shower can create a small leakage path.

**Fix:**

- Check for water inside the outlet box (power off first) and let it dry fully.
- Fit a weatherproof **"in-use" bubble cover** on outdoor outlets. These stay sealed even with a cord plugged in.
- If it trips only during rain, the moisture may be in an exterior light fixture or buried cable on the same circuit. Have an electrician check it.

## 2. A faulty appliance or cord

Appliances with heating elements or motors (hair dryers, space heaters, kettles, washing machines, refrigerators, sump pumps, power tools) can develop small insulation faults as they age. The GFCI notices before you would.

**Fix:**

- Use the one-at-a-time test above to find the device.
- Try the suspect device on a different GFCI. If it trips that one too, the device is the problem.
- Inspect its cord for cuts, crushed spots or burnt plug blades. Repair or replace the device. Don't plug it into a non-GFCI outlet to "solve" it.

## 3. Something on the downstream (LOAD) side

A single GFCI often protects other regular outlets wired after it through its **LOAD** terminals. A fault anywhere downstream (a wet outdoor outlet, a damaged cable, a loose connection) trips the first GFCI, even if that outlet looks fine.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/gfci-line-load.svg" alt="Back of a GFCI outlet: incoming hot and neutral wires on the LINE terminals, wires to other outlets on the LOAD terminals" width="640" height="440" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Anything connected to LOAD is protected by this GFCI, and a fault there trips it.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

- Find which outlets lose power when the GFCI trips. Those are downstream.
- Check each of them for moisture, damage or a plugged-in device.
- If you're comfortable doing so (power off and verified), an electrician's trick is to disconnect the LOAD wires temporarily. If the GFCI stops tripping, the fault is downstream.

## 4. A worn-out GFCI

GFCIs don't last forever. Older units can become oversensitive and trip randomly, or fail so they no longer protect at all.

**Fix:**

- Press **TEST**. If it doesn't click off, or won't reset after testing, replace it.
- If it trips with nothing connected, no moisture, and nothing downstream, a worn unit is the likely cause.
- Replacements cost roughly $15 to $30. Newer models self-test and show a warning light when they fail.

## 5. Wiring problems

Incorrect or loose wiring can cause tripping, especially after a recent DIY job.

Common issues:

- **Line and load reversed:** the incoming power is on the LOAD terminals. Most GFCIs made since 2006 won't reset at all if wired this way.
- **Shared neutral:** the neutral from another circuit is connected to the GFCI's load side, which the GFCI reads as leakage.
- **Loose connections or backstabbed wires** that heat up or arc.

**Fix:** These need an electrician unless you're experienced with home wiring.

## 6. Too many GFCIs or very long circuits

Every long cable and every motor leaks a tiny amount of current to ground. On a long outdoor run or a circuit with several motor loads, these small amounts can add up toward the trip point. Two GFCIs protecting the same outlets can also cause confusion when you're trying to reset.

**Fix:** Ask an electrician to split heavy motor loads onto separate GFCI-protected circuits, or move protection closer to the load.

## 7. Power surges and storms

A nearby lightning strike or utility switching can trip a GFCI once. That's normal. If it resets and holds, there's nothing to fix. Repeated tripping during storms usually points back to moisture (cause 1).

## GFCI won't reset at all?

- **No power reaching it.** Check the breaker panel. The breaker may have tripped too. Reset it fully (push it to OFF, then ON).
- **An active fault.** Many GFCIs refuse to reset while a ground fault is present. Unplug everything and try again.
- **Miswired (line/load reversed)** or **failed unit.** Call an electrician or replace the GFCI.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- It trips with nothing plugged in and no moisture, and a new GFCI doesn't fix it.
- You see scorch marks, smell burning, or feel heat at the outlet.
- The breaker trips along with the GFCI.
- It trips whenever it rains and you can't find the wet spot.

Never replace a GFCI with a regular outlet where one is required (bathrooms, kitchens, garages, outdoors, basements and laundry areas under current US electrical codes). It's there to prevent shocks.
