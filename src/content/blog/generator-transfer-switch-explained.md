A portable generator can keep your fridge, furnace, sump pump and lights running in an outage, if it connects to your house the safe way. There are **three proper options**: a **manual transfer switch**, an **interlock kit** on your main panel, or an **automatic transfer switch** (mostly used with permanent standby generators).

All three do the same essential job: they make it **impossible for the generator and the utility to feed your wiring at the same time**. The one thing you should never do is plug the generator into a dryer or wall outlet with a homemade double-plug cord.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>An interlock kit is usually the simplest and lowest-cost choice if one is listed for your exact panel, and it lets you choose any circuits. A manual transfer switch is a better fit when no interlock fits your panel or you want a fixed set of circuits ready to go. An automatic transfer switch is for standby generators that start on their own. All of them need a licensed electrician and usually a permit.</p></div>

> **Safety first:** Never connect a generator to your house wiring without a transfer switch or listed interlock. Run the generator **outdoors only**, never in a garage, basement, shed or porch, and keep it well away from windows, doors and vents. Carbon monoxide can kill in minutes. Have working CO alarms inside, and if one goes off, get everyone out first and read [what to do when a carbon monoxide alarm goes off](/home-safety/carbon-monoxide-alarm-going-off/).

## Never backfeed through a dryer outlet

"Backfeeding" means pushing generator power into a regular outlet so it flows backward into your panel. People do it with a cord that has a male plug on both ends, nicknamed a **suicide cord** for good reason.

- **It can kill line workers.** With your main breaker on, the power flows out through your meter to the transformer on the pole, which steps it up to thousands of volts on the lines. A crew working on "dead" lines can be electrocuted.
- **It can kill you.** The pins on the loose end of the cord are live as soon as the generator runs.
- **It can start a fire.** Outlets and household cords aren't built to feed a whole panel, and nothing protects the wiring from overload the way a proper generator breaker does.
- **It can wreck the generator** when utility power returns.
- **It's against code** and illegal in many areas.

Until you have transfer equipment, run **heavy-duty outdoor extension cords** from the generator to individual appliances.

## How to choose: quick questions

1. **Do you want a permanent standby generator** that starts on its own? You'll want an **automatic transfer switch** (topic 3).
2. **Is an interlock kit listed for your panel's make and model?** Your electrician can check the panel label. If yes, an **interlock** is usually the simplest option (topic 2).
3. **No listed interlock, or your panel is full or outdated?** A **manual transfer switch** (topic 1) installs beside the panel instead.
4. **Check your generator's biggest outlet and wattage** (topics 5 and 6). It decides the size of the inlet, cord and switch.
5. **Call a licensed electrician** for a quote and the permit (topic 8).

## 1. Manual transfer switch

A manual transfer switch is a separate box, mounted next to your main panel, that works like a small subpanel. The electrician moves a **selected set of circuits** (often somewhere around 6 to 10, depending on the model) so they run through it. Each circuit gets its own switch with two positions: **LINE** (utility) and **GEN** (generator).

**Good to know:**

- You choose the circuits at install time. Adding more later means another electrician visit.
- Each circuit switches separately, so you can add loads one at a time.
- It works with almost any panel, which makes it the go-to when no interlock is listed for yours.

## 2. Interlock kit

An interlock kit is a **mechanical slide plate** fastened to the front of your main panel. The electrician adds a **generator breaker** (a two-pole breaker fed from the inlet box) near the main breaker. The plate is shaped so that **the main breaker and the generator breaker can never both be on**. To switch to generator power, you turn the main off, slide the plate, then turn the generator breaker on.

Generator mode: main breaker off, plate slid over to block the main, generator breaker on" width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>The slide plate lets only one of the two breakers be on.</figcaption></figure>

**Good to know:**

- The kit **must be listed for your panel's make and model**. Panels differ in breaker layout and cover screws, and a universal or homemade plate isn't acceptable. Some panels have no listed kit at all.
- Every circuit in the panel can run on generator power, so **you manage the load** by switching off breakers you don't need.
- The generator breaker usually needs a **hold-down or retaining kit** so it can't be pulled loose. Follow the kit's instructions.

## 3. Automatic transfer switch

An automatic transfer switch (ATS) watches utility power. When it drops out, the ATS signals a **standby generator** to start, then moves your house (or a selected set of circuits) to generator power once the generator is up to speed. When the utility returns, it switches back and shuts the generator down.

**Good to know:**

- It's almost always part of a **permanently installed standby generator**, fueled by natural gas or propane, not a portable unit you wheel out.
- Some cover the whole house, some a set of essential circuits, and some shed big loads like AC when the generator nears its limit.

## 4. Inlet box and generator cord

With a manual transfer switch or interlock, the generator doesn't plug into anything inside. It plugs into a **power inlet box** mounted on an outside wall, wired to the transfer switch or generator breaker.

- The inlet has **recessed male pins** and the generator cord has a **female connector** on that end, so no live pins are ever exposed.
- The cord must match the generator outlet and the inlet, at the same amp rating or higher.
- Place the inlet where the generator can run **well away** from windows, doors and vents, not right under a bedroom window.

## 5. Check your generator's voltage and outlet

Look at the outlet panel on the generator and the label near it.

| Generator outlet | What it supplies | What it can run |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Household 5-20 outlets only | 120 V | Individual appliances with extension cords. Not for transfer equipment |
| L5-30 (3-prong twist-lock) | 120 V, 30 A | 120 V circuits only, with transfer equipment designed for it |
| L14-30 (4-prong twist-lock) | 120/240 V, 30 A | 120 V and 240 V circuits. The most common choice for home backup |
| 50 A outlet (4-prong) | 120/240 V, 50 A | Larger 120 V and 240 V loads, on larger generators |

A 4-prong outlet has two hots, a neutral and a ground, so it supplies both 120 V and 240 V, which you need for a **well pump** or other 240 V appliance. Check your generator manual before buying anything: the inlet, cord and switch must all match it.

## 6. Choose your circuits and size the generator

A transfer switch or interlock doesn't add power. The generator still has a limit, and every appliance shares it.

**How:**

1. **List what you really need:** fridge, freezer, furnace blower, sump or well pump, some lights, the router.
2. **Note two numbers for motors:** the **running watts** and the higher **starting watts** for a few seconds when they kick on. Fridges, pumps and AC compressors need the most.
3. **Add up the running watts**, then add the biggest single starting surge. Compare that with the generator's rated (running) and starting watts on its label.
4. **Leave some headroom**, and plan to start the big motor loads one at a time.

This [generator size calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/generator-size/) helps you total the loads. Electric water heaters, ranges, dryers and central AC usually need more than a typical portable can supply.

## 7. Grounding and the neutral: floating or bonded

Portable generators come with either a **floating neutral** (neutral not connected to the frame) or a **bonded neutral** (neutral tied to the frame and ground inside the generator). Your house already bonds neutral to ground at the main panel, so which type you have affects how it should connect.

- With a common transfer switch or interlock, the house and generator share one neutral, and the bond stays in your main panel. Many transfer equipment makers specify a **floating neutral** generator for this reason.
- Some setups use a **switched-neutral** transfer switch, which suits a bonded-neutral generator.
- Some generators can be converted between the two. Only do this the way the generator manufacturer describes.

There's no single answer for every home. **Follow the transfer equipment and generator manuals**, and let your electrician decide how the neutral and grounding are handled, including whether the generator needs its own ground rod.

## 8. Permits, inspection and who installs it

Transfer switches, interlocks and inlet boxes all connect to your service panel, so **most US jurisdictions require an electrical permit and an inspection**. Rules vary by city and county, so check with your local building department.

Hire a **licensed electrician**. They'll confirm the panel can take the equipment, size the wiring and breaker, handle the neutral question and pull the permit.

## Transfer switch vs interlock kit: side by side

| | Manual transfer switch | Interlock kit | Automatic transfer switch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where it goes | Separate box beside the panel | Plate on the main panel cover | Separate box near the meter or panel |
| Circuits powered | A fixed set chosen at install | Any circuit in the panel | Whole house or chosen circuits |
| Load control | Switch each circuit on separately | You turn breakers off and on | Automatic, some with load shedding |
| Panel needs | Works with most panels | Listed kit for your exact panel, plus space for a breaker | Depends on the model |
| Generator type | Portable | Portable | Mostly standby |
| Switching | By hand | By hand | Automatic |

## Using it safely during an outage

- **Set up:** generator outdoors and far from the house, cord connected to the inlet before you start it.
- **Switch over:** main off (or circuits to GEN), start the generator, then turn on the generator side and add loads one at a time.
- **Switch back** when power returns: generator breaker off, back to utility, then stop the generator. Refuel only with the engine off and cool. The [power outage checklist](/home-safety/power-outage-checklist/) covers the rest of your plan.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician for any of these:

- Installing a **transfer switch, interlock kit or inlet box**. This is panel work and isn't a DIY job for most homeowners.
- You aren't sure whether your generator has a **floating or bonded neutral**, or which your transfer equipment needs.
- Your panel is **full, damaged, recalled or very old**, or no interlock is listed for it.
- You've been backfeeding through an outlet. Stop using that cord now and use extension cords until the work is done.
