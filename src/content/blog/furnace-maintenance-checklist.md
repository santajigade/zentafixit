A gas furnace that sat idle all summer deserves an hour of attention before you need it. Most of this checklist is simple: **a fresh filter, working CO and smoke alarms, clear space around the furnace, open vents, clear outdoor pipes, a flowing condensate drain and a test run**. The checks that need gauges and training belong to a **yearly professional tune-up**.

Pick a mild fall day. If anything turns up a problem, you still have time to get it fixed before the first cold night.

> **Safety first:** Turn the furnace **off at its service switch** (it looks like a light switch on or near the furnace) before opening any panel or the filter door, and wait for the blower to stop. **If you smell gas** (a rotten egg smell), don't touch any switches, phones or flames near it. Leave the house right away and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Leave gas valves, burners, wiring and venting repairs to a licensed technician.

## Your pre-winter checklist

1. **Replace the filter** and, if you have a whole-house humidifier, its pad.
2. **Test the CO and smoke alarms** and change their batteries.
3. **Clear the area** around the furnace of storage and anything flammable.
4. **Open the supply vents** and clear the return grilles.
5. **Check the outdoor intake and exhaust pipes** for leaves, nests and snow.
6. **Check the condensate drain** on a high-efficiency furnace.
7. **Look over the flue pipe and the burner flame.**
8. **Test-run the heat** with the thermostat.
9. **Book a professional tune-up** if it's been a year or more.

## 1. Replace the filter and humidifier pad

A clogged filter starves the furnace of airflow. The heat exchanger can overheat, and a safety switch then shuts the burners off, so you get short bursts of heat and cold rooms.

**How:**

1. Set the thermostat to **OFF** and turn off the furnace service switch.
2. Slide out the filter from its slot at the furnace or behind the return grille. Note the size printed on the frame.
3. Slide in a new one with the **airflow arrow pointing toward the furnace**.

For intervals and which MERV rating to buy, see [how often to change your furnace filter](/hvac/how-often-change-furnace-filter/).

**Humidifier pad:** If a whole-house humidifier is mounted on your ductwork, its pad (often called a water panel or evaporator pad) collects mineral scale. Many manufacturers suggest a new pad each heating season. Turn off its water supply, swap the pad, then turn the water back on. If it's a bypass type with a damper, set the damper to the winter position. Check the humidifier's manual for the right pad and settings.

## 2. Test your CO and smoke alarms

A furnace problem can release **carbon monoxide**, a gas you can't see or smell. Working alarms are your backup if something goes wrong between tune-ups.

**How:**

- Press and hold the **test button** on each CO and smoke alarm until it sounds.
- Replace batteries in battery-powered alarms, and the backup battery in hardwired ones.
- Check the **date on the back**. Smoke alarms are generally replaced every 10 years. CO alarms often have a shorter life, so follow the replace-by date the maker prints on it.
- Make sure you have CO alarms on each level and near sleeping areas, following the alarm maker's instructions.

If a CO alarm goes off at any point, treat it as real. See [what to do when a carbon monoxide alarm goes off](/home-safety/carbon-monoxide-alarm-going-off/).

## 3. Clear the area around the furnace

Gas furnaces need air to burn properly and space for service. Boxes, laundry and clutter pushed against the cabinet can block air openings and are a fire risk.

**How:**

- Move storage away from the furnace on all sides. The label inside the furnace door lists the required clearances.
- **Never store gasoline, paint, solvents, aerosol cans or cleaning chemicals** in the furnace room. Fumes can be drawn into the burners and ignite.
- If the furnace is in a closet, don't block the **louvered door** or any vent openings. They supply combustion air.
- Sweep or vacuum dust and pet hair from the floor around the furnace and the return grille.

## 4. Open the supply vents and clear the return grilles

Supply vents blow warm air into rooms. Return grilles (usually larger, with no louvers to adjust) pull air back to the furnace.

**How:**

- Walk through the house and open the supply registers you closed for summer.
- Move rugs, furniture and curtains off vents and returns.
- Vacuum dust from the grilles.

Closing a lot of registers to "save heat" can make the blower work harder and doesn't usually save much. Keep most of them open.

## 5. Check the outdoor intake and exhaust pipes

High-efficiency furnaces usually vent through **two plastic (PVC) pipes** that run out a side wall or the roof. One brings in combustion air and the other carries exhaust out. If either is blocked, a safety switch stops the furnace, and a blocked exhaust is a carbon monoxide hazard.

**How:**

- Find where the pipes end outside. Look for **leaves, grass, bird or wasp nests**, spider webs and debris inside the openings.
- Clear the area around them, and trim back shrubs.
- In winter, keep **snow and ice** off the pipe ends, especially after drifting snow. Check them after every big storm.
- Don't add your own screens or caps. If the pipes are cracked, sagging or have come apart, call a technician.

Older furnaces usually vent through a metal pipe into a chimney instead (see step 7).

## 6. Check the condensate drain (high-efficiency units)

High-efficiency furnaces make water as they cool the exhaust. It runs through a **plastic trap** and a small hose or pipe to a floor drain or a condensate pump. A clog can shut the furnace down or cause a leak.

**How:**

1. With the furnace off, follow the drain hose from the furnace to where it empties.
2. Check that the hose isn't kinked, cracked or disconnected and that the floor drain is clear.
3. If you have a **condensate pump**, pour a little water into its reservoir and check that it switches on and pumps out.
4. Look for water stains, rust or puddles under the furnace.

Some traps can be removed and rinsed, but the design varies by brand. Check your furnace manual or leave it to the tune-up. Gurgling or water around the furnace after it runs is worth fixing now.

## 7. Look over the flue pipe and burner flame

**Older furnaces:** a metal flue pipe runs from the top of the furnace to the chimney. Look at it with a flashlight, without touching it, for **rust, holes, gaps at the joints, sagging or loose sections**. Gaps can leak exhaust into the house. Don't tape, push or try to fix it. Call a technician.

**Flame check:** many furnaces have a small **sight window** in the front panel so you can see the burners. With the furnace running a heat cycle, look through it:

- **Steady, mostly blue flames** are normal. A few brief orange flecks can be dust burning off.
- **Yellow, orange, floppy (lazy) or wavy flames** can mean poor combustion.
- **Flames that lift off the burner**, roar or flicker wildly are also a problem.

If you see anything other than steady blue, turn the furnace off at the thermostat and call a technician. Poor combustion can produce carbon monoxide. Don't remove panels to look, since most furnaces have a door switch that shuts them off, and the burner area should only be opened by a pro.

## 8. Test-run the heat before the first cold night

**How:**

1. Turn the furnace service switch back **ON** and close any panels you opened.
2. Replace the thermostat batteries if it uses them.
3. Set the thermostat to **HEAT** and a few degrees above room temperature.
4. Within a minute or two, you should hear the small draft fan start, then the burners light. The main blower usually follows after a short delay.
5. Within a few minutes, warm air should come from the vents.

A dusty smell on the first run is common and should fade. If the furnace won't start, starts and shuts off, or blows cold air, turn it off and see [why a furnace won't turn on](/hvac/furnace-not-turning-on/). Many furnaces also have a status light that blinks a code, and the code chart is usually on the inside of the panel door.

## 9. Book a professional tune-up

Some checks need instruments and training. A yearly tune-up, ideally in early fall, usually includes:

- **Heat exchanger inspection** for cracks or corrosion, which can leak exhaust into the air you breathe.
- **Gas pressure** checked and adjusted to the manufacturer's setting.
- **Combustion check**, often with an analyzer, plus a look at the venting.
- **Burner and flame sensor cleaning.** A dirty flame sensor is a common reason a furnace lights and then shuts off.
- **Ignitor, electrical connections and controls** tested.
- **Blower, safety switches and the condensate system** checked.

Ask what's included before you book, since tune-up packages vary. If your furnace is under warranty, check whether regular maintenance is a condition of coverage.

## Things to leave to a pro

- Anything involving the gas valve, gas lines or burners.
- Repairing or reconnecting flue or PVC vent pipes.
- Working on the ignitor, control board or wiring.
- Diagnosing a yellow or lifting flame, or a CO alarm.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a licensed HVAC technician if:

- You smell gas (call the gas utility from outside first).
- The burner flame is yellow, lazy or lifting.
- The flue pipe is rusted, gapped or loose, or a PVC vent pipe is damaged.
- The furnace short cycles, won't light, or keeps shutting off.
- The condensate drain leaks or keeps clogging.
- It's been more than a year since the last professional tune-up.

Tell them what you've already done (filter, alarms, pipes, drain). It saves time and lets them focus on the checks you can't do yourself.
