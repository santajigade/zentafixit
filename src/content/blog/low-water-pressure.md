Low water pressure turns a shower into a drizzle and makes filling a pot take forever. The first thing to figure out is **whether it's one fixture or the whole house**, because that points to completely different causes.

One weak faucet usually means **a clogged aerator or a partly closed valve** under that sink. A whole house problem is more often **the main shutoff, the pressure regulator, a leak or the water supply** itself.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Try several fixtures.** Run the kitchen sink, a bathroom sink, a shower and an outdoor faucet. Only one weak? Start with cause 1. All weak? Keep going.
2. **Compare hot and cold.** If only the hot side is weak everywhere, jump to "Only the hot water is weak?" below.
3. **Ask a neighbor** or check your utility's website for repair work, main breaks or service notices.
4. **Measure it.** Use a gauge (next section) so you have a real number, not just a feeling.

## Test your pressure with a gauge

A **hose-thread water pressure gauge** costs little at any hardware store and takes the guesswork out.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/low-water-pressure-path.svg" alt="Diagram of the water path into a house: the street supply goes through the water meter, the main shutoff valve and the pressure regulator before branching to fixtures; a pressure gauge is screwed onto an outdoor hose faucet; a scale shows below 40 psi as low, about 40 to 60 psi as typical and above 80 psi as too high" width="640" height="440" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Where pressure can be lost, and where to test it. Ranges are general guidance.</figcaption></figure>

1. Turn off all water in the house: faucets, showers, the washer, the dishwasher, irrigation and the ice maker.
2. Screw the gauge onto an **outdoor hose faucet** or the **washing machine cold spigot**. Use one as close to the main shutoff as you can.
3. Open that faucet fully and read the gauge. This is your **static pressure**. The gauge reads gauge pressure (psi above the surrounding air), which is what plumbing ranges use. For the difference, see this explainer on [absolute vs gauge pressure](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/absolute-vs-gauge-pressure/).
4. Have someone open a tub faucet fully and watch the needle. A big drop when water flows can point to a restriction such as a partly closed valve, a clogged filter or old pipes.

As general guidance, **many homes run around 40 to 60 psi**, and **about 80 psi is a common upper limit**. Well systems usually cycle between two set points, so their pressure rises and falls with use.

## 1. A clogged aerator or showerhead

The aerator is the small screen at the tip of a faucet. Mineral scale, sand and bits of old pipe collect there, especially after water work in the street. Showerheads clog the same way.

**Fix:**

1. Unscrew the aerator by hand, or with pliers wrapped in a rag to avoid scratching it. Some recessed aerators need a small key that comes with the faucet.
2. Rinse out debris and scrub the screen with an old toothbrush.
3. Soak it in **white vinegar** for an hour or so to dissolve scale, then rinse and refit it.
4. For a showerhead, unscrew it and soak it the same way, or tie a bag of vinegar over it.
5. Replace the aerator if it's damaged. Take the old one to the store to match the size and thread.

## 2. A shutoff valve not fully open

A valve that's only partly open is a common cause, often after a repair, a home purchase or a plumber's visit.

**Fix:**

- **One fixture weak:** check the small shutoff valves under the sink or behind the toilet. Turn them counterclockwise until they stop.
- **Whole house weak:** find the **main shutoff valve** where the water line enters the house (often in the basement, crawlspace, garage or a utility closet). A gate valve with a round wheel should be turned fully counterclockwise. A **ball valve** with a lever is fully open when the lever is parallel to the pipe.
- Don't force an old valve that's stiff. If it won't open fully or starts to leak, call a plumber.

## 3. A failing pressure regulator (PRV)

Many homes have a **pressure reducing valve (PRV)**, a bell-shaped brass valve on the main line just after the shutoff. It lowers high street pressure to a safe level. When it wears out or gets clogged, pressure can drop low, or swing up and down.

**Fix:**

1. Find the PRV near the main shutoff. Look for an **adjusting screw with a locknut** on top.
2. With a gauge on a nearby faucet, loosen the locknut and turn the screw a little at a time. On many models clockwise raises pressure, but check the label or the maker's instructions.
3. Recheck the gauge after each small turn, then tighten the locknut.
4. If the pressure doesn't respond, keeps drifting, or the valve is leaking, the PRV likely needs replacing. That involves cutting into the main line, so most people use a plumber.

## 4. A hidden leak

A leak in the supply line, under a slab or in the yard sends water somewhere other than your faucets. Pressure drops, and the water bill usually rises.

**Fix:**

1. Turn off every fixture and water-using appliance.
2. Find your **water meter** (often in a box near the curb, or in the basement) and watch the small **leak indicator** dial or the last digit for 15 to 30 minutes.
3. If it moves with everything off, water is going somewhere. Check toilets, outdoor faucets, irrigation lines and the water heater.
4. Look for wet spots, sounds of running water, or green patches in the yard along the supply line.

You can estimate how much a leak costs with this [water leak calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/ecology/water-leak/). A running toilet is one of the most common hidden leaks, so check the toilets first.

## 5. A problem on the city side

If pressure dropped suddenly for the whole house, the cause may be outside your home: a main break, hydrant flushing, repair work, or peak demand on hot summer mornings and evenings.

**Fix:**

- Ask neighbors if their pressure is low too.
- Check your water utility's website or social media for notices, or call them.
- If only your home is affected, ask the utility to check the meter and the service line to your house. The curb valve or meter is their equipment in most areas.

## 6. Well pump pressure switch or tank

On a private well, the **pressure switch** turns the pump on when pressure falls to a lower set point and off at a higher one. Settings like 30/50 or 40/60 psi are common, but check the label inside the switch cover. A worn switch, clogged switch tube or a waterlogged **pressure tank** can leave you with low or pulsing pressure.

**Fix:**

- Watch the gauge near the pressure tank as water runs. It should fall to the lower setting, then the pump should start and pressure should climb back.
- If the pump cycles on and off rapidly, the tank may have lost its air charge.
- A clogged sediment filter after the tank is a common, easy fix. Change the cartridge.

> **Safety first:** A well pressure switch carries live electrical current, often 240 V. Don't remove the cover or adjust the switch unless you know how and the power to the pump is off at the breaker. Otherwise, call a well contractor.

## 7. A clogged filter or water softener

A whole-house filter cartridge or a softener with a clogged resin bed restricts flow to every fixture after it.

**Fix:**

- Change the whole-house filter cartridge on the schedule the maker recommends, sooner if pressure has dropped.
- Put the softener or filter in **bypass** for a quick test. If pressure jumps back, the system is the cause. See our guide to a [water softener not working](/plumbing/water-softener-not-working/).

## 8. Old or corroded pipes

Older homes with **galvanized steel pipes** can lose pressure over decades as rust and scale build up inside and narrow the pipe. Pressure is usually weak everywhere and gets worse slowly, and you may see brown water after the water has been off.

**Fix:**

- A plumber can confirm it by comparing pressure and flow at different points.
- The long-term fix is repiping with copper or PEX. It's a big job, so get more than one quote.

## Only the hot water is weak?

If cold water is fine and hot is weak everywhere, the problem is at the water heater:

- Check that the **shutoff valve on the cold inlet** to the water heater is fully open.
- **Sediment** in a tank heater, or scale in a tankless unit, can restrict flow. Flushing the tank or descaling the tankless unit per the manual often helps.
- If hot water is weak at one fixture only, it's that fixture's cartridge or valve.

## When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if:

- Your gauge reads well below the typical range and the main shutoff is fully open.
- Pressure is above about 80 psi, or swings up and down, and the PRV won't adjust.
- The water meter shows a leak you can't find.
- The main shutoff or PRV is stiff, leaking or very old.
- You have galvanized pipes and pressure keeps getting worse.

For wells, call a well contractor for pressure switch, tank or pump problems.
