Buffering means your TV or streaming device can't get video data as fast as it plays it. It stops, loads, plays a bit and stops again, or the picture drops to a blurry low quality.

The usual causes are **weak Wi-Fi at the TV**, **too little speed for the quality you're streaming**, **other devices using the connection**, and **an app that needs a cache clear or update**. Sometimes it's **your provider or the streaming service** itself. A couple of quick checks tell you which.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Try a second streaming app or service.** If everything buffers, the problem is your network or provider (fixes 1 to 4, 7, 8). If only one app does, start with fixes 5, 6 and 8.
2. **Run a speed test on your phone, standing next to the TV**, connected to the same Wi-Fi network. Mobile data off. Many streaming apps also have a network test in their help or settings menu.
3. **Compare with a test next to the router.** A big drop between the two means weak Wi-Fi at the TV (fix 2).
4. **Note the time.** Buffering mostly in the evening points to a busy network (fixes 4 and 8).

## 1. Check your speed against what the stream needs

Every stream needs a steady speed, and higher quality needs more. These are **rough figures per stream**. Each service publishes its own recommendations, so check its help page for exact numbers.

| Quality | Rough speed per stream |
| --- | --- |
| Standard definition | A few Mbps |
| HD (720p or 1080p) | About 5 to 10 Mbps |
| 4K (Ultra HD) | About 15 to 25 Mbps |

**Fix:**

- Add up everything happening at once. Two 4K streams plus a game download need far more than one HD stream.
- Leave headroom. Wi-Fi rarely delivers your full plan speed, and the speed needs to stay steady, not just peak in a test.
- If your speed test at the TV is below what you need, work through fixes 2 to 4 before upgrading your plan.
- If your test next to the router is far below what you pay for, restart the modem and router (fix 7) and then talk to your provider.

## 2. Improve the Wi-Fi signal to the TV

TVs are often at the far end of the house, in a cabinet, or against a wall full of other electronics. Streaming sticks plug in behind the TV, where the TV's own metal back can block the signal.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/streaming-keeps-buffering-chain.svg" alt="The path a stream takes from the streaming service through your internet provider, modem and router, then Wi-Fi or cable, to the TV or streaming stick, with what can slow down each step" width="640" height="360" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A slow link anywhere in the chain causes buffering.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

- **Move the router** up off the floor, out of cabinets, and as close to the middle of the home as you can.
- **Use the 5 GHz band** for the TV if it's within a room or two of the router. It's faster but has less range than 2.4 GHz. Some routers combine both under one name and choose for you.
- **Move a streaming stick out from behind the TV** with a short HDMI extender cable. Many sticks include one in the box.
- **Power the stick from its own adapter**, not the TV's USB port. Some TV USB ports don't supply enough power, which can cause odd behavior.
- **Add mesh Wi-Fi or an access point** if the TV is far away. Placement matters, so see our guide on [where to place mesh Wi-Fi units](/networking/mesh-wifi-placement/).

## 3. Use a wired connection

A cable gives the TV a steady connection that walls, distance and neighbors' networks can't affect. It's the single most reliable fix for buffering.

**Fix:**

- Run an **Ethernet cable** from the router to the TV, game console or streaming box. Then select wired or Ethernet in the device's network settings.
- Many TVs have **100 Mbps** Ethernet ports. That's normal and still plenty for 4K streaming.
- Some streaming sticks have no Ethernet port but accept a USB Ethernet adapter. Check the maker's list of compatible adapters.
- Can't run a cable? **Powerline adapters** carry the connection over your electrical wiring. Plug them straight into wall outlets, not surge strips. If the wired link misbehaves, see [Ethernet not working](/networking/ethernet-not-working/).

## 4. Pause other devices using bandwidth

Your connection is shared. Large downloads, game updates, cloud photo backups, video calls and security cameras uploading video can all crowd out the stream.

**Fix:**

- Pause big downloads and game updates while you watch. Consoles and PCs often download updates in the background.
- Check whether cloud backups (photos, computer backups) are running, and schedule them for overnight.
- Look at your router's app. Many show which devices are using the most data right now.
- If your router has **QoS** or a **media/streaming priority** setting, turn it on and prioritize the TV. Results vary by router.
- Remove devices you don't recognize from the network and change the Wi-Fi password if needed.

## 5. Clear the app's cache or reinstall it

When only one app buffers or freezes, its stored data or an old version may be the problem.

**Fix:**

- **Update the app** and the TV or streaming device's software. Look in the settings for a system or software update.
- **Clear the cache:** on Android TV, Google TV and Fire TV devices, open the settings, find the app list, select the app and choose **Clear cache**. Menu names vary by model.
- **Other platforms** may not offer a cache option. Restarting the device (fix 7) clears temporary data, or uninstall and reinstall the app.
- **Sign out and back in** if the app has playback errors as well as buffering.

## 6. Lower the streaming quality

If your connection can't keep up with 4K, most services drop the quality automatically. If buffering continues, set a lower quality yourself so playback stays smooth.

**Fix:**

- Check the **playback** or **video quality** settings in the app or in your account on the service's website. Some services set this per profile.
- Choose a lower setting, such as HD instead of 4K, or a data saver option.
- Turn it back up once you've fixed the Wi-Fi or connected a cable.

## 7. Restart the TV, streaming device and router

TVs and streaming sticks run for weeks without a real restart. Many TVs only go into standby when you press the power button. A full restart clears memory and network glitches.

**Fix:**

1. **Unplug the TV and any streaming device** from the power for about a minute, then plug them back in.
2. **Restart the modem and router:** unplug both, wait about 30 seconds, plug in the modem first and wait for its online light, then plug in the router.
3. Start the stream again and test.

## 8. Check whether the problem is your provider or the service

Sometimes nothing in your home is wrong.

**Fix:**

- **Check the streaming service's status** on its help site or social media, especially during big live events or new releases.
- **Check your provider's app** for outages or slowdowns in your area.
- **Turn off any VPN** on the router, TV or phone you cast from. VPNs add distance and can be slower, and some services restrict them.
- **Test at different times.** Fast in the morning but slow every evening points to congestion on your provider's network.

## Buffering on every service, every night?

- **Test speed with a cable** plugged straight into the router at the busy time. If it's far below your plan, the bottleneck is your provider, not your Wi-Fi.
- **Check your plan.** A plan that was fine for one TV may not cover several 4K streams plus remote work. Compare your real use with fix 1.
- **Check your equipment's age.** An old router or modem may not handle your plan's full speed. Your provider can check a rented modem for you.

## When to call your internet provider

Call your provider if:

- Wired speed tests at the router are consistently well below your plan.
- Speeds collapse at the same time every evening.
- The modem drops its online light or restarts during buffering.
- You rent the modem or gateway and it's several years old.

If your provider's tests look fine and only the TV struggles, a local network installer can run a cable to the TV or set up mesh Wi-Fi properly.
