**If your carbon monoxide (CO) alarm is sounding its full, loud pattern, stop reading and get everyone outside now, including pets. Call 911 or the fire department from outside.** Don't go back in until responders tell you it's safe. Come back to the rest of this guide once you're out.

Carbon monoxide has **no smell, no color and no taste**, so the alarm is your only warning. The most common reasons a CO alarm sounds or beeps are **a real CO buildup from a fuel-burning appliance, car or generator**, a **low battery chirp**, or an **alarm that has reached end of life**. Only the last two are safe to deal with yourself, and only after you're sure it's a chirp, not the alarm.

> **Safety first:** Never press silence and stay inside, and never just open the windows and wait it out. Never run a portable generator, grill, camp stove or car inside your home or garage, even with the door open. If you also smell gas (a rotten egg smell), don't touch light switches or appliances, leave and call 911 or your gas utility from outside.

## What to do right now

If the alarm is sounding its full pattern, do these in order. Don't stop to find the source.

1. **Get everyone out to fresh air.** Bring children, older relatives and pets. Leave the door closed behind you if you can.
2. **Call 911 or the fire department from outside**, a neighbor's house or your car parked well away from the garage.
3. **Count heads.** Make sure nobody is still inside. Don't go back in for anyone or anything. Tell the dispatcher if someone is missing.
4. **Watch for symptoms.** Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, weakness or flu-like feelings all need medical help. Tell the dispatcher.
5. **Stay out until responders say it's safe** to go back in, even if the alarm stops.

## 1. Get everyone out to fresh air

The goal is to stop breathing the gas as fast as possible. Fresh outdoor air is the fix, not a window cracked open in one room.

- Go straight out. Don't search for the source or shut off appliances on the way unless a responder asks you to and it's safe.
- Wake anyone who is asleep. CO can make people drowsy and confused, so they may not react to the alarm.

## 2. Call 911 from outside

Call 911 or your fire department once you're outside, even if everyone feels fine. Firefighters can measure CO with their own meters, find likely sources and air out the home safely.

- Tell them a **carbon monoxide alarm** is sounding and whether anyone has symptoms.
- Don't go back in when the alarm stops. It may have stopped because the reading dropped while the doors were open, not because the source is gone.

## 3. Get medical help for anyone with symptoms

Early symptoms of CO poisoning can feel like the flu or a bad cold: **headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, weakness, confusion or shortness of breath**. A clue is that several people (or pets) feel ill at once, and feel better away from home.

**Fix:**

- Tell the 911 dispatcher right away if anyone has symptoms. Responders can check them on the spot.
- Anyone who is very drowsy, confused or hard to wake needs emergency care now.
- Babies, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with heart or lung conditions may be at higher risk. Get them checked even with mild symptoms.

## 4. Tell a CO alarm from a chirp

Once you're safe, it helps to know what you heard. Two very different sounds come from a CO alarm:

- **Alarm:** loud, repeating and urgent. **Many CO alarms use a pattern of 4 quick beeps, then a pause, repeating.** Some combination smoke and CO units also speak a warning or flash a light.
- **Chirp:** a single short chirp, usually about once a minute. This is a battery, end-of-life or fault warning, not a CO detection.

Patterns vary by brand, so **check the label on the back or the manual**. If you can't tell which one it is, treat it as an alarm and get out. CO alarms are designed to sound based on both the CO level and how long it has been present, so a home can have a dangerous buildup even when nobody notices anything wrong.

## 5. Fix a low battery chirp

If you're sure it's a **single chirp about once a minute**, not the alarm pattern, the battery is the usual cause.

**Fix:**

1. Find the chirping unit. Stand under each alarm and wait, because chirps echo.
2. Remove the alarm from its base or open the battery door.
3. Fit a **new battery of the exact type on the label**.
4. Refit the alarm and press the test button. Follow the manual for how long to hold it.
5. If it still chirps, see [why a smoke detector keeps chirping](/home-safety/smoke-detector-chirping/). The same reset steps often apply to CO and combination alarms.

Some CO alarms have a **sealed battery** that can't be replaced. When those chirp for low battery, the whole alarm needs replacing.

## 6. Replace an alarm at end of life

CO sensors wear out. **Many CO alarms are designed to last about 7 to 10 years**, and many give an end-of-life chirp or show a message when that time comes. A new battery won't stop it.

**Fix:**

- **Check the label** on the back for the manufacture date or a replace-by date.
- If it's at or past the maker's life, **replace the whole alarm**, ideally the same day.
- For a hardwired or interconnected alarm, turn off the breaker first and use a compatible replacement. Call an electrician if the connector doesn't match.

## 7. Know the common CO sources

Carbon monoxide comes from burning fuel: natural gas, propane, oil, wood, charcoal, gasoline or kerosene. Common sources in a home:

| Source | What can go wrong |
| --- | --- |
| **Furnace or boiler** | Cracked heat exchanger, poor venting, dirty burners |
| **Gas water heater** | Blocked or disconnected vent pipe, backdrafting |
| **Gas range or oven** | Burners not burning cleanly, oven used to heat the home |
| **Fireplace or wood stove** | Closed damper, blocked chimney, poor draft |
| **Blocked flue or chimney** | Nests, debris, snow or ice blocking the exhaust |
| **Car in an attached garage** | Engine left running, even with the garage door open |
| **Portable generator** | Run indoors, in a garage or too close to doors and windows |
| **Grills and camp stoves** | Used indoors, in a garage or a tent |

**Never use a portable generator, charcoal or gas grill, or camp stove indoors or in a garage**, even with the door open. Run a generator outside, well away from doors, windows and vents, and follow the manual. If your generator is giving you trouble, see [why a portable generator won't start](/electrical/portable-generator-wont-start/), but never move it inside to work on it while it's running.

## 8. Get appliances checked before using them again

Responders can clear the air and tell you when it's safe to go back in, but that doesn't fix the source. A CO alarm that went off for a real reason will often go off again.

**Fix:**

1. **Follow the responders' advice.** They may shut off an appliance or the gas supply and tell you not to use it.
2. **Have a qualified technician inspect every fuel-burning appliance** (furnace, water heater, range, fireplace, dryer if it's gas) and the vents and chimney before you use them again.
3. **Don't restart** an appliance that responders or the technician shut off, and don't relight pilots yourself unless the technician says it's fine.
4. **Test your CO alarms** once you're back in, and replace any that are past their date.

## 9. Put CO alarms in the right places

Placement matters so the alarm wakes you in time.

- Put a CO alarm **on every level** of the home, including the basement.
- Put one **outside each sleeping area**, close enough to hear it with bedroom doors closed.
- **Follow the manufacturer's instructions** for height and distance from fuel-burning appliances, kitchens, bathrooms and vents. They differ by model.
- **Test monthly** with the test button and replace batteries as the label says.

## Alarm went off but responders found nothing?

It happens, and it's still right that you got out and called. CO levels can drop quickly once doors are opened, and some sources only produce CO under certain conditions, such as on a cold morning when the furnace runs hard.

- **Check the alarm's age.** An alarm past its date can misbehave. Replace it.
- **Have the fuel-burning appliances inspected anyway**, especially if the alarm sounded more than once.
- **Think about what was running** at the time: a car warming up in the garage, the oven, a fireplace, or a generator outside near a window.
- **Never unplug or remove the alarm** because it "went off for nothing." If it sounds again, get out and call 911 again.

## When to call a pro

Call **911 or the fire department** any time a CO alarm sounds its full alarm pattern, or anyone has symptoms. Then call a qualified technician if:

- Responders found CO or a likely source, or shut off an appliance.
- Your furnace, water heater or fireplace hasn't had a professional check in a while. A yearly inspection before heating season is a good habit.
- You see soot, rust or water streaks around an appliance vent, or a pilot flame that looks yellow and lazy instead of crisp and blue.
- A hardwired or interconnected CO alarm needs replacing and you're not comfortable working at the breaker. An electrician can do it.

Many local fire departments can advise on where CO alarms should go. Call their non-emergency number to ask.
