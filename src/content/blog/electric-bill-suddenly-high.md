A suddenly high electric bill is usually explained by one of three things: **you used more kWh**, **you paid more per kWh**, or **the bill covered a different period or an estimated reading**. Separating those three takes ten minutes with your last few bills.

If you really did use more power, the usual suspects are **heating or cooling in extreme weather**, a **space heater**, the **water heater**, or a **pump or old fridge that runs far more than it should**. Here's how to find which.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Find the kWh used** on this bill and on the same month last year. Most bills show a 12-month usage chart.
2. **Check the billing days.** A 35-day bill will be higher than a 28-day bill for the same home.
3. **Look for the word "estimated"** (or an "E") next to the meter reading.
4. **Compare the price per kWh** and any new fees or riders on the bill.
5. **Do the meter test** in the section on reading your meter below to catch anything running when it shouldn't.

## 1. Heating or cooling working harder

Heating and cooling are usually the biggest electric loads in a US home, so a heat wave or cold snap shows up on the bill right away. Heat pumps are a special case: when it gets very cold, or the thermostat is set to **emergency heat**, they switch on electric backup heat strips that use far more power than the heat pump itself.

**Fix:**

- Compare kWh with the same month last year and with the local weather that month.
- Check the thermostat isn't stuck on **EM HEAT** or **AUX** and that the schedule is still set.
- Replace a dirty air filter and keep the outdoor unit clear.
- If the system runs nonstop and still can't keep up, call an HVAC technician.

## 2. Your rate or plan changed

If your kWh barely moved but the dollar total jumped, the price changed. Utilities adjust rates, fuel charges and riders, and some plans switch to a higher rate after a promotional period or move you onto tiered or time-of-use pricing.

**Fix:**

- Compare the rate per kWh and the list of charges line by line with an older bill.
- Look for tiered pricing: on some plans, kWh above a set amount cost more.
- Ask your utility (or retail supplier, in deregulated states) whether a cheaper plan fits your usage.

## 3. An estimated meter reading

When a meter reader can't get access, or a smart meter fails to report, the utility may **estimate** your use. A low estimate followed by an actual reading puts two months of "catch up" on one bill.

**Fix:**

- Look for "estimated" or "E" next to the reading.
- Read the meter yourself (see below) and compare.
- Call the utility with your own reading and the date you took it.

## 4. A new appliance or device

A new hot tub, second fridge, electric dryer, EV, aquarium, crypto miner or gaming PC can add a noticeable chunk of kWh each month. So can a guest, a new baby (more laundry), or someone now working from home.

**Fix:**

- Think back to what changed around the date the bill went up.
- Plug suspect 120 V devices into a **plug-in watt meter** for a day to see their real kWh.
- Estimate the cost of anything with this [electricity cost calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/everyday-life/electricity-cost/): watts, hours per day and your rate.

## 5. Space heaters

Most portable space heaters draw up to about **1,500 W** on high. Run one for 8 hours a day and that's about 12 kWh a day, every day. Two of them in a cold month can rival what a whole small home normally uses.

**Fix:**

- Use the lowest setting that keeps you comfortable, and only in the room you're in.
- Turn them off when the room is empty, and never leave them running unattended or overnight near bedding or curtains.
- Fix drafts or a weak furnace rather than relying on space heaters long term.

## 6. The water heater

An electric water heater is often one of the largest loads in the house. Anything that makes it reheat more raises the bill: a **hot water leak**, a dripping hot tap, a higher thermostat setting, more showers, or a tank full of sediment.

**Fix:**

- Check for drips at hot taps, under the tank and at the relief valve pipe.
- Many households are comfortable at about **120°F**. Check the setting on the tank (power off at the breaker before removing any cover).
- Flush sediment per the manual, and insulate exposed hot water pipes.

## 7. A pool or hot tub pump

A single-speed pool pump running many hours a day is a big load, and a hot tub heater adds more in cold weather. A timer that reset after a power outage can leave a pump running around the clock.

**Fix:**

- Check the pump timer's clock and on/off times after any outage.
- Run the pump only as many hours as your pool actually needs. Ask your pool professional.
- Consider a variable-speed pump, which uses much less energy at lower speeds.
- Keep the hot tub cover closed and in good shape.

## 8. A failing fridge or freezer

A fridge or freezer with **dirty condenser coils**, a **torn door gasket** or a weak fan can run almost constantly. An old second fridge or chest freezer in a hot garage is a classic hidden cost.

**Fix:**

- Vacuum the condenser coils (unplug first).
- Close the door on a dollar bill. If it slides out easily, the gasket isn't sealing.
- Listen: if it never cycles off or the inside isn't staying cold, it may need service.
- Unplug a nearly empty second fridge if you don't need it.

## 9. A well pump that keeps running

A well pump should run only when you use water. If it **clicks on with no taps open**, or turns on and off every few seconds while you run water, it's using far more power than normal. Common causes are a leak, a running toilet, a waterlogged pressure tank or a failing pressure switch.

**Fix:**

- Turn off all water and listen. If the pump still cycles, you likely have a leak somewhere.
- Check toilets for silent running. See [toilet keeps running](/plumbing/toilet-keeps-running/).
- Pressure tank and switch problems need a well or pump professional.

## 10. A misread meter or a longer billing period

Meters are rarely wrong, but readings and bills can be. A misread digit, a bill for more days than usual, or a meter swap can all cause a one-off jump.

**Fix:**

- Compare the "current reading" on the bill with your own reading of the meter.
- Check the start and end dates of the billing period.
- If your reading is well below the billed one, contact the utility with a photo of the meter and the date.

## How to read your electric meter

**Digital meter:** the display shows total kWh, sometimes cycling through other screens. Wait for the screen labeled kWh (check your utility's guide if you're unsure which screen is which).

**Dial meter:** read the dials **left to right**. Neighboring dials turn in opposite directions. If a pointer sits **between two numbers, write the lower one**; if it's between 9 and 0, write 9.

To find use for a period, subtract an earlier reading from a later one. Then try the **everything-off test**:

1. Note the reading, or watch the digital meter's usage indicator.
2. Switch off everything you can, or turn off the **main breaker** (skip this if anyone relies on powered medical equipment).
3. Watch for 5 to 10 minutes. With the main off, the meter should not count up at all.
4. With only normal standby loads on, it should move very slowly. Fast movement means something is running. Turn off breakers one at a time to find which circuit.

Small always-on devices add up too. See [phantom power and standby loads](/solar-power/phantom-power-standby/).

## Bill still doesn't make sense?

- **Ask for hourly data.** Many utilities with smart meters show hourly or daily use online, which pinpoints when the extra use happens.
- **Request a meter test.** Many utilities will test a meter on request. Ask about any fee first.
- **Check for shared wiring.** In older multi-unit buildings, a neighbor's outlet or a common-area light can end up on your meter. The everything-off test will show it.
- **Escalate.** If the utility won't resolve a billing dispute, your state public utility commission handles consumer complaints.

## When to call a pro

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The meter keeps counting with your **main breaker off**.
- A circuit you can't identify uses power constantly, or you find a warm breaker, outlet or cord.
- You suspect wiring is shared with another unit.

Call an HVAC technician if the heat pump runs on backup heat all the time, and a well or pump professional if the well pump cycles with no water in use.
