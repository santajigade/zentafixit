An air conditioner makes water as it cools. Humid air passes over the cold indoor coil, moisture condenses on it, drips into a drain pan and flows away through a condensate drain line. When you see water inside the house, that water has found a different way out.

Most indoor leaks come from **a clogged condensate drain line**, **a frozen evaporator coil** that floods the pan as it melts, a **cracked or rusted drain pan**, or a **failed condensate pump**. Less often, the line has come apart, the unit isn't level, or ducts are sweating in a hot attic.

> **Safety first:** Water and electricity sit close together in an air handler or furnace cabinet. Turn the system off at the thermostat and switch off power at the breaker or the service switch next to the unit (it often looks like a light switch) before opening any panel or reaching into the pan. Mop up standing water before you work. Don't touch wiring, and call a technician if water has reached the electrical parts. If your system shares a cabinet with a gas furnace, don't touch the gas controls.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Turn the AC off** at the thermostat and set the fan to **AUTO**, not ON.
2. **Pull the air filter** and look at it. A filter caked in dust is a strong clue that the coil froze.
3. **Look for ice** on the copper refrigerant line near the indoor unit or on the coil itself (shine a flashlight through any viewing panel).
4. **Find the drain pan** under the coil and check it with a flashlight.
    - **Pan full of water?** The drain line is blocked (cause 1).
    - **Pan dry or nearly dry, but water below it?** The pan is cracked or a fitting has come loose (causes 4 and 6).
5. **Check any small pump box** next to the unit. If it is full and the pump doesn't run when you lift its float, the pump has failed (cause 5).

## 1. A clogged condensate drain line

This is the most common cause. Over time, dust, algae and slime grow inside the drain line, especially near the trap (the U-shaped bend near the unit). Water backs up into the pan and spills over the edge.

**Fix:**

1. Turn the AC off at the thermostat and the breaker.
2. Find where the line ends. It is usually a PVC or plastic pipe that exits near the outdoor unit, at a foundation wall or into a floor drain or utility sink.
3. Hold a **wet/dry shop vacuum** hose tightly over the pipe end (wrap a rag around the joint to seal it) and run it for a minute or two. You'll often pull out a slug of slime and water.
4. Find the **access port** on the line near the indoor unit. It is often a capped T-shaped fitting or a small vent pipe.
5. Pour in about **a cup of distilled white vinegar**, wait about 30 minutes, then flush with water and confirm it drains out the far end. Check your manual first, as some makers recommend plain water only. Never mix vinegar with bleach or other cleaners.
6. Repeat the vinegar flush every few months during cooling season to help keep algae from coming back.

## 2. The secondary drain is carrying water

Many attic and closet units have a **secondary (backup) drain**: either a second outlet on the coil pan or a shallow overflow pan under the whole unit. Its line often ends over a window, a soffit or another spot where you'll notice it. Water dripping from that pipe is a warning that the main drain is blocked.

**Fix:**

- Treat it as a clogged primary drain (cause 1) and clear the main line.
- If the overflow pan under the unit holds standing water, empty it with a shop vacuum and check that its own drain or **float switch** works.
- A float switch is a small sensor in the pan or drain line that shuts the AC off when water rises. If your AC stopped cooling and the pan is full, this switch may have done its job. It should reset once the water is gone.

## 3. A frozen evaporator coil

When too little warm air passes over the coil, it drops below freezing and ices over. When the system shuts off, the ice melts faster than the pan and drain can handle, and water spills onto the floor. The usual causes are a **dirty air filter**, **closed or blocked vents**, a weak blower and **low refrigerant** from a leak.

**Fix:**

- Turn cooling off and set the fan to **ON** to help the ice melt. This can take a few hours. Put towels down to catch the melt water.
- Replace the air filter, open all supply vents and clear anything blocking return grilles.
- Restart the AC once the coil is fully thawed. If it freezes again with a clean filter and open vents, the refrigerant may be low. That needs an HVAC technician. Our guide to an [AC unit freezing up](/hvac/ac-unit-freezing-up/) covers this in more detail.

## 4. A cracked or rusted drain pan

Older metal pans rust through, and plastic pans can crack with age. Water then leaks out of the bottom of the pan even though the drain line is clear. Look for rust, holes or a dry pan with water directly underneath it.

**Fix:**

- For a small crack in a removable pan, some homeowners use a waterproof sealant as a short-term patch. Treat it as temporary.
- A pan that is badly rusted or built into the coil usually means replacing the pan or the coil. That is a job for an HVAC technician.

## 5. A failed condensate pump

Units in basements, closets or anywhere the water can't flow downhill use a small **condensate pump**: a box with a reservoir, a float and a motor that pushes water out through a thin tube. If the pump fails, its tank overflows.

**Fix:**

- Make sure the pump is **plugged in** and that its outlet has power. Some pumps share an outlet with other equipment.
- Lift the float gently (or pour in a little water). The pump should switch on. If it doesn't, the motor or float switch has failed.
- Clean slime out of the reservoir and check the outlet tube for kinks or clogs.
- Replacement pumps are widely available. Match the voltage and the safety switch wiring. If the pump's safety switch is wired into the AC controls, have a technician do the swap.

## 6. A disconnected or damaged drain line

Drain line joints can pull apart, especially after other work near the unit, and lines can crack. Water then pours out at the joint instead of running away.

**Fix:**

- Follow the line from the unit with a flashlight and look for **separated fittings**, cracks or wet spots along its length.
- Push loose fittings back together. PVC joints may need to be cut and re-glued with the right primer and cement.
- Check that the line **slopes downhill** all the way to its outlet. A sagging section can hold water and gunk.

## 7. Sweating ducts or vents

Supply ducts carry very cold air. In a hot, humid attic or crawlspace, water can condense on the outside of a duct or a ceiling vent (register), then drip through the ceiling. This looks like a leak but is not coming from the unit at all.

**Fix:**

- Check attic ducts for missing, torn or wet insulation and gaps at the joints.
- Seal duct joints with foil tape or mastic and replace damaged insulation.
- If vents themselves drip, it can point to high indoor humidity or low airflow. A technician can check both.

## 8. The unit isn't level

The coil pan is designed to tilt slightly toward its drain outlet. If the indoor unit has shifted, settled or was installed level the wrong way, water can pool at the far end of the pan and spill over even when the drain is clear.

**Fix:**

- Put a small level on top of the unit or the pan and compare it to the drain side.
- Small corrections are sometimes possible with shims, but moving a unit that is connected to ducts and refrigerant lines is a job for a technician.

## Window or ductless AC leaking inside?

- **Window units** should tilt slightly toward the outside so water drains out the back. If yours drips indoors, check the tilt and the installation instructions, and clean any drain holes or channels in the base pan.
- **Ductless mini-split** indoor heads have their own small drain pan and line. A clogged line or a dirty filter is the usual cause. Clean the filters as the manual describes and have the line cleared if water drips from the head.
- **Portable ACs** collect water in a tank or need a drain hose. Empty the tank or check the hose isn't kinked or raised above the outlet.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a pro if:

- The coil keeps **freezing** even with a clean filter and open vents.
- The **drain pan is rusted** through or built into the coil.
- Water has reached **wiring, the furnace controls or the blower**.
- The leak continues after you've cleared the drain line and checked the pump.
- A condensate pump or float switch is **wired into the AC controls**.

A leaking AC is usually a plumbing problem, not a cooling problem. Clear the drain first, then call a technician for anything involving refrigerant, wiring or the coil itself.
