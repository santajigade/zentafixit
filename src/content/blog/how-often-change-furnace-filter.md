Your furnace filter catches dust, lint and pet hair before they reach the blower, the heat exchanger and the AC coil. When it clogs, the whole system struggles for air. How often you change it depends mostly on **how thick the filter is**, **how dusty your home is** and **whether you have pets or allergies**.

There's no single interval that fits every house. The reliable habit is to **check the filter monthly**, follow the interval printed on the filter, and replace it sooner when it looks gray and packed.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>As a rule of thumb, change 1-inch filters every 1 to 3 months and thicker 4 to 5-inch media filters every 6 to 12 months, sooner with pets, allergies or dust. > **Safety first:** Turn the system off at the thermostat (or the furnace power switch) before pulling the filter, so the blower doesn't suck debris in while the slot is open. Don't remove the furnace burner or blower doors to reach a filter unless your manual shows that's where it lives.

## How to tell if your filter needs changing: quick check

1. **Turn the system off** at the thermostat.
2. **Slide the filter out** and note which way the arrow pointed.
3. **Hold it up to a light.** If you can barely see light through it, or the pleats are gray and packed with dust, replace it.
4. **Check the date** if you wrote one on the frame last time. If it's past the interval on the packaging, replace it even if it looks fine.
5. **Write today's date** on the new filter's frame before you slide it in.

## 1. Find your filter and its size

Most furnaces have the filter in one of three places: a **slot in the side of the furnace or air handler** (often with a thin removable cover), a **return air grille** in a wall or ceiling, or a separate **media cabinet** between the return duct and the furnace.

The size is printed on the edge of the frame, as width x height x depth in inches, for example 16 x 25 x 1. That's the **nominal size**, the name it's sold under. The actual filter is often a little smaller so it slides in easily, and the box may list both.

**How:**

- Read the size off the old filter's frame and buy the same nominal size.
- If the label is worn off, measure the slot or the old frame. Round to the nearest common size, and check the actual dimensions printed on the new filter's package.
- Never fold, trim or force a filter. Gaps let unfiltered air bypass it, and a crushed frame can collapse into the blower.

## 2. Know the typical interval for your filter thickness

Thickness matters most. A deeper filter has more pleated media, so it can hold more dust before it starts to restrict airflow. The ranges below are **general guidance only**. The filter maker's recommendation and what you see on monthly checks come first.

| Filter depth | Typical change interval |
| --- | --- |
| 1 inch | About 1 to 3 months |
| 2 inch | About 2 to 3 months |
| 4 inch | About 6 months |
| 5 inch media | About 6 to 12 months |

Thin fiberglass filters (the see-through blue or green kind) are mostly there to protect the equipment and usually need changing more often than pleated ones. Washable filters should be cleaned on the schedule in their instructions and must be fully dry before they go back in.

**How:**

- Run the system a lot in summer and winter? Lean toward the short end of the range.
- Mild spring and fall with the system mostly off? The filter will last longer.

## 3. Change it sooner with pets, allergies or dust

Some homes load filters much faster than others. Check monthly and replace sooner if any of these apply:

- **Pets**, especially dogs or cats that shed heavily.
- **Allergies or asthma** in the household. A fresh filter does its job best.
- **Renovation, sanding or drywall work.** Fine dust can clog a filter in days.
- **Smoking indoors** or frequent use of a wood stove or fireplace.
- **Several people** at home most of the day, or a dusty or windy location.

During construction work, cover nearby return grilles if you can and check the filter weekly. If wildfire smoke is a concern in your area, look at public health guidance for your region on filters and indoor air.

## 4. Choose a sensible MERV rating

**MERV** (Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value) is a standard scale for how well a filter catches particles of different sizes. Residential filters usually fall between **MERV 1 and 16**. The higher the number, the smaller the particles it can trap.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/how-often-change-furnace-filter-merv.svg" alt="MERV scale from 1 to 16 in four bands: MERV 1 to 4 basic fiberglass for large dust and lint; MERV 5 to 8 standard pleated for dust, pollen and mold spores; MERV 9 to 12 better pleated for finer dust and pet dander; MERV 13 to 16 high efficiency for smaller particles including some smoke, which may restrict airflow on some systems" width="640" height="400" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Higher MERV catches smaller particles, but your system has to be able to push air through it.</figcaption></figure>

In general terms:

- **MERV 1 to 4:** basic fiberglass. Catches large dust and lint, mainly protects the equipment.
- **MERV 5 to 8:** standard pleated filters. Good for household dust, pollen and mold spores. A common, safe choice.
- **MERV 9 to 12:** better pleated filters. Catch finer dust and more pet dander. A good middle ground for many homes with pets or allergies.
- **MERV 13 to 16:** high efficiency. Catch smaller particles, including some smoke and bacteria. Many systems can use MERV 13, but not all.

Some retailers sell filters with their own rating scales instead of MERV. Those numbers are not the same as MERV, so compare using the MERV figure if the package lists one.

**How:** For most homes, a **MERV 8 to 11 pleated filter** is a sensible default. If you want MERV 13 or higher for allergies or smoke, check your furnace or air handler manual for filter guidance, or ask an HVAC technician whether your system can handle it. A thicker 4 or 5-inch media cabinet often handles higher MERV filters with less airflow penalty than a 1-inch slot.

## 5. Don't go too restrictive

A filter that's too dense for your system, or simply clogged, chokes the airflow. Your furnace and AC are designed to move a certain amount of air, and when they can't, problems follow.

Signs your filter is too restrictive or overdue:

- **Weak airflow** from the vents, or rooms far from the furnace stay cold or warm.
- The furnace **starts, runs briefly, then shuts off** and repeats. This can be the high limit safety switch tripping from overheating. A blinking code on the furnace's control board may point to it (see [how to read furnace blinking light codes](/hvac/furnace-blinking-light-codes/)).
- **Ice on the indoor AC coil** or the larger copper line in summer.
- A **whistling sound** at the filter slot or return grilles.
- The filter is **bowed or sucked inward**.

**How:** Replace the filter first. If symptoms only started after switching to a higher MERV filter, go back to a lower rating or a thicker filter cabinet, and ask a technician to check your system's airflow.

## 6. Install it with the arrow pointing the right way

Every disposable filter has an **airflow arrow** on the frame. Air flows from the house, through the return duct, through the filter and into the furnace blower. The arrow should point **toward the furnace** and **away from the return duct**.

**How:**

1. With the system off, slide the old filter out and note the arrow direction.
2. Write the date on the new filter's frame.
3. Slide it in with the arrow pointing toward the furnace or air handler. For a filter behind a ceiling or wall return grille, the arrow points **into the duct**, away from the room.
4. Make sure it sits flat with no gaps, then replace the slot cover. A missing cover lets unfiltered air leak in.
5. Turn the system back on.

A filter installed backwards still catches some dust, but pleated filters are built to work in one direction, and some can sag or load unevenly when reversed.

## 7. Set a reminder

Most clogged filters aren't a choice. They're just forgotten.

**How:**

- Set a **repeating phone reminder** to check the filter on the first of each month.
- Keep **one or two spares** next to the furnace so you aren't tempted to wait.
- Some smart thermostats can remind you to change the filter based on run time. Check your thermostat's settings or app.
- Some filter makers and retailers offer subscriptions that ship filters on a schedule.

## Can't find the filter?

- **Look at every return grille.** Larger homes may have two or more return grilles with filters behind each, and sometimes no filter at the furnace.
- **Check the furnace cabinet** for a narrow slot near where the return duct joins it, usually on the side or bottom. The cover may be a thin metal flap or a magnetic strip.
- **Look for a box** fitted between the duct and the furnace. A media cabinet often has a hinged or lift-off door.
- **Check the manual.** The installation or owner's manual usually shows the filter location for your model.

If you still can't find one, your system may have been installed without a filter. Ask an HVAC technician to look, because running without one lets dust coat the blower and coil.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a licensed HVAC technician if:

- The furnace keeps shutting off on its own, even with a fresh filter.
- The AC coil freezes again after you've replaced the filter and let it thaw.
- Airflow is weak everywhere with a clean filter and open vents.
- You want to move to MERV 13 or higher and aren't sure your system can handle it.
- You'd like a thicker media filter cabinet installed for longer filter life.
