There are two ways to charge an EV at home. **Level 1** uses a regular **120 V** household outlet and the portable cord many EVs come with. **Level 2** uses a **240 V** circuit, like a clothes dryer or range, and a wall-mounted or plug-in charger.

The difference that matters is **speed**: Level 1 adds a few miles of range per hour, Level 2 adds several times that. Whether you need Level 2 depends on **how far you drive each day** and **how long the car sits at home**.

## 1. Voltage and power

Charging speed comes down to power, which is volts times amps.

- **Level 1:** 120 V, usually about **12 A** from a standard 15 A or 20 A outlet. That's roughly **1.4 kW**. Some cords also offer a lower setting, often around 8 A.
- **Level 2:** 240 V at anywhere from about **16 A to 48 A** for typical home units. That's roughly **3.8 to 11.5 kW**.

Both deliver AC power. A charger inside the car (the **onboard charger**) converts it to DC for the battery, and that onboard charger sets a ceiling. If your car's onboard charger is rated, say, 7.2 kW, a 48 A wall unit won't charge it any faster than a 32 A one. Check your car's spec sheet for its maximum AC charging rate.

## 2. Charging speed in miles of range per hour

Miles per hour of charging depends on power and on how efficient your car is (miles per kWh). These are typical ranges, not promises:

| | Level 1 (120 V) | Level 2 (240 V) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical power | About 1.4 kW | About 3.8 to 11.5 kW |
| Range added per hour | About 3 to 5 miles | About 12 to 35+ miles |
| Range in a 10-hour night | About 30 to 50 miles | Often a full charge |
| Full charge from low | Often 1 to 3 days or more | Often overnight |

Larger, heavier EVs like trucks and big SUVs get fewer miles per kWh, so they sit at the low end of each range. Cold weather also slows charging and cuts range.

## 3. Match it to your daily driving

The simple test: **will one night of charging replace what you drove that day?**

1. **Work out your normal daily miles**, including errands, not just the commute.
2. **Count the hours the car sits plugged in** at home on a normal night.
3. **Multiply those hours by 3 to 5 miles** for Level 1. If the result covers your daily miles with some room to spare, Level 1 works.
4. **Allow for bad days:** cold weather, an unplanned trip, or a night you forget to plug in. Level 1 recovers slowly from those.

Example: you drive 30 miles a day and park from 7 pm to 7 am (12 hours). At 3 to 5 miles an hour, Level 1 adds about 36 to 60 miles. That covers you. At 70 miles a day, it doesn't, and the battery slowly drains over the week.

## 4. Cost to buy, install and run

**Running cost is about the same.** You pay for kWh either way, at the same home rate. Level 2 is often slightly more efficient because fewer hours of charging means less time running the car's electronics, but the difference is small. See [how much it costs to charge an EV at home](/ev-charging/ev-home-charging-cost/) to work out your own numbers.

**Upfront cost is where they differ:**

- **Level 1:** often nothing extra if your car came with a portable cord and you have a good outlet near where you park. Some EVs no longer include one, so check what came with yours.
- **Level 2:** the charger itself commonly runs from a few hundred dollars to around $800, plus installation by an electrician. Installation might be a few hundred dollars for a short, simple run, or several thousand if your panel needs an upgrade. See [installing a Level 2 charger](/ev-charging/level-2-charger-installation/) for what drives the price.

Many utilities offer rebates or special EV rates that can cut the cost of Level 2. Check your utility's website before you buy.

## 5. Other differences that matter

- **Convenience:** Level 2 makes plugging in less of a routine you have to plan around.
- **Time-of-use rates:** if your utility charges less overnight, Level 2 can finish inside a short cheap window. Level 1 may need the whole night and spill into pricier hours.
- **Smart features:** most Level 2 units offer apps, scheduling and energy tracking. Many cars can schedule charging on Level 1 too.
- **Plug-in hybrids:** with small batteries, many PHEVs refill on Level 1 overnight, and some have onboard chargers too small to benefit much from a fast Level 2 unit.
- **Dual-voltage portable cords:** some portable cords accept swappable plugs for a 120 V outlet or a 240 V outlet such as a NEMA 14-50, so one cord can do both. Use only plugs and adapters made or approved by the cord's maker.
- **Outlet wear:** Level 1 pulls current for hours. An old or loose outlet can overheat. A new, good-quality outlet on a lightly loaded circuit is a cheap upgrade.

## When Level 1 is enough

Level 1 is often all you need if:

- You drive roughly **30 to 40 miles a day or less** and park at home for 10 hours or more most nights.
- You have a **plug-in hybrid** with a small battery.
- You can top up at work or at public chargers when you need extra.
- You **rent** and can't install a 240 V circuit.

Plenty of drivers start with Level 1, track their charging for a few weeks, and upgrade only if it falls short.

## When to go Level 2

Choose Level 2 if:

- You regularly drive more than Level 1 can replace overnight.
- More than one EV shares the home charger.
- You want the car full every morning, or you use time-of-use rates with a short cheap window.
- You live somewhere cold, where charging is slower and range drops in winter.

If you're adding a 240 V circuit, hire a licensed electrician. They'll check your panel's capacity, size the circuit correctly and pull any permit your area requires.
