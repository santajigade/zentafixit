A shower faucet that keeps dripping after you turn it off has a valve that no longer seals tightly. Water pressure pushes a little past a worn part and comes out of the shower head or the tub spout.

The usual cause is **a worn or gritty cartridge in a single-handle valve, or a hardened rubber washer in an older two or three handle valve**. Before you take anything apart, make sure the drip isn't just leftover water draining out, which is normal.

> **Safety first:** Turn off the water before you remove a cartridge or stem, or water will spray out at full pressure. Lay a towel in the tub so small screws and clips don't go down the drain. If you see water damage in the wall or a stain on the ceiling below, stop and call a plumber.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Wait 5 minutes** after turning the shower off. If the drip slows and stops, it was leftover water (cause 1).
2. **See where the water comes out.** A drip from the shower head or tub spout points to the valve inside (causes 2 to 4). Water around the handle or trim plate points to cause 6.
3. **Count the handles.** One handle that controls on, off and temperature is a single-handle cartridge valve (cause 2). Separate hot and cold handles usually use stems with washers or small cartridges (cause 3).
4. **Check whether it's hot or cold.** Let the drip run onto your hand for a minute. On a two or three handle shower this tells you which side is leaking.
5. **Run the shower in shower mode.** If water still pours from the tub spout, the diverter is worn (cause 5).

## 1. Leftover water draining (often normal)

When you shut off the water, some stays in the pipe above the valve, the shower arm and the head. It drains out as drips for a minute or two, sometimes longer with a large rain-style head.

**Fix:**

- Wait 5 minutes. If the dripping stops on its own, there's nothing to repair.
- If the head drips for a long time but then stops, clean the head's nozzles. Mineral deposits can hold water and let it out slowly.
- If it never stops, move on to cause 2 or 3.

## 2. Worn cartridge (single-handle faucets)

Most modern showers use one handle that controls flow and temperature through a cartridge. The rubber seals on it wear and harden over time, and grit can scratch the sealing surfaces, so a little water gets past even when it's off.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/leaking-shower-faucet-cartridge.svg" alt="Exploded view of a single-handle shower valve: handle and set screw, trim plate, retaining clip or nut, cartridge with rubber seals, and the valve body in the wall with optional built-in stops on each side" width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>The cartridge slides into the valve body and is held by a clip or nut.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

1. Shut off the water. Look for built-in stops on the valve once the trim is off, or use the main shutoff.
2. Pop off the handle cap, remove the handle screw or set screw, and take off the handle and trim plate.
3. Note which way the cartridge faces (take a photo), then pull the **retaining clip** with pliers or unscrew the **retaining nut**.
4. Pull the cartridge straight out. A stuck one may need a cartridge puller made for your brand.
5. Take the old cartridge to the store to match it. Put a thin coat of plumber's grease on the new seals and install it facing the same way.
6. Turn the water back on slowly and test hot, cold and off before you put the trim back.

## 3. Worn washer or seat (two or three handles)

Older showers with separate hot and cold handles (and sometimes a third handle for the diverter) often use compression stems. A rubber washer on the end of each stem presses against a brass seat to stop the flow. As the washer hardens, you have to crank the handle harder to stop the drip.

**Fix:**

1. Use the hot or cold test above to find the leaking side, then shut off the water.
2. Remove that handle and the trim sleeve. Unscrew the stem with a deep shower valve socket (a common, low-cost tool).
3. Replace the washer and its brass screw, and the O-ring on the stem. Take the stem to the store to match the parts.
4. Look into the valve with a flashlight. If the seat is pitted or rough, replace it with a seat wrench, or have a plumber do it.
5. Reassemble and close the handle only until the water stops. Overtightening wears the new washer quickly.

The same stem and washer layout is used in many sink faucets. Our [dripping faucet guide](/plumbing/leaky-faucet/) has a labeled diagram of the parts.

## 4. Mineral buildup or debris in the valve

Hard water leaves scale on cartridges and seats, and grit from a water main repair or plumbing work can lodge in the valve. Either one can stop a seal from closing fully, even on a newer cartridge.

**Fix:**

- Remove the cartridge (cause 2 steps) and look for white crust or grit on the seals and in the valve body.
- Wipe the valve body clean with a soft cloth. Some makers say you can soak a cartridge in white vinegar to loosen scale, but check the instructions, because others say not to.
- If the seals are scratched or flattened, replace the cartridge rather than cleaning it.
- If scale keeps coming back, test your water hardness. A water softener can help, and our guide to a [water softener that isn't working](/plumbing/water-softener-not-working/) covers the common problems.

## 5. Tub spout runs during a shower: the diverter

In a tub and shower combination, a diverter sends water up to the shower head. If water keeps pouring from the tub spout while the shower runs, the diverter is worn. This isn't the same as a drip when everything is off, but people often notice the two together.

**Fix:**

- **Lift-gate diverter on the spout:** the usual fix is a new tub spout. Some slip on with a set screw underneath, others thread onto the pipe. Check which you have before buying one.
- **Separate diverter handle or button on the valve:** the diverter cartridge or stem needs replacing, the same way as cause 2 or 3.

## 6. Leaking around the handle or trim plate

Water seeping out around the handle usually means a worn O-ring on the cartridge or stem. Water running behind the trim plate when you shower can come from cracked caulk or a missing gasket that lets shower spray get into the wall opening.

**Fix:**

- Replace the O-rings, or the whole cartridge if they come as one part.
- Check the trim plate gasket or bead of caulk around it. Some trim instructions say to leave a small gap at the bottom of the caulk so any water that gets in can drain out. Follow your trim instructions.
- Don't run the shower without the trim plate fitted.

## 7. Reassembled wrong after a repair

If it started dripping, or the temperature changed, right after work on the valve, a part may be in the wrong way. Common slips are a cartridge rotated 180 degrees, a retaining clip not fully seated, or a dirty valve body that stops the new seals from seating.

**Fix:**

- Shut off the water, remove the cartridge, clean the valve body and reinstall it, matching your photo or the maker's instructions.
- If hot and cold are swapped, rotate the cartridge or follow the maker's steps to adjust it.
- If the hottest setting is too hot or too cold, adjust the handle's rotation limit stop (see your manual).

## Water behind the wall or a ceiling stain?

A stain or drip on the ceiling under a bathroom, soft drywall next to the shower, or a musty smell can mean water is leaking inside the wall. It may be coming from a valve connection, a drain, or failed grout and caulk, not the faucet you see.

- Stop using that shower if you can, and dry the area.
- If you can reach an access panel behind the valve, look for drips with a flashlight while someone runs the water.
- Call a plumber soon. Hidden leaks can rot framing and grow mold.

To get a feel for how much water a steady drip adds up to, try this [water leak calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/ecology/water-leak/).

## When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if:

- The built-in stops and the main shutoff won't close, so you can't turn off the water.
- The cartridge or stem is seized and won't come out without force.
- The valve body is cracked, corroded or leaks at its pipe connections.
- You see signs of water inside the wall or on the ceiling below.
- The valve is old and parts are no longer made. A plumber can replace the valve, which usually means opening the wall or tile.
