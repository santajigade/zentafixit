A **heat pump** and a **furnace** both keep your house warm, but they do it in completely different ways. A heat pump **moves heat** from the outdoor air into your home using electricity. A gas furnace **burns fuel** to make heat. That one difference drives almost everything else: efficiency, running cost, cold-weather performance and whether you also get air conditioning.

There's no single winner. The better choice depends mostly on **your climate**, **your local electricity and gas prices**, and **what you already have** (ductwork, a gas line, an aging AC).

## How to compare them for your home: quick checklist

1. **Note your climate.** How often does it drop below freezing, and how cold do your coldest weeks get?
2. **Find your energy prices.** Look at a recent electric bill for the price per kWh and a gas bill for the price per therm (include delivery charges, not just supply).
3. **Check what you have.** Do you have ducts, a gas line, and an AC that is near the end of its life?
4. **Check your electrical panel.** A heat pump needs a 240 V circuit, and backup heat strips need more capacity.
5. **Get quotes for both**, plus a dual-fuel option if you have gas, with a proper heat load calculation.

## 1. How a heat pump and a furnace make heat

A **heat pump** works like an air conditioner in reverse. Refrigerant absorbs heat from the outdoor air (there is still usable heat in air well below freezing), a compressor concentrates it, and an indoor coil releases it into your ducts. In summer, a reversing valve flips the flow and it cools your home like a normal AC.

A **gas furnace** burns natural gas (or propane) in a burner. The flame heats a metal heat exchanger, a blower pushes house air across it, and the combustion gases leave through a vent pipe or flue. Because a heat pump moves heat instead of making it, it can deliver more heat than the energy it uses. A furnace can't.

## 2. Efficiency ratings: AFUE, HSPF2, SEER2 and COP

The two systems use different ratings, so you can't compare the numbers directly.

- **AFUE** (furnaces): the percentage of the fuel's energy that ends up as heat in your home over a season. High-efficiency condensing furnaces are rated 90% or higher.
- **HSPF2** (heat pump heating): how much heat a heat pump delivers per unit of electricity over a typical heating season. Higher is better.
- **SEER2** (cooling): the same idea for cooling. It applies to heat pumps and central ACs.
- **COP** (coefficient of performance): heat out divided by energy in at a given moment. A COP of 1 means one unit of heat for one unit of electricity. Heat pumps often run at a COP of roughly 2 to 4 in mild weather, and lower as it gets colder.

**HSPF2** and **SEER2** come from an updated test method and aren't directly comparable with older HSPF and SEER numbers. Find the ratings on the yellow EnergyGuide label or the spec sheet.

## 3. Does a heat pump work in cold weather?

Yes, but its performance changes with the temperature. As it gets colder outside, there's less heat to pull from the air, so a heat pump's **output and efficiency drop** just as your house needs **more** heat. The temperature where the two lines cross is called the **balance point**.

Below the balance point, something else has to help:

- **Backup electric heat strips** inside the air handler. They work in any weather but run at a COP of about 1, so they cost much more to run. Your thermostat may show this as "AUX" or "EM HEAT".
- **A gas furnace**, in a dual-fuel system (see section 8).

**Cold-climate heat pumps** use variable-speed compressors and other design changes to keep much more of their capacity in deep cold. Many are designed to keep heating well below 0°F. In hard-winter areas, check the spec sheet for **heating capacity at low temperatures** and ask your contractor where your balance point will be.

A heat pump also runs **defrost cycles** in cold, damp weather, and its supply air feels warm rather than hot. That's normal. If yours seems to blow cold air for long periods, see [heat pump blowing cold air](/hvac/heat-pump-blowing-cold-air/).

## 4. Operating cost: electricity vs gas prices

What you pay depends on two things: **the price of each unit of energy** and **how efficiently the system turns it into heat**. Compare the cost of the same amount of delivered heat:

- **Gas furnace:** a therm of gas contains 100,000 Btu. Divide your price per therm by the AFUE (as a decimal) to get the cost of 100,000 Btu of delivered heat.
- **Heat pump:** a kWh of electricity contains about 3,412 Btu. Multiply your price per kWh by 29.3 (the kWh in 100,000 Btu), then divide by the COP.

The reasoning that falls out of this:

- **Cheap gas and expensive electricity** favor the furnace, especially in very cold weather when the heat pump's COP drops.
- **Moderate electricity and expensive gas or propane** favor the heat pump, often by a wide margin.
- **Climate matters.** The milder your winters, the more hours the heat pump runs at a high COP.

Use the full price on your bills, including delivery fees and taxes, not just the supply rate.

## 5. Upfront cost

Installed prices vary a lot by region, home size, ductwork and electrical work, so get several quotes.

- **A gas furnace alone** is usually the cheaper purchase, often a few thousand dollars installed for a straight replacement.
- **A ducted heat pump** usually costs more than a furnace alone, but it also replaces your central AC. Compare it against a **furnace plus a new AC**.
- **Cold-climate models**, new ductwork, a panel upgrade or a new 240 V circuit all add cost.
- **No ducts?** Ductless mini-split heat pumps are an option.

## 6. Cooling: one system or two

A heat pump **heats and cools** with the same outdoor unit and indoor coil. A furnace **only heats**, so you need a separate central AC, which shares the furnace's blower and ducts.

If your AC is due for replacement anyway, that's a good moment to consider a heat pump, since you'd buy an outdoor unit either way.

## 7. Lifespan and maintenance

Lifespans depend on installation, climate and maintenance, so these are broad expectations only:

- **Furnaces** often last around 15 to 20 years or more. They only run in winter.
- **Heat pumps** often last around 10 to 15 years. They run year-round for heating and cooling, which adds wear.

Both need regular filter changes and periodic professional service. A furnace also needs its burners, flue and heat exchanger checked. Keep a heat pump's outdoor unit clear of snow, ice and leaves.

## 8. Dual-fuel (hybrid) systems

A **dual-fuel** or **hybrid** system pairs a heat pump with a gas furnace instead of electric heat strips. The heat pump handles mild and moderate weather efficiently, and the furnace takes over below a changeover temperature, when the heat pump struggles or when gas becomes the cheaper fuel.

- It suits homes that **already have gas** and live in **cold climates**.
- The changeover point is set on a compatible thermostat or control board.
- It keeps a fuel-burning appliance in the house, so the safety points below still apply.

## 9. Safety

A **gas furnace is a combustion appliance**. A blocked flue, cracked heat exchanger or poor venting can let **carbon monoxide** (CO) into your home. CO has no smell or color.

- Install **CO alarms** on every level and near sleeping areas, and follow your local code and the alarm maker's instructions.
- Have the furnace inspected regularly by a qualified technician.
- If a CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call 911 or your gas utility. See [carbon monoxide alarm going off](/home-safety/carbon-monoxide-alarm-going-off/).

A heat pump burns no fuel, so it produces no CO. You still need CO alarms if the home has any other fuel-burning appliance or an attached garage. Heat pumps and backup heat strips draw a lot of power at 240 V, so wiring work belongs with a licensed electrician or HVAC installer.

| Factor | Heat pump | Gas furnace |

## Incentives and rebates

Federal tax credits for heat pumps and efficient furnaces have changed several times in recent years, and some have ended. State, utility and other rebates may still be available, often only for certain efficiency levels or cold-climate models. **Rules and deadlines change often**, so check current requirements with the IRS, your state energy office and your utility before you buy. The ENERGY STAR website (energystar.gov) is a good place to look up qualifying equipment.

## Which one should you choose?

- **Mild or moderate climate, or no gas line:** a heat pump is usually the strongest choice.
- **Cold climate, no gas:** look at a **cold-climate heat pump** sized for your coldest days.
- **Cold climate with cheap gas:** a high-efficiency furnace, or a **dual-fuel** system if you also need a new AC.
- **Propane or oil heat:** a heat pump can often lower running costs, alone or as a hybrid.

## When to talk to an HVAC contractor

Talk to a licensed HVAC contractor before you decide, and ask for:

- A **room-by-room heat load calculation** (often called a Manual J) rather than sizing by the old unit or square footage alone.
- Quotes for a heat pump, a furnace with AC, and a dual-fuel option where gas is available.
- The **low-temperature heating capacity** of any heat pump they suggest, and where backup heat will kick in.
- Whether your **ducts and electrical panel** can handle the new system.

If you already have a heat pump that isn't keeping up, it may need a repair rather than a replacement. Start with [heat pump not heating](/hvac/heat-pump-not-heating/).
