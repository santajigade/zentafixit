A ceiling fan that won't run usually has a simple cause. Fans have **several separate controls** (wall switch, pull chain, remote, reverse switch) and any one of them in the wrong position stops the motor. Before you assume the fan is dead, check each one.

If the controls are fine, the most common parts to fail are the **remote receiver**, the **capacitor** (which helps the motor start) and **loose wires** in the ceiling canopy. A truly burnt-out motor is the least likely cause.

> **Safety first:** Use a sturdy step ladder, never a chair. Before opening the canopy or switch housing, **turn off the breaker**, not just the wall switch, and confirm the wires are dead with a non-contact voltage tester. Keep hands clear of the blades when testing, and never run a fan that wobbles badly or smells burnt.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Check the wall switch.** Some rooms have two switches, one for the fan and one for the light.
2. **Check whether the light works** (if your fan has one).
    - **Light works, fan doesn't:** power is reaching the fan. Look at the pull chain, remote, reverse switch and capacitor (causes 2 to 5).
    - **Nothing works:** check the breaker, the remote receiver and the canopy wiring (causes 1, 3 and 6).
3. **Listen closely** with the fan switched on. A **hum with no movement** points to the capacitor or reverse switch.
4. **Give a blade a gentle push** with the fan switched on. If it starts spinning, the capacitor is very likely bad.

## 1. The wall switch or breaker is off

It sounds obvious, but it happens a lot: someone switched off the wall switch to use the remote, or the breaker tripped. Many fans with a remote need the **wall switch left on** all the time.

**Fix:**

- Turn the wall switch on and leave it on if you use a remote.
- Check the breaker panel. A tripped breaker often sits in the middle position: push it firmly to OFF, then ON.
- If the breaker trips again when the fan starts, stop and call an electrician.

## 2. The pull chain is on OFF

Fans with a pull chain usually cycle through **high, medium, low and off**. If the chain was left on off, the wall switch and remote can't start the motor. A pull-chain switch can also wear out and stop making contact.

**Fix:**

- With the wall switch on, pull the chain **one click at a time**, waiting a few seconds after each, through a full cycle.
- If nothing happens on any setting, or the chain feels loose or broke off inside, the switch may need replacing. These are inexpensive parts, but they're wired inside the switch housing, so match the number of wires and positions exactly.

## 3. Remote batteries or receiver pairing

Remote-controlled fans have a **receiver** hidden in the canopy or the switch housing. If the remote's batteries are low or the remote has lost its pairing, the fan won't respond.

**Fix:**

1. Put **fresh batteries** in the remote. Check that the remote's light (if it has one) flashes when you press a button.
2. **Re-pair the remote.** Many need you to switch power off at the wall or breaker, switch it back on, then press a pair or learn button within a short window. Check your remote's instructions for the exact steps.
3. Older remotes use small **DIP switches** (tiny on/off switches) inside the battery compartment that must match switches on the receiver. A neighbor's remote or a recent battery change can upset them.
4. If the remote is fine but nothing responds, the **receiver may have failed**. Replacement receivers are available, but must match the fan's wiring. A failed receiver is a common cause of a fan that does nothing at all.

## 4. The reverse switch is stuck in the middle

Most fans have a small **direction switch** on the motor housing for summer and winter rotation. If it's left halfway between positions, the motor gets confused signals and may not run, or just hums.

**Fix:**

- Switch the fan **off** and let the blades stop completely.
- Push the reverse switch **firmly all the way** to one side, then try again.
- Never flip it while the blades are spinning.

## 5. A failed capacitor

Ceiling fan motors use a **capacitor** to get started and to set the speeds. When it weakens, the fan may **hum without spinning**, spin very slowly, **start only if you push a blade**, or work on some speeds but not others. It's one of the most common failures on older fans.

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the breaker and confirm the power is off.
2. Open the **switch housing** below the motor (sometimes under the light kit). The capacitor is usually a small black box with several wires.
3. Note the printed values: the **microfarad (µF) ratings**, the **voltage rating** and the number of wires. Photograph the connections before you remove anything.
4. Fit an **exact match** and reconnect the wires the same way. Swelling, cracks or a burnt smell confirm the old one failed.

If you'd like to understand how these parts fail and are tested, this guide on [how to test a capacitor](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/how-to-test-a-capacitor/) covers the basics. If you're not comfortable with this kind of wiring, an electrician or handyman can replace it quickly.

## 6. Loose wiring in the canopy

Fans vibrate, and over years the **wire connectors** in the ceiling canopy or the switch housing can loosen. A loose connection can make the fan work intermittently, stop completely, or only run when you wiggle it.

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the breaker and confirm the power is off.
2. Support the fan and lower the canopy cover according to the fan's instructions.
3. Check that each **wire connector** is tight and no bare copper shows beyond it. Gently tug each wire.
4. Look for **scorched or melted** insulation. If you see any, stop and call an electrician.

## 7. A worn-out motor

If the controls, capacitor and wiring check out, the motor itself may have failed. Signs include a **burning smell**, a motor that's **very hot** to the touch, grinding or scraping noises, or blades that are stiff to turn by hand with the power off.

**Fix:**

- Switch it off at the breaker and don't use it if it smells burnt.
- Fan motors are rarely worth rebuilding. Replacing the whole fan is usually cheaper and quicker.

## Nothing works, not even the light?

- Confirm the **breaker** is on and test the wall switch with a non-contact tester at the switch (power at the switch means the fault is further along).
- If the fan has a remote, suspect the **receiver**, which controls both the light and the fan.
- Check the canopy wiring for a loose or burnt connection.
- If it was **just installed**, recheck the wiring against the fan's instructions, or have an electrician look at it.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The breaker trips when the fan starts.
- You see **scorched wires**, smell burning, or the canopy is hot.
- There's no power at the fan and the breaker and switch are on.
- You're not comfortable working on a ladder with wiring overhead.

Most fan failures come down to a switch setting, a remote, or a capacitor, all cheap and fixable.
