A circuit breaker trips when it senses more current than the circuit's wiring can safely carry, or, for special breakers, a shock or arcing hazard. Each trip is the breaker doing its job: stopping the wire inside your walls from overheating.

When one keeps tripping, the cause is usually **too many things running on one circuit**, a **short circuit or ground fault** in a device or the wiring, or a **sensitive AFCI or GFCI breaker** reacting to a real problem. Less often, the **breaker itself is worn out**.

> **Safety first:** You can safely reset breakers and unplug devices yourself. Do not remove the panel cover (the dead front). The large wires inside stay live even with the main breaker off. Stand on a dry floor, use one hand on the breaker, and stop if you see scorch marks, smell burning or the panel feels hot.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Note what was running** when it tripped and **how long** after you switched it on.
2. **Unplug everything** on that circuit and switch off its lights.
3. **Reset the breaker** properly (see below).
    - **Trips immediately with nothing connected?** The fault is in the wiring, a fixture or the breaker. Skip to the "won't reset" section and plan to call an electrician.
    - **Stays on?** Plug devices back in **one at a time**, waiting a few minutes after each.
4. **If one device trips it** every time, even alone, that device has a short or ground fault (causes 2 and 3).
5. **If it only trips with several devices running**, the circuit is overloaded (cause 1).

## How to reset a breaker properly

A tripped breaker often sits in a **middle position**, between ON and OFF, so it can look like it's still on. Some have a colored flag in a small window.

1. **Turn off or unplug** the big loads on that circuit first.
2. **Push the handle firmly to OFF.** You should feel a click as the mechanism re-latches.
3. **Push it back to ON.**
4. If it **won't stay on** or trips again right away, don't keep trying. Repeated resets into a fault stress the breaker and the wiring.

## 1. An overloaded circuit

This is the most common cause. A standard 15 amp circuit at 120 V can supply about 1,800 watts, and a 20 amp circuit about 2,400 watts. A space heater alone often draws around 1,500 watts, so add a hair dryer, toaster or vacuum on the same circuit and the breaker trips after a few seconds to a few minutes. Kitchens, bathrooms and home offices with space heaters are the usual trouble spots.

**Fix:**

- Find the wattage on each appliance's label and add them up. This [amps to watts calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/amps-to-watts/) helps if the label lists amps instead.
- Move heaters, kettles, microwaves and hair dryers to **different circuits**. Find out which outlets are on which breaker by switching one off and checking.
- A common rule of thumb is to keep steady loads to about **80% of the breaker rating** (about 1,440 W on 15 A, 1,920 W on 20 A).
- If you regularly need more power in one room, ask an electrician about adding a **new circuit**. Power strips don't add capacity.

## 2. A short circuit

A short happens when a hot wire touches a neutral wire, so current flows with almost nothing to limit it. The breaker trips **instantly**, often with a loud snap, and sometimes you'll see a flash or smell burning at an outlet, plug or fixture.

**Fix:**

- Use the one-at-a-time test. If one device trips it immediately, inspect its **cord and plug** for damage, scorching or melted spots. Repair or replace the device.
- If it trips with **nothing plugged in**, the short is in the wiring, an outlet, a switch or a light fixture. Leave the breaker off and call an electrician.
- Check outlets for **black marks** or melted plastic. A burnt outlet must be replaced before the circuit is used.

## 3. A ground fault

A ground fault is current escaping to ground, through a metal box, a grounding wire, water or a person. Moisture is a common trigger: wet outdoor outlets, a leaking pipe above a fixture, or water in a buried cable. A standard breaker trips on a strong ground fault; a GFCI breaker trips on a much smaller one.

**Fix:**

- Check outdoor outlets, garage outlets and exterior lights on that circuit for water. Let them dry with the power off.
- Fit **weatherproof "in-use" covers** on outdoor outlets.
- If it trips every time it rains and you can't find the wet spot, have an electrician check buried or exterior wiring.

## 4. An AFCI breaker sensing an arc

AFCI (arc-fault circuit interrupter) breakers have a **TEST button** and are common in bedrooms and living areas of newer homes. They trip when they detect the electrical signature of **arcing**: a damaged cord, a loose wire, a cord pinched under furniture or nailed through, or a loose connection in an outlet.

**Fix:**

- Look for **damaged or crushed cords**, especially behind beds, couches and desks. Replace them.
- Unplug devices one at a time. Some older **vacuum cleaners, treadmills and tools with brushed motors** can occasionally trip AFCIs.
- Some AFCI breakers show the reason for the last trip with an indicator light. Check the breaker label or the maker's instructions.
- If it trips with nothing connected, or after recent wiring work, call an electrician. Loose connections and shared neutrals between circuits are common causes and need a pro.

## 5. A GFCI breaker detecting leakage

GFCI breakers also have a **TEST button** and protect bathrooms, kitchens, garages, outdoor outlets, hot tubs and similar areas. They trip at a few thousandths of an amp leaking to ground. Some breakers are **dual function**, combining AFCI and GFCI protection in one.

**Fix:**

- Treat it like a tripping GFCI outlet: check for **moisture** and unplug appliances one at a time. Our guide to a [GFCI outlet that keeps tripping](/electrical/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/) walks through the same steps.
- Motors and heating elements (pumps, refrigerators, washers, heaters) can develop small leaks as they age.
- Press **TEST** once a month. If it doesn't trip, the breaker needs replacing.

## 6. A worn-out or loose breaker

Breakers are mechanical and can wear out, especially after many trips. A worn one may trip at loads well under its rating. A breaker that isn't seated firmly on the panel's bus bar can overheat at the connection.

**Fix:**

- Feel the breaker face lightly with the back of your hand. **Warm is a warning, hot is a stop.** Also listen for buzzing.
- If the breaker trips at light loads that the circuit handled for years, or it's hot, buzzing, discolored or smells burnt, **call an electrician**. Replacing breakers means removing the panel cover, which is not a DIY job.

## Never tape, hold or upsize a breaker

It's tempting to tape a handle on, hold it in place, or swap a 15 amp breaker for a 20 amp one so it stops tripping. Don't. The breaker size is matched to the **wire gauge** (14-gauge copper is typically on 15 A, 12-gauge on 20 A). A bigger breaker lets the wire overheat inside the wall before anything trips, which is how electrical fires start. A breaker that can't trip can't protect you.

## Breaker trips instantly or won't reset?

- **Nothing plugged in and it still trips?** There's a short or ground fault in the wiring, an outlet, a switch or a fixture. Leave it off.
- **Handle feels loose or won't latch?** The breaker may be damaged.
- **Main breaker tripping?** The whole house is affected. Call an electrician, and your utility if you see damage at the meter.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- It trips with **nothing connected**, or trips the moment you reset it.
- You see **scorch marks**, smell burning, or the breaker or panel is **hot or buzzing**.
- An AFCI or GFCI breaker keeps tripping and you can't link it to a device or moisture.
- You need more capacity in a room, or anything requires opening the panel.

A tripping breaker is a warning, not an annoyance. Find the reason, and let a pro handle anything inside the panel or walls.
