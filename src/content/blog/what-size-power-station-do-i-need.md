A portable power station is a big rechargeable battery with built-in outlets. To size one for a power outage you need two numbers: **how many watts** your devices draw at once, and **how many watt-hours** they use over the time you need them to run.

Most people get it wrong in one of two ways: they buy a station that **can't handle the startup surge** of a fridge or pump, or one with **too little battery** to last the night. These steps take care of both.

## How to size it: the short version

1. **List the essentials** you want to keep running, and write down the watts from each nameplate.
2. **Add the running watts** of everything that might run at the same moment. This sets the minimum **output rating** (W).
3. **Find the biggest startup surge** (usually a fridge or pump). This sets the minimum **surge rating**.
4. **Multiply watts by hours** for each item and add them up. Divide by 0.85. This sets the minimum **battery capacity** (Wh).

## 1. Learn the difference between watts and watt-hours

Every power station lists two main numbers, and they mean different things.

- **Watts (W)** is power: how much the station can deliver at one moment. A station rated 1,000 W can run a 900 W microwave but not a 1,500 W space heater.
- **Watt-hours (Wh)** is energy: how much is stored in the battery. A 1,000 Wh station could, in theory, run a 100 W load for 10 hours.

A big battery with a small inverter can run small things for a long time. A small battery with a big inverter can run a microwave, but not for long. You need both numbers to fit your plan.

## 2. List what you need to run

Start with real essentials: food storage, light, communication, and any medical equipment. Find the watts on each device's nameplate, power adapter or manual. A plug-in watt meter (about $15 to $30) measures the real draw and is more accurate than any chart.

The figures below are **typical approximate ranges only**. Your appliances may be very different, so always check the nameplate.

| Appliance | Typical running watts | Typical startup surge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refrigerator (full-size) | 100 to 250 W while the compressor runs | 600 to 1,200 W |
| Chest freezer | 50 to 150 W while running | 400 to 800 W |
| Sump pump (1/3 to 1/2 HP) | 800 to 1,100 W | 1,300 to 2,200 W |
| CPAP machine | 30 to 60 W (more with heated humidifier) | None |
| Wi-Fi router and modem | 10 to 30 W together | None |
| LED light bulb | 8 to 12 W each | None |
| Laptop | 30 to 100 W | None |
| Phone charging | 5 to 20 W | None |
| TV (LED, 32 to 55 inch) | 50 to 150 W | None |
| Microwave | 1,000 to 1,500 W | None |
| Coffee maker | 600 to 1,200 W | None |
| Space heater | Up to 1,500 W | None |

If a label lists amps and volts instead of watts, multiply them, or use this [amps to watts calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/amps-to-watts/).

If someone in your home relies on powered medical equipment, ask the device maker or your provider about battery runtime and backup options too.

## 3. Add up the running watts

Add the running watts of everything that could be on **at the same time**. Your station's continuous output rating must be higher than this total. Leave about 20% headroom so the inverter isn't working at its limit.

Heaters, microwaves, kettles and hair dryers draw a lot of power. Run them one at a time, or leave them off the list. A space heater at 1,500 W would drain a 2,000 Wh station in a little over an hour.

## 4. Check the biggest startup surge

Anything with a motor or compressor, such as a **refrigerator, freezer, sump pump or window AC**, draws a short burst of extra power when it starts. Power stations list a **surge** or **peak** rating for this, often around double the continuous rating, but it varies by model.

**Fix:**

- Find the largest single surge on your list. The station's surge rating must cover it, on top of whatever else is running.
- If the station shuts off or shows an overload warning when the fridge kicks on, the surge is too much. Turn other loads off and try again.
- Check the inverter is **pure sine wave**. Most quality power stations are, and motors and medical devices run best on it.
- Many well pumps and central AC units run on **240 V**. Most portable stations supply only 120 V, so check before you count on one.

## 5. Estimate hours of use and watt-hours

Now work out how much energy you'll use. For each device:

**Watts × hours of use = watt-hours (Wh)**

Be realistic about hours. Lights run in the evening, a laptop maybe a few hours, a router all day.

A fridge is the tricky one, because its compressor cycles on and off. The best guide is the yellow **EnergyGuide label** (or the spec sheet), which lists estimated **kWh per year**. Divide by 365 for a daily figure. Many modern fridges land around 1 to 2 kWh per day. Keep the doors closed during an outage and it will use less.

## 6. Allow for inverter losses

Turning the battery's DC power into 120 V AC wastes some energy as heat. Most power station inverters are roughly **85 to 90% efficient**, and the station itself uses a little power just to stay on.

**Fix:** Divide your total watt-hours by **0.85** to get the battery size you need. If you can charge phones and laptops from the USB or USB-C ports instead of the AC outlets, you skip some of that loss.

You can check your runtime numbers with this [inverter battery backup calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/everyday-life/inverter-battery-backup/).

## 7. Work through an example

Here's a plan for a 24-hour outage. The watts are examples, not measurements of your appliances.

| Item | Watts | Hours | Watt-hours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Refrigerator (label: about 500 kWh per year) | 150 W running | Cycles all day | about 1,400 Wh |
| Wi-Fi router and modem | 20 W | 24 h | 480 Wh |
| Laptop | 50 W | 4 h | 200 Wh |
| Two LED bulbs | 20 W | 5 h | 100 Wh |
| Two phone charges | 20 W | 2 h | 40 Wh |
| **Total** | **260 W** | | **2,220 Wh** |

- **Output:** 260 W running plus headroom. Almost any mid-size station handles this.
- **Surge:** the fridge may need up to about 1,200 W for a moment, so look for a surge rating comfortably above that.
- **Battery:** 2,220 Wh ÷ 0.85 = about **2,600 Wh**.

So you'd want a station of roughly 2,600 Wh or more, or a smaller one plus a way to recharge it during the day.

## 8. Plan how to recharge it

Long outages need a recharge plan. Most stations can charge from a wall outlet, a car's 12 V socket, or **solar panels**.

- **Check the station's solar input limits:** maximum watts and the allowed voltage range. Panels wired together must stay inside that voltage range.
- **Expect less than the panel's label.** Real output drops with heat, clouds, shade and a low sun. Many US locations get roughly 4 to 6 hours of strong sun on a clear summer day, and fewer in winter.
- **Rough planning:** 400 W of panels × 5 sun hours × 0.75 for real-world losses ≈ 1,500 Wh on a good day. That could cover a fridge but not much more.
- Point panels at the sun, keep them out of shade, and move them during the day if you can.

## 9. Use it safely

- **Never backfeed your house.** Don't plug a power station (or generator) into a wall outlet with a double-male cord. It can energize utility lines and injure line workers, and it bypasses your breakers.
- Run devices from the station's own outlets with properly rated extension cords. Don't chain several cords together.
- Keep it dry and away from heat, and leave the vents clear.
- Follow the manufacturer's charging temperature limits. Many lithium batteries shouldn't be charged below freezing.
- Keep it charged before storm season. Most lose a little charge in storage, so top it up every few months.

## Need to power whole circuits or the whole house?

If you want to run hardwired loads like a furnace blower or a well pump, or power circuits through your panel, the station must connect through a **transfer switch** or a **power inlet box with an interlock kit**. This keeps your home disconnected from the grid while the station feeds it.

Some larger power stations and home battery systems are designed for this. It needs a **licensed electrician**, and often a permit, to install. For whole-home backup, a permanently installed home battery or standby generator may suit you better than a portable unit.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- You want the station to power circuits through your breaker panel.
- You need to run 240 V equipment like a well pump.
- You're installing a power inlet, interlock or transfer switch.
- A breaker or outlet you plan to use is warm, discolored or smells of burning.

Sizing the station yourself is easy. Connecting it to your home's wiring is a job for a pro.
