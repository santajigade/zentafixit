A Level 2 charger runs on a **240 V** circuit, the same kind of supply as an electric dryer or range. Buying the charger is the easy part. What decides the price and the timeline is **whether your electrical panel has spare capacity**, **how far the charger is from the panel**, and **what your local permit rules require**.

This guide covers what to sort out before you buy, so the electrician's quote makes sense and there are no surprises.

> **Safety first:** Adding a 240 V circuit means working inside your electrical panel, where some parts stay live even with the main breaker off. Leave panel work, wiring and breaker sizing to a licensed electrician, and use a charger that's safety-listed (look for a UL, ETL or similar certification mark).

## Before you call an electrician

1. **Find your car's maximum AC charging rate** (in kW or amps) in the manual or spec sheet. There's no benefit in a charger stronger than the car can accept.
2. **Decide where you'll park** and which side your charge port is on.
3. **Take clear photos** of your panel with the door open, including the main breaker's rating and the label.
4. **Check your utility's EV page** for rebates, approved charger lists and EV rates.
5. **Ask two or three electricians for quotes**, ideally ones who install EV chargers regularly.

## 1. Check your panel capacity

Your home's service (often 100 A, 150 A or 200 A) has to carry everything at once: HVAC, water heater, range, dryer and now hours of EV charging. An electrician works this out with a **load calculation** under the electrical code, not by counting empty breaker slots. Empty slots don't mean spare capacity.

**What happens next:**

- **Plenty of room:** add a dedicated 240 V circuit for the charger.
- **Tight:** set the charger to a **lower current**. Many chargers let the installer choose the maximum. A 24 A or 32 A setting still refills most daily driving overnight.
- **Not enough:** a **load management device** can pause or slow charging when other big loads run, or you may need a **panel or service upgrade**. Upgrades cost the most and may involve your utility.

## 2. Size the circuit to the charger

EV charging is treated as a **continuous load** because it can run at full current for hours. The general rule is that the circuit and breaker must be rated at **125% of the charger's continuous current**. Put the other way, the charger can use up to 80% of the breaker rating.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/level-2-charger-installation-circuit-sizing.svg" alt="Table showing charger current and the circuit size it usually needs under the 125% rule: 16 A needs 20 A, 24 A needs 30 A, 32 A needs 40 A, 40 A needs 50 A, 48 A needs 60 A and is usually hardwired" width="640" height="400" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Charger amps times 1.25 gives the usual circuit size.</figcaption></figure>

Wire size depends on the breaker, the wire type and how long the run is, so leave it to your electrician. If you ever install a bigger charger later, the circuit may need new wire too. Some people pre-wire for a higher-amp circuit than they need today; ask your electrician whether that's worth it.

## 3. Permits and inspection

Most areas require an **electrical permit** for a new 240 V circuit, followed by an **inspection**. Your electrician usually pulls the permit. Skipping it can cause trouble with insurance, rebates and selling the house later.

- Ask whether the quote includes the permit fee.
- In some areas the utility also wants to know about new EV charging, especially if you're applying for a rebate or EV rate.
- Requirements vary by city and county. Check with your local building department if you're unsure.

## 4. Plug-in or hardwired

| | Plug-in (NEMA 14-50 outlet) | Hardwired |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Max charging current | 40 A (on a 50 A circuit) | Up to 48 A on a 60 A circuit, more with some units |
| Take it when you move | Yes | Needs an electrician |
| Outlet wear | Plug and outlet can wear or overheat over time | No outlet to wear |
| GFCI considerations | Newer codes in many areas require GFCI protection for 240 V outlets in garages and outdoors, which can cause nuisance trips with some chargers | Uses the charger's built-in protection, ask your electrician |

If you go with an outlet, insist on a **heavy-duty, industrial-grade** receptacle. Cheap outlets are a known overheating point under hours of high current. For a deeper look, see [NEMA 14-50 vs hardwired](/ev-charging/nema-14-50-vs-hardwired/).

## 5. Choose the location

- **Cable reach:** measure from the mounting spot to the charge port, with the car parked normally. Allow some slack, and check the charger's cable length before buying.
- **Outdoors:** pick a charger rated for outdoor use and mount it where the cable won't lie in standing water or get run over.
- **Distance from the panel:** every foot of wire, conduit and wall to drill through adds cost. A charger on the other side of the garage wall from the panel is the cheapest case.
- **Wi-Fi:** if you want app features, scheduling or utility programs, check the signal at the spot.
- **Two EVs:** consider a charger that can share power between two cars, or two chargers with load sharing, rather than two full circuits.

## 6. What drives the cost

Prices vary a lot by region. As a rough guide only:

- **Charger:** from a few hundred dollars to around **$800** for common home units.
- **Simple install** (short run, panel has room): often a few hundred dollars to around **$1,500**.
- **Long runs, trenching, subpanels, drywall repair:** can push the install past **$2,000**.
- **Panel or service upgrade:** often adds **several thousand dollars**, sometimes more if the utility has to upgrade the service drop.
- **Permit:** a fee set by your city or county.

Getting several quotes is the best way to know what's normal where you live.

## 7. Rebates and EV rates

- **Utility rebates:** many utilities offer rebates on the charger or install, sometimes only for approved, network-connected models. Some require you to apply **before** installation.
- **EV or time-of-use rates:** cheaper overnight electricity can cut your running cost a lot. Some utilities require a separate meter or an approved charger to qualify.
- **State and local programs:** some states, cities and electric co-ops offer their own incentives.
- **Federal tax credits:** these rules have changed recently. Check IRS.gov or ask a tax professional for the current status rather than relying on older articles.

Once you're charging, work out what it really costs with [how much it costs to charge an EV at home](/ev-charging/ev-home-charging-cost/).

## When to call an electrician

For this job, from the start. A licensed electrician should:

- Do the **load calculation** and tell you what current your panel can support.
- **Size the breaker and wire** to the charger and your local code.
- **Pull the permit** and be there for the inspection if required.
- Advise on **GFCI requirements**, outlet vs hardwired, and load management.

Call them back if the new circuit's breaker trips, the outlet or plug feels hot, or you smell burning near the charger. Stop charging until it's checked.
