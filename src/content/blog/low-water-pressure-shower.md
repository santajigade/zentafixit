A weak shower usually has a simple cause. Start by finding out **whether the problem is only this shower or the whole house**. If every faucet is weak, read our guide to [low water pressure in the house](/plumbing/low-water-pressure/) instead.

If only the shower is weak, the most common causes are **a clogged showerhead or inlet screen, a low-flow restrictor, or a clogged or worn valve cartridge**. Most of them take less than an hour to fix.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>Unscrew the showerhead and run the water without it. If the flow is strong, clean the head, its inlet screen and restrictor in white vinegar, or replace the head. > **Safety first:** Turn off the water before you pull a shower cartridge, either at the stops on the valve or at the main shutoff, and open the shower to release pressure. Shower water can be hot enough to scald, so test the temperature by hand after any valve work. Don't force a stiff old valve, shutoff or corroded fitting. If something won't move, stop and call a plumber before it cracks or starts leaking inside the wall.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Run a sink in another room.** If it is weak too, the problem is house-wide (cause 8).
2. **Try hot and cold separately.** Turn the shower to full cold, then full hot. If only hot is weak, go to cause 7. If both are weak, keep going.
3. **Remove the showerhead** (cover the nut with a rag and turn it counterclockwise) and run the water straight from the arm into a bucket. Strong flow means the head is the problem (causes 1 to 3). Still weak means the valve or the pipes (causes 5 to 8).
4. **Handheld or tub and shower combo?** Check the hose and the diverter too (cause 4).
5. **Think back.** Did it start after a water shutoff, plumbing work or a new showerhead? That points to debris (causes 2 and 6) or a restrictor (cause 3).

## Measure your flow with a bucket test

A bucket test turns "it feels weak" into a number you can compare.

1. Turn the shower fully on (full cold is fine).
2. Hold a 1-gallon container under the head and time how many **seconds** it takes to fill.
3. Divide 60 by that number to get **gallons per minute (gpm)**. For example, 30 seconds gives 60 / 30 = 2 gpm, and 60 seconds gives 1 gpm.

Compare the result with the flow rating printed on the showerhead. New showerheads in the US are limited by federal rules to a maximum of **2.5 gpm at 80 psi**, and some states set lower limits. So a reading well below the head's own rating means something is restricting it. If you want the number in liters per minute, use this [flow rate converter](https://zentacalculator.com/conversion/flow-rate-converter/).

Repeat the test with the showerhead removed. A big jump in flow confirms the head is the restriction.

## 1. A clogged showerhead

Hard water leaves mineral scale in the nozzles, and some block completely. The spray turns weak and uneven, with jets shooting sideways.

**Fix:**

1. If the head has soft **rubber nozzles**, rub each one with your finger or a soft brush first. This often breaks the scale loose with the water running.
2. Unscrew the head, or tie a plastic bag of **white vinegar** over it with a rubber band so the face is submerged.
3. Soak it for an hour or so, then scrub the face with an old toothbrush and run the water to flush it.
4. On brass, nickel, bronze or other special finishes, check the maker's care instructions first, since a long acid soak can damage some finishes.
5. If it is still weak after cleaning, a new showerhead is an inexpensive fix.

## 2. A clogged inlet screen

Many showerheads have a small **mesh screen** inside the inlet, where the head threads onto the arm. It catches grit, rust and pipe tape, and it is a common clog after plumbing work or a water main repair.

**Fix:**

1. Unscrew the head and look inside the threaded inlet.
2. Pry the screen out gently with a small flat screwdriver or tweezers, rinse it and brush it clean.
3. Before refitting the head, run the water for a few seconds with the head off to flush loose debris out of the arm.
4. Wrap the arm threads with plumber's tape and refit the head hand tight, plus a small turn with a wrench over a rag.

## 3. A low-flow restrictor

Most showerheads contain a **flow restrictor**, a small plastic or rubber disc with a hole, usually behind the inlet screen. It limits the flow to the head's rating. Scale can narrow that hole, and a head with a very low rating may simply feel weak in your home.

**Fix:**

- Clean the restrictor with the screen. Don't enlarge the hole or damage it.
- Removing a restrictor may be against state or local water rules, and it usually voids the showerhead warranty. Many heads are also designed around their restrictor and may spray poorly without it.
- The better fix is a showerhead designed to give a strong spray at its rated flow. Check its gpm rating and reviews before you buy.

## 4. A kinked hose or a sticking diverter

On a **handheld shower**, the hose can kink or the hose washer can block the flow. A **diverter** sends water up to the showerhead or down to the tub spout. A worn diverter lets some water leak out of the spout while the shower runs, so less reaches the head.

**Fix:**

- Straighten the hose, check it for kinks, and look inside both ends for a screen or a misplaced washer.
- If water runs from the tub spout while the shower is on, the diverter is not sealing. On a pull-up spout diverter, replacing the whole spout is often the simplest fix.
- A separate diverter valve or a switch on the handheld bracket can be cleaned or replaced. Check the maker's instructions.

## 5. Stops on the valve not fully open

Some shower valves have **built-in stops**, small screw-type shutoffs on the hot and cold inlets behind the trim plate. If someone closed them for a repair and didn't open them all the way, the flow stays low.

**Fix:**

1. Remove the handle and trim plate (usually one or two screws).
2. Look for slotted stops on the valve body. Turn each one fully open, usually counterclockwise. Check your valve's instructions to be sure.
3. Don't force a stop that won't turn. Old ones can break or leak.

## 6. A stuck or clogged valve cartridge

The **cartridge** inside the shower valve controls flow and temperature. In a **pressure-balancing** valve, a small spool shifts to keep the temperature steady. In a **thermostatic** valve, a temperature element does the job, and some models have small inlet screens. Debris after a water shutoff, or years of scale, can make either one stick or restrict flow.

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the water at the stops or the main shutoff, then open the shower to drain the pressure.
2. Remove the handle, trim and the retaining clip or nut. Note how the cartridge sits before you pull it, since hot and cold orientation matters.
3. Pull the cartridge. A stuck one may need a cartridge puller made for your valve brand.
4. Flush the valve body by briefly opening the water with a towel over it, then clean any screens and inspect the cartridge for scale or worn seals.
5. Reinstall it, or fit an exact replacement. Take the old one to the store or use the valve model number. Our [leaky faucet guide](/plumbing/leaky-faucet/) shows the same basic cartridge steps.
6. Turn the water back on slowly and check the hot limit and temperature before anyone showers.

## 7. Only the hot side is weak

If the cold water is strong and the hot is weak, the restriction is on the hot side. Check the sink in the same bathroom. If its hot water is weak too, the cause is likely at the **water heater**: a partly closed valve on the heater, **sediment** in a tank, or scale in a tankless unit.

**Fix:**

- Weak hot at every faucet: see the hot water section in our [whole-house pressure guide](/plumbing/low-water-pressure/), and check the water heater valves and the flushing or descaling steps in your heater's manual.
- Weak hot at the shower only: it is usually the hot side of the cartridge or a hot stop (causes 5 and 6).
- A pressure-balancing valve cuts the cold flow when hot pressure is low, to prevent scalding. That is why a hot-side problem can make the whole shower feel weak.

## 8. An upper floor, old pipes or a whole-house problem

Showers on an upper floor get a little less pressure because the water has to climb. In older homes, **galvanized steel pipes** narrow slowly as rust builds up inside, and the shower is often the first place you notice. A failing **pressure regulator (PRV)** or a partly closed main valve lowers pressure everywhere.

**Fix:**

- Test the house pressure with a gauge and check the main shutoff and PRV, as described in the whole-house guide linked above.
- If you have galvanized pipes, brown water after a shutoff or pressure that gets worse each year, have a plumber assess repiping.

## Still weak after a new showerhead?

- Run the bucket test with the head off. If it is still slow, the head was never the problem. Go to causes 5 to 8.
- Check the new head's inlet for a screen clogged by debris from the old arm.
- Compare the new head's gpm rating with the old one. A lower rated head will deliver less water.

## When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber if:

- The flow is still weak with the head off and the stops open, and you are not comfortable pulling the cartridge.
- The cartridge is stuck, the valve is corroded, or a stop or fitting starts to leak.
- Water leaks inside the wall, or you see stains or soft drywall near the shower.
- You have galvanized pipes, or the whole house is weak and the PRV and main valve check out.
- The temperature swings or gets too hot after a valve repair.
