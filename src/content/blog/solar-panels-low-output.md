Solar panels rarely fail outright. When your system makes less power than you expect, the cause is usually something outside the panels: **the season, shade, dirt, heat**, or **an inverter or breaker that has stopped the system**. Sometimes nothing is wrong at all and the **monitoring app has simply lost its connection**.

The key is telling a normal dip from a real fault. A slow, seasonal change is expected. A sudden drop on a clear day, or zero output, needs attention.

> **Safety first:** Don't climb onto the roof to inspect or clean panels. Falls are the biggest risk here, and panels, wiring and connectors carry high DC voltage whenever the sun is up, even with the breaker off. Look from the ground with binoculars. Leave opening the inverter, disconnects or wiring to your installer or a licensed electrician.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Look at the inverter itself** (or the gateway display) on a sunny midday. If it shows power being produced, the panels are working, even if the app says zero.
2. **Check the inverter status light.** Green is usually normal. Red, orange or a message on the screen usually means a fault. Colors and codes vary by maker, so check your manual.
3. **Compare like with like.** Pull up the same month last year in your app or on your electric bills, and compare clear days with clear days.
4. **Walk around the house** at the time output drops. Look for shadows on the panels from trees, chimneys, vents or a new building.
5. **Check the electrical panel** for a tripped solar breaker, and check that the rapid shutdown switch (if you have one) is on.

## 1. Season and sun angle

This is the most common "problem" and it isn't one. In winter the days are shorter and the sun is lower, so the same panels collect much less energy than in June. Cloudy weeks in spring or fall do the same.

**Fix:**

- Compare against the **same month last year**, not last month.
- Expect a clear summer day to beat a clear winter day by a wide margin.
- To see what your system should make for your location, panel size and season, try this [solar output calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/ecology/solar-output/). If your real numbers are close to the estimate, the system is healthy.

## 2. Shading from trees, chimneys or new buildings

Even partial shade can cut output by more than you'd expect. On many string inverter systems, shade on part of one panel can pull down the whole string. Systems with microinverters or optimizers handle shade better, but the shaded panels still lose power.

**Fix:**

- Watch the panels at the hour output drops. A shadow that crosses at the same time each day is your answer.
- Trees grow. Trimming branches that now shade the array often restores output. Use an arborist for anything near the roof or power lines.
- If a new obstruction can't be removed, ask your installer whether optimizers or microinverters would help.

## 3. Dirt, pollen, snow and bird droppings

A light layer of dust makes little difference, and rain washes most of it away. Heavy pollen, dust from farms or construction, bird droppings and leaves stuck at the bottom edge can block enough light to notice. Snow stops production until it slides or melts off.

**Fix:**

- Rinse from the ground with a **garden hose** and a **soft brush on an extension pole**, early in the morning or in the evening when the glass is cool.
- Don't use a pressure washer, abrasive pads or strong cleaners. Check your panel warranty for cleaning rules.
- Let snow slide off on its own. Scraping can scratch the glass.
- For a roof that can't be reached from the ground, hire a solar cleaning service.

## 4. Hot weather

Panels like sun but not heat. As the cells get hotter, their output drops a little. Panels on a roof in summer run much hotter than the air around them, so a clear, cool spring day can beat a hot July afternoon.

**Fix:** There's nothing to fix. If you want to check, look up the **temperature coefficient** on your panel datasheet. It tells you how much output falls per degree of heat. Panels mounted with an air gap underneath run cooler than ones mounted flat.

## 5. Inverter errors or a fault light

The inverter turns the DC power from the panels into AC power for your home. If it detects a problem, such as a grid voltage issue, a ground fault or overheating, it stops producing and shows a light or a code. On microinverter systems, a single failed unit only takes out one panel, so the drop is smaller and easy to miss.

**Fix:**

1. Note the status light color and any code or message, then **look it up in your inverter manual** or the maker's website.
2. Check that the inverter's vents are clear and it isn't sitting in direct afternoon sun with no airflow.
3. Many manuals list a **shutdown and restart sequence** for the disconnects. Follow it exactly as written. Don't open the inverter.
4. If the fault comes back, call your installer. Inverters usually carry their own warranty, often separate from the panels.

## 6. A tripped breaker or the rapid shutdown switch is off

A grid-tied system needs a working connection to the grid to produce. If the **solar breaker** in your electrical panel has tripped, or the **AC disconnect** or **rapid shutdown switch** (often a labeled switch near the meter or main panel) has been turned off, the system stops. Someone may have switched it off during a repair, or a storm may have tripped it.

**Fix:**

- Find the breaker labeled solar, PV or inverter. If it's tripped, push it fully to OFF, then back ON. If it trips again, stop and call your installer. For more, see [why a breaker keeps tripping](/electrical/breaker-keeps-tripping/).
- Check that the rapid shutdown switch and AC disconnect are in the ON position.
- **During a power outage**, a system without a battery shuts off by design to protect utility workers. It restarts on its own when grid power returns, often after a delay of a few minutes.

## 7. Monitoring is offline, not a real drop

Most monitoring apps rely on your home Wi-Fi or a cellular link. A new router, a changed Wi-Fi password or a power cut to the gateway can make the app show zero or a flat line while the panels keep working.

**Fix:**

- Check the inverter display or your **utility meter**. If power is being produced or sent to the grid, the problem is only monitoring.
- Reconnect the inverter or gateway to Wi-Fi using the maker's app or instructions.
- Missing data may not come back, so compare your electric bill with last year's to see if anything was really lost.

## 8. Normal panel degradation

All panels lose a little output each year. About half a percent per year is a commonly quoted figure for modern panels, and most panel warranties guarantee a set share of the original output after 25 years. Check your warranty for the exact numbers.

**Fix:** None needed. This shows up as a slow drift over many years, never a sudden drop. If one year is far below the last on similar weather, look for causes 2, 3, 5 and 6 instead, or a damaged panel (cracked glass, browning or burn marks you can see from the ground).

## Producing nothing at all?

- **Is it daytime and not under heavy snow?** Panels produce nothing at night and very little under thick snow.
- **Is the grid down?** Without a battery, the system stays off until grid power returns.
- **Check the solar breaker, AC disconnect and rapid shutdown switch.** All must be on.
- **Check the inverter.** A blank screen or a red light points to an inverter fault or no power reaching it.
- **Check for a utility notice.** After a new meter installation or service work, the system may need the utility or installer to turn it back on.

## When to call your installer

Call your solar installer or a licensed electrician if:

- The inverter shows a **fault code** that returns after a restart, or a **ground fault** warning.
- The solar breaker **trips again** after you reset it.
- You see **cracked glass, burn marks, melted connectors** or damaged wiring.
- Output is well below last year on similar days and you can't find shade or dirt to explain it.
- One panel or one string reads much lower than the others in your app.

Panel and inverter repairs involve high DC voltage and roof work. Leave them to a pro, and check your warranty first, since many repairs are covered.
