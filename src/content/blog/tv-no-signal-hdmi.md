A **No Signal** message means the TV is working but isn't receiving a picture on the input you've selected. The TV, the cable and the device (cable box, streaming stick, game console or computer) all have to be on, connected and agreeing on the same format.

Most of the time the cause is simple: the **wrong input is selected**, the **device is off or asleep**, or the **cable is loose or faulty**. Less often, the TV and device fail a **format or copy protection handshake**, especially through a receiver or soundbar.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Read the port number** on the back of the TV where the cable is plugged in, then select that same input with the remote.
2. **Plug a different device** (a streaming stick or game console you know works) into the same port with the same cable.
    - **It works?** The original device or its settings are the problem (fixes 2, 7, 8).
    - **Still no signal?** The cable, the port or the TV is the problem (fixes 3, 4, 9).
3. **If you use a receiver or soundbar** in between, connect the device straight to the TV. If that works, the problem is in the middle box (fix 6).

## 1. Select the right input (source)

TVs number their HDMI ports (HDMI 1, HDMI 2 and so on), and the TV only shows the one you pick. It's easy to land on the wrong one after a power outage, a firmware update or a family member using the remote.

**Fix:**

- Press **Input** or **Source** on the remote and step through each HDMI input slowly. Give each one a few seconds.
- Match the input to the number printed beside the port on the TV.
- If the remote is lost, most TVs have an input button on the side, bottom or back of the set. Check your manual.

## 2. Make sure the device is on and awake

A cable box, console or computer in standby often sends no signal at all. Some devices go to sleep after a period of no use and the TV then reports No Signal.

**Fix:**

- Look for the device's power light and press a button on **its own** remote or controller to wake it.
- Check the device's power cord and power adapter. Streaming sticks powered from a TV's USB port sometimes don't get enough power. Try the wall adapter that came with it.
- **Computer:** wake it with the keyboard or mouse. On Windows, press **Windows key + P** and choose **Duplicate** or **Extend** so it sends a picture to the TV.

## 3. Reseat the cable and try another port

HDMI connectors are a friction fit and can work loose, especially behind a wall-mounted TV or when the cable is pulled tight. A damaged port or a faulty cable gives exactly the same No Signal message.

**Fix:**

1. Unplug the cable at **both ends**, check the connectors for bent or dirty pins, and push each one in firmly and straight.
2. Try a **different HDMI port** on the TV, then select that input.
3. Swap in a **known-good cable**, ideally a short one. If the new cable works, throw away the old one.
4. Avoid tight bends right at the connector. Leave some slack so the plug isn't being pulled.

## 4. Power cycle the TV and source in the right order

The TV and the device exchange information over HDMI when they first connect. If that exchange gets stuck, a full power cycle usually clears it. A plain off and on with the remote often isn't enough, because many TVs only go into standby.

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the TV and the device.
2. **Unplug both from the wall** (or power strip) and wait **60 seconds**.
3. Plug in and turn on the **TV first**, and select the right HDMI input.
4. Then plug in and turn on the **device**. Give it a minute to boot.

## 5. Turn off HDMI-CEC to rule out a conflict

HDMI-CEC lets devices control each other over the cable, for example turning on the TV and switching inputs when you start a console. It's handy, but devices can fight over which input is active, or switch the TV to the wrong one. Each brand gives it its own name, such as Anynet+ (Samsung), SimpLink (LG) or BRAVIA Sync (Sony).

**Fix:**

- Turn off HDMI-CEC in the TV's settings (look under general, external device or input settings, and check your manual for the exact name).
- Turn it off on the device too if it has a CEC option.
- Power cycle both (fix 4) and test. If the picture comes back, leave CEC off or turn it back on after a firmware update.

## 6. Take the receiver or soundbar out of the chain

An AV receiver, soundbar, HDMI switch or splitter sits between the device and the TV, and **every link must support the same resolution, refresh rate and copy protection**. One older box in the middle can block the whole picture.

**Fix:**

1. Connect the device **straight to the TV** with a known-good cable.
2. If that works, the receiver or soundbar is the problem. Power cycle it, check it's set to the right input, and update its firmware.
3. Check that its HDMI input and output are rated for the format you're sending (for example 4K at 60Hz with HDR, or 4K at 120Hz). The manual or the label by the ports will tell you.
4. A common fix is to connect the device directly to the TV and send sound to the soundbar or receiver through the TV's **HDMI ARC or eARC** port instead.

## 7. Lower the resolution or refresh rate

If the device sends a format the TV can't display, the TV may show No Signal. This often happens after you change a resolution or refresh rate setting, move a console or computer to a different TV, or turn on 4K at 120Hz on a port that doesn't support it.

**Fix:**

- Connect the device to a different TV or monitor that does work, set it to **1080p at 60Hz**, then move it back and step the setting up.
- **Computer:** most graphics settings revert automatically after about 15 seconds if you don't confirm a new resolution. Wait and see if the picture returns.
- **Game consoles:** PlayStation and Xbox consoles have a low-resolution or safe mode startup for exactly this problem. Follow the steps on the console maker's support page for your model.
- Some TVs need an enhanced or full-bandwidth HDMI setting turned on for each port before they accept 4K HDR or 120Hz. Check your TV's manual for its name.

## 8. Clear an HDCP handshake problem

HDCP is the copy protection used by streaming apps, cable boxes and Blu-ray players. The device and the TV (and anything in between) check each other before the picture appears. If that check fails, you may see No Signal, a black screen, a brief picture that then drops, or an HDCP error message. 4K content generally needs **HDCP 2.2** support on every device in the chain.

**Fix:**

- Power cycle everything in order (fix 4). This clears most HDCP failures.
- Remove any splitter, switch or capture device and test again.
- Use the HDMI port on the TV marked for 4K or HDCP 2.2 if your TV labels them.
- Make sure everything in the chain supports HDCP 2.2 if you're watching 4K. Older receivers often don't.

## 9. Use a cable fast enough for 4K and 120Hz

HDMI cables are certified by speed, not by version number. A cable that worked fine for 1080p can fail at 4K with HDR or at 120Hz, showing No Signal, flickering or sparkles. Long cable runs make it worse. (If you're curious why fast signals are so fussy about cables, here's a primer on [signal integrity](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/what-is-signal-integrity/).)

| Cable label | Max bandwidth | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High Speed | 10.2 Gbps | 1080p, 4K at 30Hz |
| Premium High Speed | 18 Gbps | 4K at 60Hz with HDR |
| Ultra High Speed | 48 Gbps | 4K at 120Hz, 8K |

**Fix:**

- For 4K at 120Hz (newer consoles and gaming PCs), use a **certified Ultra High Speed HDMI** cable. Certified cables carry a label on the packaging you can scan to check it.
- Plug it into an **HDMI 2.1** port that supports 120Hz. On many TVs that's only one or two of the ports.
- For long runs, use an **active or fiber optic HDMI cable**. These are usually directional, so the end marked "source" must go to the device.

## Still no signal? Update firmware, then reset

**Update the TV's firmware.** TV makers fix HDMI and compatibility bugs in updates. Connect the TV to the internet and run a software update from its settings menu. If it can't connect, many makers offer the update as a USB download on their support site. Update the device and any receiver or soundbar too.

**Factory reset as a last resort.** A factory reset returns every setting to default and can clear a corrupted setting. It also erases your Wi-Fi details, app sign-ins and picture settings, so do it only after everything above. Use the reset option in the TV's settings menu and follow your manual.

## When to get it repaired

Contact the TV maker or a TV repair shop if:

- **No input shows a picture** from any known-good device and cable, even after a factory reset.
- One HDMI port is **loose, bent or damaged** and the others work. You can keep using the good ports.
- The TV's own menus and apps also show a black screen or flicker. That points to the screen or the main board, not HDMI.

If the TV is under warranty, contact the maker before opening it or paying for a repair.
