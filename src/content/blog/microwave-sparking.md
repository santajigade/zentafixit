Sparks or little flashes of light inside a microwave are called **arcing**. They happen when the microwave energy jumps through the air to a sharp metal edge or a hot spot instead of being absorbed by the food. A brief spark is alarming, and it can damage the microwave or start a fire if you keep running it.

The most common causes are **metal in the oven, food splatter on the waveguide cover, a burnt waveguide cover and chipped interior paint**. Most of these are safe and cheap to fix from the inside of the cooking cavity. A few faults sit inside the cabinet and are a job for a technician only.

> **Safety first:** The moment you see sparks, press STOP or open the door, then unplug the microwave. If there are flames, **keep the door closed** to starve the fire of air, stop or unplug the unit if you can reach it safely, and call 911 if it does not go out quickly. Never remove the outer cabinet. The **high-voltage capacitor** inside can hold a dangerous, even deadly, charge long after the microwave is unplugged. Everything in this guide is done from inside the cooking cavity, with the microwave unplugged.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick check

1. **Stop and unplug.** Let the oven cool for a few minutes and open the door to clear any smoke.
2. **Look for metal.** Check the food, wrapper, container and any utensil for foil, twist ties, staples, metal trim or a metal rack.
3. **Find the spot.** With a flashlight, look for black marks, pits or soot on the waveguide cover (the small flat panel on an inside wall or ceiling), the walls, the ceiling and the door.
4. **Run a water test.** If nothing looks damaged, put a microwave-safe cup of water inside and run it for about 1 minute. Watch through the door.
    - **No sparks?** The cause was almost certainly what you were heating (causes 1, 6 or 7).
    - **Sparks again?** Look closer at the cover, paint and rack (causes 2 to 5). If those all look fine, see cause 8.

## 1. Metal inside: foil, twist ties, trim or utensils

Metal is the number one cause. Thin or crumpled metal, sharp edges and points (foil, twist ties, staples on takeout bags, a fork) concentrate the energy and spark almost at once. Dishes and mugs with gold or silver trim can also spark, and the trim often turns black.

**Fix:**

- Remove all foil, twist ties, clips, staples and utensils before heating.
- Move food into a plain glass or ceramic dish labeled microwave safe.
- Don't use plates, bowls or mugs with metallic paint, trim or a metallic glaze.
- Some manufacturers allow small, smooth pieces of foil for shielding. Only do this if your manual says so, and keep foil well away from the walls.
- Wipe out any soot, then check the spot where it sparked for burn marks (causes 3 and 4).

## 2. Food splatter on the waveguide cover

The waveguide cover is the thin panel, often made of a mineral called mica, that covers the opening where microwave energy enters the cavity. It sits on a side wall or the ceiling, depending on the model. Grease and food splatter on it can heat up, char and start to arc.

**Fix:**

1. Unplug the microwave.
2. Wipe the cover gently with a soft cloth dampened with warm water and a drop of dish soap. Mica is fragile, so don't scrub or soak it.
3. Dry it fully and clean the walls and ceiling around it too.
4. Cover food with a microwave-safe lid or paper towel to stop new splatter.

## 3. A burnt or damaged waveguide cover

Once splatter chars into the cover, the burnt spot keeps arcing even after cleaning. Look for black spots, holes, cracks or layers peeling apart. A cover that has burnt through can let grease reach the waveguide opening behind it.

**Fix:**

- Replace the cover. On most models it comes off with clips or a single screw from inside the cavity, no cabinet removal needed. Check your manual.
- Order the cover listed for your model number (on a label inside the door frame or on the back). Generic mica sheets can be cut to match the old one if an exact part isn't available.
- While it is off, look into the opening with a flashlight. If you see burnt metal or heavy grease inside the waveguide, don't use the microwave until a technician checks it.

## 4. Chipped paint or a damaged cavity coating

The inside walls are coated with paint or enamel. If it chips, scratches through or rusts, the bare metal edge can arc. Check the walls, the ceiling and the floor under the turntable.

**Fix:**

1. Unplug the microwave and clean the area.
2. For small chips, some manufacturers and parts suppliers sell **touch-up paint made specifically for microwave cavities**. Ordinary paint is not suitable. Follow the product directions and let it cure fully before use.
3. If the damage is large, rusted through or right next to the waveguide opening, replacing the microwave is usually the safer choice.

## 5. A metal rack used the wrong way

Some over-the-range and convection microwaves come with a metal rack. Used wrongly, it can spark against the walls or against a metal pan.

**Fix:**

- Use the rack only with the microwave that came with it, and only in the modes your manual allows. Some racks are meant for convection or combination cooking only.
- Seat it fully in its supports so it doesn't touch the walls.
- Check the rack's feet and supports. If the coating is chipped or a support is missing, replace the rack.
- Never put a metal pan or foil on the rack in microwave mode unless your manual says it's allowed.
- If you aren't using it, take the rack out.

## 6. Certain foods that spark

Some foods spark even with no metal around. Dense vegetables such as carrots, green beans, peppers, kale and spinach contain minerals that can cause small arcs, especially at thin edges or dry ends. Grapes and similar small round foods can also produce sparks, and you should never microwave them on purpose.

**Fix:**

- Cut vegetables into even pieces and trim thin, dry tips.
- Add a little water to the dish and cover it.
- Use a lower power level and stir partway through.
- If one food sparks and others don't, the microwave is probably fine. A clean water test confirms it.

## 7. Running it empty (or nearly empty)

With nothing inside to absorb the energy, it reflects around the cavity. That can cause arcing and over time damage the waveguide cover and the magnetron (the part that makes the microwaves). A tiny amount of food can do the same.

**Fix:**

- Never run the microwave empty, even for a quick test. Use a cup of water.
- Keep kids from starting it with nothing inside. Use the child lock if your model has one.
- If it sparked after running empty, inspect the cover for burn marks (cause 3) and do a water test.

## 8. Worn stirrer, magnetron or other internal faults

Some microwaves use a stirrer (a slowly turning fan blade, usually behind a cover in the ceiling) to spread the energy. If it stops turning or is damaged, hot spots and arcing can result. Sparks with nothing metal inside, a clean cover and good paint can also point to a failing magnetron or a problem in the waveguide or high-voltage circuit. Loud buzzing, a burning smell from the vents or sparks you can see behind the cover are other warning signs.

**Fix:** Stop using the microwave and call a qualified appliance technician, or replace it. These parts sit inside the cabinet, next to the high-voltage capacitor. Don't open it, even if it has been unplugged for a while. If your microwave also stopped heating, see [microwave not heating](/appliances/microwave-not-heating/) for the other signs of a high-voltage fault.

## Fire inside the microwave?

- **Keep the door closed.** Opening it feeds the fire fresh air.
- **Press STOP and unplug it,** or turn off its breaker, if you can do so safely.
- **Wait.** Small fires from food or paper usually burn out in the closed cavity.
- **Get out and call 911** if the fire doesn't go out quickly, spreads outside the microwave or the cabinet above it is smoking. Only use an extinguisher if you know how. See [how to use a fire extinguisher](/home-safety/how-to-use-fire-extinguisher/).
- After any fire, don't use the microwave again until a technician has checked it. In most cases, replace it.

## When to call an appliance technician

Call a qualified appliance technician, or plan to replace the microwave, if:

- It sparks with nothing metal inside and the waveguide cover and paint look fine.
- You see burnt metal or grease inside the waveguide opening.
- The stirrer doesn't turn, or you hear loud buzzing or smell burning from the vents.
- There was a fire inside it.
- The door, hinges or door seal are damaged, or the door doesn't close firmly.
- Chipped or rusted paint covers a large area.

For a countertop microwave, a repair inside the cabinet can cost close to the price of a new one, so replacing it often makes sense. Over-the-range and built-in models cost more to replace, so a repair quote is more often worth getting.
