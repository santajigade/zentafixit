A little work in spring keeps your central air running well when the first hot week arrives. Most of the checklist is simple: **a fresh filter, a clean and clear outdoor unit, a flowing condensate drain, intact pipe insulation and a thermostat test**. The parts that need gauges and training belong to a **yearly professional tune-up**.

Set aside an hour or two on a mild day. If anything on this list turns up a problem, you still have time to get it fixed before you really need the AC.

> **Safety first:** Central AC runs on 240 V. Turn the system off at the thermostat, then switch off the **outdoor disconnect** and the **AC breaker** before cleaning the outdoor unit. Never open the outdoor unit's electrical panel: the capacitor inside can hold a dangerous charge even with power off. Leave refrigerant, wiring and motors to a licensed technician.

## Your pre-summer checklist

1. **Replace or check the air filter** at the indoor unit or return grille.
2. **Turn off power** at the thermostat, disconnect and breaker.
3. **Clear debris and plants** from around the outdoor unit.
4. **Gently rinse the condenser coil** with a garden hose.
5. **Flush the condensate drain** and check the drain pan.
6. **Inspect the foam insulation** on the refrigerant lines.
7. **Restore power and test** the thermostat in COOL mode.
8. **Book a professional tune-up** if it's been a year or more.

## 1. Replace or check the air filter

A clogged filter cuts airflow across the indoor coil, which reduces cooling and can even freeze the coil. It's the single easiest thing on this list.

**How:**

1. Set the thermostat to OFF.
2. Slide out the filter at the indoor unit or behind the return grille. Note the size on the frame and the airflow arrow.
3. Slide in a new one with the arrow pointing toward the indoor unit.

For intervals and which MERV rating to buy, see [how often to change your furnace filter](/hvac/how-often-change-furnace-filter/). The same filter serves your AC.

## 2. Turn off power before outdoor work

The outdoor unit usually has its own **disconnect box** on the wall next to it, plus a double breaker in your main panel.

**How:**

1. Set the thermostat to **OFF**.
2. At the disconnect, pull out the block or flip the switch to **OFF**.
3. At the main panel, switch the AC or condenser breaker to **OFF**.

If you're not sure which breaker it is, check the labels or leave both off until you're done. The outdoor fan can start without warning when the thermostat calls for cooling, so don't skip this step.

## 3. Clear around the outdoor unit

The outdoor unit pulls air in through its sides and blows it out the top. Leaves, grass clippings, weeds and shrubs crowding it reduce that airflow and make it work harder.

**How:**

- Remove leaves, sticks and debris from around and on top of the unit.
- Trim plants and shrubs back so there's open space on all sides. A couple of feet is a common recommendation. Your manual may list an exact clearance.
- Keep the area above the unit clear, with no decks, shelves or branches close overhead.
- Check that the unit sits **level** on its pad. A badly tilted unit can be a sign the ground has shifted, which a technician should look at.
- Point lawn mower discharge and dryer vents away from the unit in future.

## 4. Gently rinse the condenser coil

The thin metal fins around the outdoor unit release heat from your home. When they're packed with dirt or cottonwood fluff, cooling drops.

**How:**

1. Confirm the power is off (step 2).
2. Brush loose fluff and leaves off the fins with a **soft brush**, moving along the fins, not across them.
3. Rinse the fins with a **garden hose on a gentle spray**, aiming straight in rather than at an angle. Work around the unit from top to bottom.
4. **Never use a pressure washer.** It flattens the soft aluminum fins and blocks airflow.
5. Avoid spraying directly into the electrical panel or service access cover.
6. If a few fins are bent, a **fin comb** can straighten them. Large flattened areas are a job for a technician.

Don't remove the top fan assembly to clean inside. That involves wiring and is best left to a pro during a tune-up. Commercial coil cleaners exist, but follow the label exactly or leave chemical cleaning to a technician.

## 5. Check the condensate drain

Your AC pulls water out of the air. It drips into a pan under the indoor coil and runs out through a drain line, usually PVC pipe, to the outside, a floor drain or a small pump. Algae and sludge can block it, and many systems have a **float switch** that shuts cooling off when the water backs up.

**How:**

1. Find the drain line near the indoor unit. Many have a **capped access tee** on top.
2. Pour in a cup of water or distilled white vinegar and check that it drains freely.
3. Find where the line ends outside or at a floor drain. If it's slow, use a **wet/dry vacuum** on that end for a minute or two to pull out the clog.
4. Look in the drain pan with a flashlight for standing water, rust or cracks.
5. If you have a **condensate pump**, pour water into its reservoir and check that it switches on and pumps out.

Water stains or puddles near the indoor unit are worth fixing now, before summer humidity fills the pan every day.

## 6. Check the refrigerant line insulation

Two copper lines run between the indoor and outdoor units. The **larger line** carries cold refrigerant and is wrapped in black foam insulation. Without it, the line sweats and drips, and the system loses some cooling.

**How:**

- Walk the visible length of the lines, especially outdoors where sun and weather break the foam down.
- Replace cracked, crumbling or missing sections with **foam pipe insulation** sized to the pipe. Slit-style foam slips on without disconnecting anything.
- Outdoors, wrap the foam with **UV-resistant tape** to protect it from the sun.
- Don't bend, kink or pull on the copper lines while you work.

Oily residue on the lines or fittings can point to a refrigerant leak. Leave that for a technician.

## 7. Restore power and test the thermostat

**How:**

1. Turn the breaker and disconnect back **ON**. If the unit has been powered off for a long time (for example, all winter), some manufacturers recommend restoring power several hours before running it. Check your manual.
2. Replace the thermostat batteries if it uses them.
3. Set the thermostat to **COOL**, fan on **AUTO**, and a setpoint a few degrees below room temperature.
4. Within a few minutes, the outdoor fan should spin and the compressor should hum steadily.
5. After about 15 minutes, the air from the supply vents should feel noticeably cooler than room air.

If the outdoor unit stays silent, hums without the fan spinning, or the air isn't cooling, turn the system off and see [why an AC runs but doesn't cool](/hvac/ac-running-not-cooling/).

## 8. Schedule a professional tune-up

Some checks need gauges, meters and training. A yearly tune-up, ideally in spring before the summer rush, usually covers:

- **Refrigerant charge** and checking for leaks. Under EPA rules, refrigerant work requires a certified technician.
- **Electrical connections, contactor and capacitor** in the outdoor unit.
- **Motors and fan blades** in the indoor and outdoor units.
- **Indoor coil and blower** cleaning where needed.
- **Temperature drop** across the indoor coil and overall airflow.
- **Thermostat calibration** and safety controls.

Ask what's included before you book, since tune-up packages vary. If your equipment is under warranty, check whether regular maintenance is a condition of coverage and keep the receipts.

## Things to leave to a pro

- Opening the outdoor unit's electrical panel or touching the capacitor.
- Adding or recovering refrigerant.
- Removing the top fan assembly or replacing motors.
- Cleaning the indoor evaporator coil, which usually means opening sealed panels.
- Any work inside the furnace or air handler beyond the filter slot.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a licensed HVAC technician if:

- The outdoor unit won't start, hums without the fan spinning, or the breaker trips.
- You see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil.
- The condensate drain keeps clogging or the pan is cracked.
- You notice oily residue on the lines, hissing or bubbling sounds.
- It's been more than a year since the last professional tune-up.

Tell them what you've already done (filter, coil rinse, drain flush). It saves time and helps them focus on the checks you can't do yourself.
