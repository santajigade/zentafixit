Most smart thermostats need **constant 24 V power** to run their screen and Wi-Fi. That power comes from the transformer in your furnace or air handler through two wires: **R** (power) and **C** (common, the return path). Many older homes were wired without a C-wire because old thermostats ran on batteries or didn't need power at all.

The good news: you usually have options. In order of what to try first, they are **an unused wire already in the wall**, **an adapter kit at the furnace**, **a plug-in transformer**, **new cable**, or **a thermostat that works without C**.

> **Safety first:** Thermostat wires carry only about 24 V, but the furnace or air handler they connect to runs on **120 V or 240 V**. Turn power off at the **furnace service switch** (it often looks like a light switch on or near the unit) or at the **breaker** before you touch any wire at either end. Don't remove sheet metal panels beyond the control board access door, and never work on gas valves or burners.

## How to tell if you have a C-wire: quick check

1. **Turn the system off** at the thermostat, then turn off power at the furnace switch or breaker.
2. **Remove the thermostat face** and look at the wall plate. Take a clear photo of every wire and the letter it's on.
3. **Look for a wire on C.** If one is there, you already have a C-wire. Confirm at the furnace that it lands on C or COM on the control board.
4. **Count all the wires,** including any that aren't connected to anything. Note how many there are and which letters are used.
5. **Check the furnace end.** Open the control board access door and see which wires from the thermostat cable connect to the board, and whether any are cut short or folded back.

Not sure what each letter means? Our guide to [thermostat wire colors and terminals](/hvac/thermostat-wire-colors/) explains R, C, W, Y, G and the rest.

## 1. Look for an unused wire behind the thermostat

Installers often use a cable with more conductors than they need and leave the extras unconnected. A spare wire is frequently **pushed back into the wall hole** or **folded down behind the wall plate**, so it's easy to miss.

**What to do:**

- Gently pull a little more cable out of the wall and look for a wire that isn't on any terminal. Its insulation may still be on the end.
- At the furnace, look for the same color wire. It's often **folded back along the outer jacket** of the thermostat cable or tucked under it.
- If you find the spare at both ends, you can use it as C (next step).

## 2. Connect a spare wire as C

A spare wire only works as C if it's connected at **both ends**: to **C** on the thermostat and to **C or COM** on the HVAC control board.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/thermostat-c-wire-adapter-spare-wire.svg" alt="Wiring view: thermostat base with R, W, Y, G and C terminals connected by a cable to the matching terminals on the HVAC control board, with the spare wire highlighted running from C on the thermostat to C or COM on the board" width="640" height="430" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>The spare wire becomes C only when it lands on C at both ends.</figcaption></figure>

**How:**

1. With power off, strip about 1/4 inch of insulation from each end of the spare wire if needed.
2. At the control board, connect it to the **C** or **COM** terminal. Some boards already have another wire there (often from the outdoor unit); the new wire can usually share it, but check the board's markings and your furnace manual.
3. At the wall plate, connect the same wire to **C**.
4. Write "C" on a piece of tape on both ends for the next person.
5. Close the access door so its safety switch is pressed, restore power and check the thermostat powers up.

If the thermostat stays blank, see the "no 24 V" section below before you change anything else.

## 3. Repurpose the G wire (with a big caveat)

If you have four wires and no spare, some people move the **G (fan)** wire to **C** at both ends. It works electrically, but you lose the thermostat's control of the fan on its own. More importantly, **on some systems the indoor blower won't run in cooling without a G signal**. The outdoor unit would then run with no indoor airflow, which can **freeze the indoor coil**.

**Only consider it if:**

- Your thermostat's instructions describe this setup and have a setting for it.
- You've confirmed (or your furnace documentation says) that the blower runs on a cooling call with G disconnected.
- You don't mind losing "fan only" mode.

When in doubt, use an adapter kit instead. It keeps all your functions.

## 4. Install a C-wire adapter kit at the furnace

A C-wire adapter kit (also sold as an **add-a-wire** kit or **power extender**) is the most common fix for a **four-wire system** (R, W, Y, G). A small module mounts at the furnace or air handler. It combines two signals, usually heating and cooling or cooling and fan, onto one wire, which frees a wire to carry C. Some kits also put a small piece at the thermostat end, and some smart thermostats include their own kit in the box.

**How it generally goes:**

1. Check the kit's **compatibility list** against your system (gas furnace, heat pump, two-stage, dual fuel and so on) and your thermostat. Not every kit works with every setup.
2. With power off, label every wire at the control board before you disconnect anything.
3. Wire the module to the board and the thermostat cable exactly as its instructions show.
4. Update the wall plate wiring as the instructions say, restore power and test heat, cool and fan.

If any wire in the instructions doesn't match what you see, stop and ask an HVAC technician. A wrong connection can blow the small fuse on the control board.

## 5. Use a 24 V plug-in transformer

A **plug-in 24 V AC transformer** plugs into a 120 V outlet and supplies power to the thermostat over its own pair of wires. It's an option when there's an outlet near the thermostat and running wires to the furnace is hard.

**Things to know:**

- How it connects depends on the thermostat. Follow the thermostat maker's instructions for an external transformer, and check that they allow one at all.
- **Don't connect a second transformer to the same R terminal** as the HVAC transformer unless the instructions say it's fine. Two transformers tied together can damage equipment.
- Use a transformer rated **24 V AC** with enough capacity for the thermostat (check its power requirement). Never connect the thermostat to 120 V.
- You'll have a visible cord down the wall, and the thermostat loses power if that outlet does.

## 6. Run new thermostat cable

Replacing the cable is the **most reliable fix** and the only real option if you have just **two wires** (often a heat-only system). New cable gives you a proper C-wire and spare conductors for future upgrades.

**What's involved:**

- Use standard thermostat wire, usually **18 gauge**, with more conductors than you need today (for example 5 or 8 so you have spares).
- The old cable can sometimes pull the new one through the wall. Tape them together well and pull slowly.
- On a finished wall, an outside wall or a long run to an attic or basement furnace, this can mean fishing wire through framing.

This is a good job to hand to an **HVAC technician** if you've never fished cable before.

## 7. Choose a thermostat that works without a C-wire

Some smart thermostats work without C, either by **power stealing** (drawing a small trickle of power through the heating or cooling wires when the system is idle) or by running on **batteries**.

**Downsides to weigh:**

- **Power stealing** can cause the furnace or fan to **click, short cycle or turn on briefly** on some equipment, and the thermostat may drop Wi-Fi or reboot when its internal battery runs low. If you're already seeing that, see [smart thermostat not connecting to Wi-Fi](/smart-home/smart-thermostat-not-connecting-to-wifi/).
- **Battery models** need fresh batteries regularly and may limit features to save power.
- The maker's compatibility checker is the best guide. Don't assume a model works on your system without checking.

## Which option should you pick?

| Your situation | Best option | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spare wire at both ends | Connect it as C | Free, about 30 min |
| Exactly 4 wires (R, W, Y, G) | Adapter kit at the furnace | Check the kit's compatibility list |
| 4 wires, fan runs on cooling without G | G wire as C | You lose fan-only mode |
| Outlet near the thermostat, hard cable run | Plug-in 24 V transformer | Follow the thermostat's instructions |
| Only 2 wires | Run new cable | Most reliable, often a pro job |
| Want to avoid wiring work | Thermostat that works without C | Possible power stealing issues |

## No 24 V system at all?

Not every thermostat circuit is a 24 V system, and none of these options apply if yours isn't.

- **Thick wires or wire nuts at the thermostat** often mean a **line voltage** thermostat for electric baseboard heat at 120 or 240 V. A standard smart thermostat can't connect to these. You need one rated for line voltage.
- **Gas fireplaces and some wall furnaces** use **millivolt** systems with no transformer. They need a thermostat made for millivolt systems.
- **Boilers and zone controls** may have the C terminal on a separate zone panel or transformer. Check the panel's labels and manual.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a licensed HVAC technician if:

- The wiring at the control board doesn't match your thermostat photos, or wires are unlabeled and you can't trace them.
- The thermostat stays blank after wiring, or the system stops working. A blown control board fuse is common after a wiring slip.
- You have a heat pump, two-stage, dual fuel or zoned system and the adapter kit instructions don't clearly cover it.
- You need new cable run through finished walls.
- You smell gas or burning, or see scorch marks on the control board. Turn the system off at the switch or breaker and get help.
