A power surge is a brief spike in voltage, lasting a tiny fraction of a second, that can stress or destroy the electronics in your home. A **whole-house surge protector** (also called a surge protective device or **SPD**) mounts at your electrical panel and diverts large surges to ground before they reach your circuits.

For most homes the best setup is **layered protection**: **a panel-mounted SPD** for surges coming in from outside, plus **plug-in surge protectors** for your most sensitive electronics. Here's how each piece works and what it can't do.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>A whole-house surge protector is worth having in most homes, and recent US electrical codes require one when a home's service is installed or replaced. It protects hardwired appliances and HVAC equipment, but it isn't a substitute for plug-in surge protectors on electronics, and nothing guarantees protection from a direct lightning strike.</p></div>

> **Safety first:** A whole-house surge protector is installed inside or beside your main electrical panel, and some connect on the side of the main breaker that stays live when the main is off. Don't open your panel to install one yourself. Have a licensed electrician do it, with a permit where required.

## How to decide: quick check

1. **Count what's hardwired or hard to replace:** HVAC system, heat pump, water heater controls, refrigerator, washer, range, garage door opener, well pump, EV charger. These can't use a power strip, and a panel SPD is their main protection.
2. **Think about your area:** frequent thunderstorms, overhead utility lines or long rural lines all mean more surge exposure.
3. **Check your existing protection:** look at your panel for a surge module with indicator lights, and check the lights on your plug-in strips.
4. **Planning panel or service work?** If you're replacing the panel or service, an SPD is likely required anyway under recent codes. Ask your electrician.

If you answered yes to any of the first two, a whole-house unit is a sensible upgrade.

## 1. What a power surge is

US homes run on 120/240 V. A surge pushes that voltage far higher for a split second. Big surges come from **lightning** striking nearby power lines or the ground, and from **utility switching** on the grid. Smaller, more frequent surges start **inside your home** when motors and compressors (AC units, refrigerators, pumps) switch on and off.

A single big surge can kill an appliance outright. Repeated small ones can wear down electronics over time, which is why control boards sometimes fail with no obvious cause.

## 2. Whole-house (panel-mounted) surge protectors

A whole-house SPD connects to your electrical system at or near the service panel. Inside are components (often metal oxide varistors, or MOVs) that do nothing at normal voltage but conduct when voltage spikes, sending the excess energy to the grounding system.

**What it protects:** everything on your panel to some degree, including **hardwired loads** like HVAC equipment, which you can't protect any other way.

**What to look for:**

- A **UL 1449** listing (the US safety standard for surge protective devices), marked on the device.
- A rating for your system: a typical US home is **120/240 V split-phase**.
- **Indicator lights** that show protection is working, and an audible alarm on some models.
- A warranty that covers the device. Some include limited coverage for connected equipment; read the terms closely.

## 3. Type 1 vs Type 2: what's the difference

The "Type" describes where the device is allowed to be installed:

| Type | Where it goes | Typical use in a home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Type 1** | Between the utility and the main breaker (line side), or on the load side | At the meter base or ahead of the main breaker; also fine in the panel |
| **Type 2** | After the main breaker (load side) | Most common: in or beside the main panel, on a breaker |
| **Type 3** | At the point of use | Plug-in surge strips and surge receptacles |

For most homes, a **Type 2** device at the main panel is the usual choice, and many products are listed as Type 1 or Type 2 so they can go in either place. Your electrician will pick what suits your panel and service. A detached garage or shop with its own subpanel may benefit from a second unit there.

## 4. Point-of-use surge protectors

Plug-in surge protector strips (Type 3) sit right at the equipment, so they catch residual surges that get past the panel device plus surges generated inside the home. Use them for TVs, computers, game consoles, routers and modems.

**Tips:**

- A cheap **power strip** is not always a surge protector. Look for "surge protector" and the **UL 1449** listing on the label.
- Choose models with **coax and Ethernet** ports if a surge can reach your gear through the cable or network line.
- Replace a strip when its **"protected" light** goes out, or after a big surge. It may still pass power after its protection is used up.
- Don't plug a space heater or other high-draw heater into a surge strip. Plug those directly into the wall.

## 5. Joules, VPR and other ratings explained

- **Joules (plug-in strips):** how much surge energy the strip can absorb before its protection wears out. Higher is generally better, but there's no single standard test behind the number, so compare within a brand rather than across brands.
- **VPR (voltage protection rating):** from UL 1449 testing, the voltage the device lets through during a test surge. **Lower is better.** For 120 V strips, 330 V is the lowest rating, with 400 V and 500 V also common.
- **Surge current (kA):** whole-house units are usually rated in **kiloamps** of surge current rather than joules. Higher numbers mean more capacity to handle repeated or large surges.
- **Indicator lights:** the simplest rating of all. If the light says protection is lost, the device is just a wire.

## 6. What surge protectors can't do

- **Direct lightning strikes.** A direct or very close strike can overwhelm any SPD. Lightning protection systems (rods and down conductors) are a separate, specialized system. During severe storms, unplugging valuable electronics is the only sure protection.
- **Power outages, brownouts and sags.** An SPD doesn't keep the power on or fix low voltage. For that you need a UPS (battery backup); see [why a UPS beeps](/solar-power/ups-beeping/) for how they behave.
- **Longer overvoltage** from a utility fault or a loose neutral. These last too long for an SPD to absorb and need an electrician or the utility to fix.
- **Surges that bypass the panel**, through cable TV, satellite, phone or Ethernet lines. Those need grounding and protection where they enter the house.
- **Poor grounding.** An SPD diverts energy to ground, so it can only work as well as your home's grounding system. Your electrician should check it during installation. (For the engineering background, see this explainer on [earthing systems and how fault current returns](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/tn-tt-it-earthing-systems/).)

## 7. Installation and cost

Installation usually means mounting the SPD in a spare breaker space or beside the panel and connecting it to a **2-pole breaker** or other connection the manufacturer specifies. Short, straight leads matter, because long wires reduce how well it works, so placement is part of the job.

**Typical cost:** the device itself often costs somewhere around **$75 to $300**, and an installed total usually runs a few hundred dollars depending on your panel and local labor rates. It's often cheaper to add one while an electrician is already doing panel work. Get a written quote.

## 8. Is it required by code?

Starting with the **2020 edition of the National Electrical Code (NEC)**, surge protection is required for services that supply dwelling units, including when a service is replaced, and the 2023 edition kept that requirement. The device must be Type 1 or Type 2.

But states and cities adopt NEC editions on their own schedules, and some change the rules. An existing home generally isn't required to add one unless you're doing work that triggers it, such as a service or panel replacement. **Check with your local building department or electrician** for what applies to you.

## Already had a big surge?

- **Check the indicator lights** on your panel SPD and every plug-in protector. Replace any that show lost protection.
- **Reset tripped breakers and GFCIs** and test outlets that went dead.
- **Note what failed and when.** Contact your homeowner's insurance and any SPD warranty provider before throwing damaged equipment away.
- If lights flicker, dim or get unusually bright, or appliances act strangely after a storm, call an electrician. That can mean a damaged neutral, which is a safety issue.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician to:

- **Install a whole-house surge protector**, or check one that shows a fault light.
- **Inspect grounding and bonding**, especially in older homes or after lightning damage.
- **Investigate repeated failures** of electronics, control boards or LED bulbs, which can point to surges or wiring problems.
- **Quote surge protection** as part of any panel upgrade, service replacement or EV charger installation.
