An outlet should feel about the same temperature as the wall around it. When an outlet, cover plate or plug feels **warm or hot**, electricity is turning into heat where it shouldn't, usually because of **too much current** or **a poor connection**.

The common causes are **an overloaded outlet**, **a space heater or other high-draw appliance**, **a worn receptacle that grips plugs loosely**, and **a loose wire connection** behind the outlet. Some are easy to fix. Others are early warnings of an electrical fire.

> **Safety first:** Don't open the outlet or remove the cover plate to look inside. Heat at an outlet can mean damaged wiring, and the box is live until the breaker is off. Don't touch a scorched or melted plug while the power is on. Turn off the breaker first, then unplug it with dry hands.

## How to tell if it's an emergency: quick test

1. **Look, smell and listen.** Check for **scorch marks or brown discoloration**, **melted or warped plastic**, a **burning or fishy smell**, **buzzing, crackling or sizzling**, or **sparks**. Any one of these: go straight to the emergency section below.
2. **Feel the cover plate**, not the slots, with the back of your hand. Hot to the touch is a warning even without other signs.
3. **If there are no danger signs, unplug everything** from the outlet and wait about 30 minutes.
4. **Feel it again:**
    - **Cooled down?** The load or the plug fit is the likely cause (causes 1 to 3).
    - **Still warm with nothing plugged in?** The problem is in the wiring (causes 4 to 6). Turn off the breaker and call an electrician.

## 1. Too much plugged in (overloaded outlet)

Every outlet and circuit has a limit. Most household outlets are on **15 or 20 amp** circuits. Plugging several heat-producing appliances into one outlet, or into a power strip on it, can push the outlet's contacts beyond what they handle comfortably.

**Fix:**

- Unplug some of the load and spread appliances across outlets on **different circuits**.
- Never plug heaters, microwaves, toasters, coffee makers or hair dryers into the same outlet at once.
- Check the wattage on each appliance's label. To convert watts to amps (at 120 V), use this [amps to watts calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/amps-to-watts/).
- If the breaker trips when you use these together, the circuit is overloaded. See [why a breaker keeps tripping](/electrical/breaker-keeps-tripping/).

## 2. A high-draw appliance like a space heater

A typical 1,500 W space heater draws about **12.5 amps** at 120 V, close to the limit of a 15 amp circuit, and runs for hours. That steady heavy load shows up any weakness at the outlet, plug or wiring as heat. Window air conditioners, dehumidifiers and portable heaters are common culprits.

**Fix:**

- Plug space heaters **directly into a wall outlet**. Don't use extension cords or power strips with them, as most heater manuals warn.
- Don't run another major load on the same outlet or circuit at the same time.
- If the plug or outlet gets more than slightly warm, stop using the heater on that outlet and have the outlet checked.
- Check the heater's plug and cord. A **discolored or hot plug** can mean a worn plug or a failing outlet. Replace a damaged heater rather than repairing the cord.

## 3. A loose plug or a worn-out receptacle

Inside an outlet, spring contacts grip the plug blades. Over years of use those springs weaken. A plug that **wobbles, droops or falls out** makes poor contact, and a poor contact heats up under load.

**Fix:**

- Try a different plug in the outlet. If plugs slip out easily, the receptacle is worn.
- **Have the receptacle replaced.** They're inexpensive, and an electrician can check the wiring at the same time. Use a tamper-resistant outlet, which current codes generally require in homes.
- Don't bend plug blades apart to make them grip tighter.

## 4. A loose wire connection behind the outlet

Wires connect to an outlet with screws or push-in holes. If a screw loosens over time, the connection develops resistance and heats up, which loosens it further. Because current for **other outlets on the circuit** often passes through this outlet's terminals, it can heat up **even with nothing plugged in**.

**Fix:**

1. Turn off the breaker for that outlet and label it so no one turns it back on.
2. Don't use the outlet until it's repaired.
3. Have an electrician open it, cut back any damaged wire, remake the connections and replace the receptacle if it's discolored.

## 5. Push-in (backstab) connections

Many outlets let wires be pushed into holes in the back instead of wrapped around screws. These **backstab** connections are quick to install but grip the wire with a small spring, and on a heavily loaded outlet they're a common source of heat.

**Fix:** Have an electrician move the wires to the **side screws** or to a receptacle with clamp-style terminals, and check the other outlets on that circuit while the power is off.

## 6. Aluminum wiring

Some US homes built roughly from the **mid-1960s to the mid-1970s** have solid aluminum branch circuit wiring. Connections between aluminum wire and standard outlets and switches can loosen and overheat over time. Signs include **warm cover plates**, **flickering lights** and a **burning plastic smell**. You may see "AL" or "ALUMINUM" printed on cable jackets in an unfinished basement or attic.

**Fix:**

- **Don't try to repair it yourself.** Standard outlets and ordinary wire connectors aren't suitable for aluminum wire.
- Have an electrician who's experienced with aluminum wiring inspect the house. Repairs use specific connectors and devices approved for aluminum.
- Until it's checked, avoid heavy loads on outlets that feel warm.

## 7. Dimmers and USB outlets: some warmth is normal

**Dimmer switches** turn some energy into heat as they work, so a dimmer feeling **warm** is normal. One that's **too hot to keep your hand on**, buzzes loudly or has discolored plastic isn't. **USB outlets** can also feel slightly warm while charging.

**Fix:**

- Check the dimmer's **wattage rating** against the total wattage of the bulbs it controls. Switching to LED bulbs cuts the load a lot, but use a dimmer rated for LEDs.
- Dimmers mounted side by side in one box usually need to be derated. Check the dimmer's instructions.
- If it still runs hot, buzzes or flickers, have it replaced.

## Smoke, sparks or a burning smell?

Act right away:

1. **If there are flames or heavy smoke, get everyone out and call 911.** Don't try to fight a fire that's spreading or between you and the exit.
2. **If there's no fire, turn off the breaker** for that outlet (or the main breaker if you don't know which one), as long as you can reach the panel safely.
3. **Never use water** on an electrical fire. For a small fire you can fight safely with a clear exit, use an extinguisher rated for electrical fires (Class C, such as an ABC extinguisher). Here's [how to use a fire extinguisher](/home-safety/how-to-use-fire-extinguisher/).
4. **Unplug devices only after the breaker is off**, and don't touch a melted plug with bare hands.
5. **If a burning smell continues** or the wall feels warm after the power is off, call the fire department. Fire can smolder inside a wall.
6. **Leave the breaker off** and call a licensed electrician before using that circuit again.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The outlet is **warm with nothing plugged in**, or stays warm after you remove the load.
- You see **scorch marks, discoloration or melting** on the outlet, cover plate or plug.
- You hear **buzzing or crackling**, or lights flicker when you touch the plug.
- Plugs **fall out** or fit loosely.
- Your home may have **aluminum wiring**.
- More than one outlet feels warm, or the **breaker trips** often.

Heat at an outlet rarely fixes itself. A service call costs far less than fire damage, so get it checked.
