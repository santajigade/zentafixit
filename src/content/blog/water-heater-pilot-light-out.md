A gas water heater's pilot is a small flame that stays lit to light the main burner. A safety device next to it, usually a **thermocouple**, tells the gas valve the flame is there. If the valve doesn't get that signal, it shuts off the gas, and the pilot won't stay lit.

Most of the time the cause is simple: **not holding the pilot knob long enough, a dirty or weak pilot flame, or a worn or misaligned thermocouple**. On newer sealed heaters, a **tripped flammable vapor sensor or a clogged air intake screen** is also common. Drafts, venting and gas supply problems cover most of the rest.

> **Safety first:** If you smell gas (a rotten egg smell), do not relight, do not switch lights or appliances on or off, and do not use your phone inside. Leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Always follow the lighting instructions printed on your heater. Keep gasoline, paint thinner and other flammable products well away from the heater. A heater that won't vent properly can put carbon monoxide (CO) into your home, so make sure you have working CO alarms.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

<figure><img src="/images/posts/water-heater-pilot-light-out-flow.svg" alt="Flowchart: relight per the label. If the pilot did not light, check the gas supply, igniter and pilot tube. If it lit but dies when you release the knob, check the hold time, flame and thermocouple. If it stays lit but dies hours later, check the draft, vent, vapor sensor and air screen" width="640" height="460" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>When the pilot goes out tells you where to look.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Check for gas smell first.** If you smell gas, stop and leave (see the safety note above).
2. **Find the lighting label** on the tank, usually near the gas control valve. Turn the knob to OFF and wait the time the label gives, so leftover gas can clear.
3. **Relight exactly per the label**, holding the pilot knob for the full time it states.
4. **Watch what happens.**
    - **No flame at all?** Look at the gas supply, the igniter and the pilot tube (causes 4 and 7, and the "won't light" section).
    - **Lights but goes out when you release the knob?** Hold time, flame or thermocouple (causes 1, 2, 3 and 8).
    - **Stays lit, then goes out hours or days later?** Draft, venting, the vapor sensor or the air screen (causes 5 and 6).

## 1. Not holding the pilot knob long enough

When you light the pilot, the thermocouple has to heat up before it makes enough signal to hold the gas valve open. Release the knob too soon and the pilot goes out, even when every part is fine.

**Fix:**

- Hold the knob down for the full time on your label, then release it slowly.
- If it goes out, turn the knob to OFF and wait the time the label gives before trying again. Don't keep clicking the igniter with gas flowing.
- If it goes out every time after a proper hold, move on to causes 2 and 3.

Once it holds, turn the knob from PILOT to ON and set the temperature dial. For other reasons a tank runs cold, see our [no hot water troubleshooting guide](/appliances/no-hot-water/).

## 2. A weak or dirty pilot flame

A healthy pilot flame is steady and mostly blue, and it wraps around the thermocouple tip. A small, lazy, flickering or mostly yellow flame often means dust, lint or rust in the pilot orifice or around the burner. A weak flame can't heat the thermocouple enough.

**Fix:**

- Look at the flame through the viewing window and compare it with any picture in your manual.
- Clean dust and lint from around the base of the heater (gas off and the heater cool).
- Cleaning the pilot orifice itself means taking the burner assembly out. On older open-door heaters an experienced DIYer can do this with the gas off. On sealed (FVIR) heaters, leave it to a pro.

## 3. Thermocouple out of position or worn out

This is one of the most common reasons a pilot lights but won't stay lit. The thermocouple is a metal rod that makes a small electric signal when heated. If its tip is bent out of the flame, its connection at the gas valve is loose, or it has simply worn out, the valve shuts the gas off. (For how thermocouples work in general, see this [thermistor vs thermocouple explainer](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/thermistor-vs-thermocouple/).)

**Fix:**

- With a flashlight, check that the thermocouple tip sits in the pilot flame. If it has been knocked aside, a pro can reposition it.
- On older heaters with an open burner door, check that the thermocouple nut at the gas control valve is snug. Don't overtighten it.
- Replacement thermocouples are inexpensive, usually under $30. On older open-door heaters, a handy homeowner can swap one with the gas off, following the part's instructions.
- On most newer sealed heaters, the thermocouple is part of a burner or pilot assembly behind a gasketed door. Call a plumber or gas technician so the door is resealed correctly.

Some newer heaters use a **thermopile** (a stronger version of a thermocouple) that powers an electronic gas valve. These often have a status light that blinks codes. Check your manual for what each pattern means.

## 4. A clogged pilot tube or orifice

The pilot tube is a thin line that carries gas from the control valve to the pilot. Spider webs, dust or debris can partly block it or the tiny orifice at the end, giving a weak flame or none at all.

**Fix:** Clearing the tube and orifice involves disconnecting gas fittings and testing them for leaks afterward. Unless you have done gas work before, have a plumber or gas technician clean it.

## 5. Flammable vapor sensor tripped or air screen clogged

Most newer tank gas heaters are **FVIR** (flammable vapor ignition resistant) models. They have a sealed combustion chamber, an air intake screen at the bottom, and a sensor or thermal cutoff that shuts the heater down if it detects flammable vapors or overheating. A screen clogged with lint, dust or pet hair starves the flame of air, which can make the pilot go out or trip the cutoff.

**Fix:**

1. Turn the gas control to OFF and let the heater cool.
2. Find the air intake screen near the base (your manual shows where) and gently vacuum or brush it clean.
3. Remove anything flammable stored near the heater, and air out the space if you have used solvents, paint or gasoline nearby.
4. Check the status light or label. On some models the sensor or thermal cutoff can be reset, but on many it is a **one-time part that must be replaced**, not reset.
5. If it trips again, call a pro. Repeated trips mean something is wrong with airflow, venting or the burner.

## 6. Draft or venting problems

A gas heater needs a steady supply of combustion air and a vent that carries exhaust outside. Wind blowing down the vent, a missing vent cap, a door that slams nearby, or strong exhaust fans in a tight house can pull or push air the wrong way and snuff the pilot.

**Fix:**

- Note when it goes out. Windy days, a running clothes dryer or bathroom fans are strong clues.
- Check that the vent pipe on top of the heater is connected, sloped upward and not rusted through, and that the vent cap outside is in place (look from the ground).
- If you have a draft hood (the metal collar on top of the tank), a pro can check whether exhaust is being pulled up the vent or spilling into the room.

**Venting problems are a carbon monoxide risk**, not just a nuisance. If your CO alarm sounds, follow our guide on [what to do when a carbon monoxide alarm goes off](/home-safety/carbon-monoxide-alarm-going-off/) and have the heater checked before using it again.

## 7. Gas supply problems

If gas isn't reaching the heater, the pilot can't light or stay lit.

**Fix:**

- Check the shutoff valve on the gas line to the heater. On a typical quarter-turn valve, the handle in line with the pipe means open.
- See whether other gas appliances (stove, furnace) work. If none do, call your gas utility.
- On propane, check the tank level. An empty or nearly empty tank is a common cause. Many suppliers need to check the system for leaks after a tank runs empty.

## 8. A faulty gas control valve

The gas control valve holds the pilot gas open while it gets a signal from the thermocouple or thermopile. If the pilot flame is good, the thermocouple is new and positioned correctly, and the pilot still won't stay lit, the valve may have failed.

**Fix:** This is a pro job. Replacing a gas control valve usually means draining the tank and testing gas connections for leaks. On an older heater, ask whether replacing the whole heater makes more sense.

## Pilot won't light at all?

- **No click or spark from the igniter.** Many heaters have a piezo igniter button. Look for a spark through the window (a dim room helps). If there is no spark, the igniter or its wire may be faulty or loose. Some labels allow a long lighter as a backup, but only if your label says so.
- **Spark but no flame.** Check the gas supply (cause 7) and a possible clogged pilot tube (cause 4).
- **Knob won't turn or stays locked.** Some valves won't move to PILOT unless pushed down first, and some lock out after a vapor sensor trip (cause 5). Check your label.
- **Electronic valve with a status light.** Look up the blink pattern in your manual before trying again.

## When to call a pro

Call a licensed plumber or gas technician if:

- You smell gas at any time (call the gas utility first, from outside).
- The pilot still won't stay lit after a correct relight and a clean air screen.
- The flammable vapor sensor or thermal cutoff keeps tripping or needs replacement.
- The thermocouple or burner sits behind a sealed combustion door.
- You see soot, scorch marks or melted parts near the burner door or the vent.
- Your CO alarm has sounded, or the pilot goes out with wind or fans running.
- The heater is also leaking or is near the end of its life.
