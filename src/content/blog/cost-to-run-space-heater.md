A plug-in space heater turns electricity straight into heat, so its running cost comes down to three numbers: **how many watts it draws**, **how many hours it actually heats**, and **what you pay per kilowatt-hour (kWh)**. Multiply them together and you have your answer.

Most portable heaters max out at **1,500 W** on a standard 120 V outlet, which is **1.5 kWh for every hour** at full power. Whether that hour costs you a few cents or close to half a dollar depends on **the rate on your own bill**, so this guide shows the method, then worked examples with clearly stated example rates.

> **Safety first:** Space heaters are a common cause of home fires. Plug yours **directly into a wall outlet**, never an extension cord or power strip, keep it **at least 3 feet** from anything that can burn, and turn it off when you leave the room or go to sleep unless the manual says otherwise. Full checklist [below](#safety-using-a-space-heater-without-risk).

## How to work out your cost: quick method

1. **Find the wattage** on the heater's label or in the manual (for example 1,500 W, or 750 W on low).
2. **Divide by 1,000** to get kilowatts (1,500 W is 1.5 kW).
3. **Multiply by the hours** it runs per day to get kWh per day.
4. **Multiply by your rate per kWh** from your electric bill. Multiply by 30 for a rough monthly cost.

If you'd rather not do the math by hand, this [electricity cost calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/everyday-life/electricity-cost/) does the same steps for you.

## 1. Find your heater's wattage

The rating label is usually on the back or bottom of the heater, near the cord. It lists volts (120 V), amps and watts. On a heater with two settings, the higher wattage is the high setting.

**Typical numbers:**

- **High:** 1,500 W is the common maximum for plug-in heaters. At 120 V that's about **12.5 amps**, most of what a standard 15 A circuit can carry.
- **Low:** often somewhere around **750 to 900 W**. Check your label, since it varies by model.
- **Fan only:** a small fraction of that. The fan alone uses little power; the heating element is what costs money.

If the label only lists amps, multiply amps by volts to get watts (12.5 A × 120 V = 1,500 W).

## 2. Find your electricity rate

Look on your electric bill for the price per kWh. Rates vary a lot by state, utility and season, so don't rely on someone else's number.

- **All-in rate:** many bills split the price into supply (generation) and delivery charges. For a realistic figure, divide the **total electric charges** by the **kWh used** that month. Leave out fixed monthly fees if you want to be precise.
- **Tiered or time-of-use plans:** your rate may be higher in certain hours or above a monthly usage limit. Use the rate that applies when the heater runs.

## 3. Estimate how many hours it really runs

A heater that's switched on for 8 hours doesn't always draw full power for 8 hours. Models with a built-in **thermostat** cycle the heating element off once the room reaches the set temperature, then back on as it cools. In a small, well-insulated room, it may be heating only part of the time. In a cold, drafty room, it may run nearly nonstop.

The only way to know your real usage is to measure it. A **plug-in energy use meter** (about $15 to $35 at hardware stores) sits between the outlet and the heater and shows total kWh. Plug the meter straight into the wall, check its label or instructions to confirm it's rated for a 15 A heater load, run the heater normally for a day, and read the kWh.

## 4. Do the math: worked examples

All examples use an **assumed rate of $0.16 per kWh**. Replace it with your own.

**Example A: high setting, 5 hours a day**
1,500 W ÷ 1,000 = 1.5 kW. 1.5 kW × 5 hours = 7.5 kWh. 7.5 × $0.16 = **$1.20 per day**, or about **$36.00 per month** (30 days).

**Example B: low setting, 8 hours a day**
750 W ÷ 1,000 = 0.75 kW. 0.75 × 8 = 6 kWh. 6 × $0.16 = **$0.96 per day**, or about **$28.80 per month**.

**Example C: high setting with thermostat cycling**
The heater is on for 8 hours, but your energy meter shows it actually used 6 kWh (the element was on about half the time). 6 × $0.16 = **$0.96 per day**, not the $1.92 you'd expect from nonstop full power.

## 5. Compare it with your central heat

Electric resistance heaters convert close to **100% of their electricity into heat**. That sounds ideal, but it doesn't make them the cheapest heat per unit. One kWh through a space heater gives you about 3,412 BTU of heat, and that's the ceiling.

- **Heat pump:** a heat pump moves heat from outside rather than making it, so in mild weather it can deliver roughly **2 to 3 times more heat per kWh** than a space heater, sometimes more. The advantage shrinks in very cold weather. For heating the whole house, a working heat pump is usually cheaper per unit of heat.
- **Electric furnace or baseboard heat:** these are also resistance heat, so the cost per unit of heat is about the same as a space heater.
- **Gas furnace:** whether gas is cheaper depends on your local gas and electric prices. Compare using your own bills.

**Where a space heater can save money** is **zone heating**: warming one small room you're sitting in while you turn the whole house thermostat down a few degrees. If you leave the central heat where it was and add a space heater on top, your bill goes up.

## 6. Ways to cut what it costs

- **Use the thermostat** on the heater and set it just warm enough, rather than running on high nonstop.
- **Try the low setting** first. In a small room it's often enough, at about half the power.
- **Use a timer** (built-in, or the heater's own auto-off) so it doesn't run after you've left.
- **Close the door** to the room you're heating and block obvious drafts.
- **Turn the central thermostat down** while you heat one room, or the heater only adds to your bill.
- **Sit near it, not far across the room.** You need less output to feel warm.

## Safety: using a space heater without risk

<figure><img src="/images/posts/cost-to-run-space-heater-safety.svg" alt="Top-down view of a space heater plugged directly into a wall outlet with a 3 foot clear zone around it, and a checklist: no extension cords or power strips, tip-over and overheat protection, safety listing label such as UL or ETL, turn it off when you leave or sleep" width="640" height="400" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Wall outlet only, a 3 foot clear zone, and a heater with tip-over and overheat shutoff.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Plug directly into a wall outlet.** No extension cords and no power strips. A 1,500 W heater draws about 12.5 A, which can overheat cords and strips not built for it.
2. **Keep 3 feet clear** around the heater: curtains, bedding, furniture, paper, clothes and anything flammable.
3. **Buy one with tip-over and overheat protection.** These shut the heater off if it falls over or gets too hot.
4. **Look for a safety certification mark** from a recognized testing lab, such as UL, ETL or CSA, on the heater or its label.
5. **Don't leave it running unattended** or while you sleep, unless the manufacturer's instructions say that model is designed for it.
6. **Place it on a hard, level floor**, away from walkways, water and wet areas such as bathrooms (unless it's rated for that use).
7. **Check the plug and outlet.** If either feels hot after running a while, stop using that outlet. See [what to do about a warm or hot outlet](/home-safety/outlet-warm-hot/).

Because one heater uses most of a 15 A circuit, running a second heater, a microwave or a hair dryer on the same circuit can trip the breaker.

## Bill jumped since you started using one?

Run the numbers above with your actual hours. A heater running 8 hours a day at full power uses 360 kWh in a month, which on its own adds a noticeable amount to most bills. If the heater math doesn't explain the increase, other causes are worth checking: see [why your electric bill is suddenly high](/solar-power/electric-bill-suddenly-high/).

## When to call an electrician

- The **breaker trips** every time you run the heater, even with nothing else on that circuit.
- An **outlet, plug or wall plate gets hot**, discolored or smells like burning.
- Lights **dim noticeably** when the heater turns on.
- You want to add a **dedicated circuit** or a hardwired 240 V heater for a room you heat often.

Stop using the heater on that outlet until it's checked. Heat at an outlet often means a loose or worn connection, which is a fire risk.
