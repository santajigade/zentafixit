A house that feels sticky, smells musty, or fogs up its windows has too much moisture in the air. You might also notice damp towels that never dry, peeling paint, or a cool but clammy feeling even with the AC running.

Most of the time the cause is **an air conditioner that isn't removing moisture well, poor ventilation in bathrooms and kitchens, or water coming in through a basement or crawl space**. Here's how to find the source and bring humidity back down.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Measure it.** Place a digital hygrometer in a central room for a few hours, then move it to the basement and to the room that feels worst. A reading above 60 percent is too high.
2. **Check the thermostat fan.** If it's set to ON instead of AUTO, switch it and check again after a day (cause 1).
3. **Compare rooms.** If one room or the basement is much higher than the rest, the source is local: a leak, a damp foundation or a missing exhaust fan (causes 3, 4, 6).
4. **Watch the AC.** If the whole house is humid and the AC runs only a few minutes at a time, the system itself is the likely problem (causes 2, 7).

## 1. The thermostat fan is set to ON

With the fan on ON, the blower keeps running between cooling cycles. Water that just condensed on the cold coil gets blown back into the house before it can drip into the drain pan, so the AC removes far less moisture.

**Fix:**

- Set the thermostat fan to **AUTO** so the blower runs only while the AC is cooling.
- If you have a smart thermostat, check for a fan "circulate" schedule and turn it down or off in humid months.
- Some thermostats have a dehumidify or "cool to dehumidify" setting. Check your manual before using it.

## 2. An oversized or short-cycling air conditioner

An AC that's too big for the house cools the air quickly and shuts off before it has run long enough to pull out much moisture. The result is a cool but clammy house. A unit that short cycles for other reasons (a bad thermostat location, a refrigerant problem) has the same effect.

**Fix:**

- Note how long each cooling cycle lasts on a warm day. Very short cycles of just a few minutes point to this problem.
- Make sure the thermostat isn't near a supply vent, a sunny window or a kitchen, which can make it shut the AC off too soon.
- Ask an HVAC technician to check the system. Options include lower fan speed settings, a variable-speed or two-stage system, or a whole-house dehumidifier added to the ductwork.

If the house is warm as well as humid, see our guide on an [AC running but not cooling](/hvac/ac-running-not-cooling/).

## 3. Not enough exhaust ventilation

A hot shower, boiling pasta or a pot of soup puts a lot of water into the air. Without a working exhaust fan it spreads through the house.

**Fix:**

- Run the bathroom fan during every shower and for about 20 minutes afterward. A timer switch makes this automatic.
- Hold a single square of toilet paper near the fan grille. If it isn't pulled against the grille, the fan is weak, blocked or not vented outside.
- Use the range hood when cooking, and check that it vents outdoors. Many hoods simply recirculate air through a filter and don't remove moisture.
- Clean dusty fan grilles, and replace noisy or weak fans.

## 4. A damp basement or crawl space

Moisture moves through concrete walls and floors, and an open crawl space can pull in humid outdoor air. That damp air then rises into the living space.

**Fix:**

- Walk around outside. Make sure gutters are clear, downspouts discharge several feet from the house, and soil slopes away from the foundation.
- Run a dehumidifier in the basement set to about 50 percent, and drain it to a floor drain or sump pit if you can.
- In a dirt-floor crawl space, a plastic vapor barrier over the ground helps a lot. Whether to seal and condition a crawl space depends on your climate and local code, so ask a pro.
- If the basement takes on water when it rains, check that your sump pump (if you have one) is working.

## 5. A dryer vent that leaks indoors

A clothes dryer pushes a lot of warm, wet air out of its vent. If the vent hose has come loose, is crushed, or ends in the basement or attic instead of outdoors, all that moisture stays inside.

**Fix:**

- Pull the dryer out and check that the vent duct is attached at both ends and has no tears.
- Go outside while the dryer runs and confirm air is coming out of the exterior vent hood.
- Replace thin plastic or foil accordion ducts with rigid or semi-rigid metal duct, which is also safer. See our [dryer vent cleaning](/home-safety/dryer-vent-cleaning/) guide.

## 6. A hidden water leak

If one room, closet or wall area is much more humid than the rest, look for a leak. Common spots are under sinks, behind the toilet, around a water heater, the AC drain pan, and under a leaking roof or window.

**Fix:**

- Look and feel for damp spots, stains, warped flooring or a musty smell.
- Check the AC indoor unit's drain pan and condensate line. A clogged line can overflow and soak the area around it.
- Turn off all water fixtures and watch the water meter for 30 minutes. If it moves, water is going somewhere.
- Repair leaks promptly. Hidden water damage gets more expensive the longer it goes.

## 7. Low airflow or a dirty coil

A clogged air filter, closed vents or a dirty indoor coil reduce how much air crosses the coil. The system can cool less evenly and remove moisture less effectively.

**Fix:**

- Replace the air filter, and open closed supply and return vents.
- Ask an HVAC technician to clean the indoor coil and check airflow and refrigerant charge during a tune-up.

## 8. Humid weather and daily habits

In hot, humid summers, every open window or door lets in moisture. Indoor plants, aquariums, drying laundry on racks and a humidifier left running also add up.

**Fix:**

- Keep windows closed on humid days and let the AC or a dehumidifier do the work.
- Dry clothes in a properly vented dryer or outside.
- Turn off whole-house or room humidifiers in warm weather, and set them lower in cold weather.

## Condensation and mold already showing?

- **Water on the inside of windows** in winter means humidity is too high for how cold the glass is. Lower humidity, run exhaust fans, and open curtains so warm air reaches the glass.
- **Small mold spots** on a hard surface can usually be cleaned with detergent and water while wearing gloves. Dry the area and fix the moisture source, or it will come back.
- **Large areas of mold**, mold inside walls, or mold after a flood need a professional. People with allergies or asthma should avoid cleaning mold themselves.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call an HVAC technician if:

- Humidity stays above 60 percent with the fan on AUTO and windows closed.
- The AC runs in very short cycles, or the house feels cold and clammy at the same time.
- Water is leaking from the indoor unit or the condensate line keeps clogging.
- You want a whole-house dehumidifier or a better-matched AC system.

For water coming through basement walls or a roof, a waterproofing contractor or roofer is the right call. For leaking pipes, call a plumber.
