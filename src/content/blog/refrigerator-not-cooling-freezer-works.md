If the freezer is still freezing but the fridge section is warm, the good news is that the compressor and refrigerant are probably fine. In most frost-free refrigerators, the only cold coil is in the freezer, and a fan pushes some of that cold air into the fresh food section.

So when only the fridge warms up, the problem is usually **airflow**: **blocked vents, an iced-up evaporator coil, a stopped evaporator fan or a stuck damper**. Here's how to find which.

<div class="key"><strong>Quick answer</strong><p>Check the temperature setting, then clear anything blocking the vents between the freezer and fridge. If the freezer back panel is frosted over or the fan is silent, the evaporator coil is iced up or the fan has failed. A full manual defrost often brings cooling back for a while, but a defrost or fan part usually needs replacing.</p></div>

> **Safety first:** Unplug the refrigerator before removing any panel, cleaning the coils or touching the fan. Pull it out slowly so you don't tear the water line or scratch the floor, and get help if it's heavy. Never chip at ice with a knife or screwdriver: you can puncture a refrigerant line.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

<figure><img src="/images/posts/refrigerator-not-cooling-freezer-works-flow.svg" alt="Flowchart: check settings and vents first; then look at the freezer back panel; heavy frost points to a defrost problem; no frost and a silent fan points to the evaporator fan; a running fan with no air reaching the fridge points to a blocked vent or the damper" width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Three checks narrow it down to one part.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Put an appliance thermometer** in a glass of water on the middle shelf and read it after several hours. Over 40°F confirms the fridge is too warm.
2. **Check the settings and the vents.** Make sure the control wasn't bumped and that no food is pressed against the vent openings.
3. **Open the freezer and look at the back wall.** A thick white layer of frost or ice on the panel points to a defrost problem.
4. **Open the freezer door and press the door switch** (the small button or lever the door pushes in). On many models the fan only runs with the door closed or the switch pressed. Listen and feel for airflow.
    - **No sound, no air?** See cause 4.
    - **Fan runs, freezer gets air, fridge doesn't?** See causes 2 and 5.

## 1. Temperature settings

It sounds obvious, but controls get bumped by bins and kids. On many fridges the fresh food dial also controls how much cold air reaches the fridge.

**Fix:**

- Set the fridge to the middle or recommended setting. Many dials use a 1 to 9 scale where a higher number means colder, but check the label or manual for your model.
- Aim for **37 to 40°F** in the fridge and **0°F** in the freezer, measured with a thermometer.
- Check for a vacation, sabbath or demo mode on digital controls. The way to turn these off varies by brand, so check your manual.
- After any change, allow up to 24 hours for temperatures to settle.

## 2. Blocked air vents

The cold air reaches the fridge through vents, usually at the top or back of the fresh food section, or in the freezer floor or back wall. A bag of frozen vegetables or a tall container can block them completely.

**Fix:**

- Find the vents in both sections. They are slots or grilles, often near the controls.
- Move food at least an inch or two away from them.
- Don't overfill the freezer. Air has to circulate through it before it reaches the fridge.
- Check that nothing has frozen solid over a vent opening.

## 3. Frosted evaporator coil (defrost problem)

Frost-free fridges melt frost off the evaporator coil at regular intervals using a small heater. If the heater, defrost thermostat or defrost control fails, frost keeps building until it blocks the airflow. The freezer may still be cold right next to the coil while the fridge warms up.

**Signs:** frost or ice on the freezer back panel, a fan that sounds like it's scraping ice, or water dripping after you leave the door open.

**Fix:**

1. Move food into coolers with ice.
2. **Unplug the fridge**, prop both doors open and lay towels down to catch meltwater.
3. Let it defrost fully. This often takes 24 hours or more. Don't use sharp tools or heat guns, which can damage the plastic liner or tubing.
4. Plug it back in and wait a day to see if cooling returns.

If cooling comes back but frost returns within days or weeks, a defrost part has failed. Experienced DIYers can test these with a multimeter using the model's service sheet. Otherwise call an appliance technician.

## 4. Evaporator fan not running

The evaporator fan sits behind the freezer back panel and pushes cold air into both sections. If it stops, the freezer may still feel cold because it sits right next to the coil, but very little cold air reaches the fridge. A noisy, squealing or chirping fan is often about to fail.

**Fix:**

1. With the freezer door closed (or the door switch pressed), listen for the fan.
2. If it's silent, check that ice isn't jamming the blade. A full defrost (cause 3) clears that.
3. If it's not iced and still won't run, the fan motor or the door switch may have failed.
4. Replacement fan motors are model-specific. Use the model number on the label inside the fridge to order the right one, unplug the fridge, and follow the maker's instructions to remove the panel. If you're unsure, an appliance technician can do it.

## 5. Stuck air damper

Many refrigerators have a **damper**, a small flap that opens and closes to control how much freezer air flows into the fridge. Some are manual, others are motorized. If it sticks closed or freezes shut, the fridge stays warm even though the fan is working.

**Fix:**

- Find the damper housing, usually at the top or back of the fresh food section where the air comes in. Check your manual for its location.
- Check that it isn't blocked by food or frozen shut. A defrost (cause 3) frees an iced damper.
- A motorized damper that has failed needs replacing. This is model-specific, so use the model number or call a technician.

## 6. Dirty condenser coils

The condenser coils release the heat the fridge removes. They are usually behind the kick plate at the bottom front or on the back. When dust and pet hair pile up, the whole fridge works harder and cooling suffers. The fridge section often shows it first.

**Fix:**

1. **Unplug the fridge.**
2. Remove the kick plate or pull the fridge out to reach the coils.
3. Clean them with a long coil brush and a vacuum. Be gentle with the tubing.
4. Clean around the condenser fan if your model has one, then plug back in.

## 7. Leaky door seals

A torn or loose gasket lets warm, humid air in. The fridge loses cold faster than it can replace it, and you may see condensation or frost near the door.

**Fix:**

- Close the door on a dollar bill. If it slides out with no resistance, the seal is weak at that spot. Check all the way around.
- Clean the gasket with warm soapy water. Sticky residue can stop it from sealing.
- Make sure the fridge is level or tilted very slightly back, so the doors swing shut on their own. Check your manual for leveling.
- Replace a torn or flattened gasket with the part for your model.

## Food warming up? Protect it first

If the fridge is above 40°F, move milk, meat, fish and leftovers to the freezer or a cooler with ice before you start troubleshooting. USDA guidance is to throw out perishable food that has been above 40°F for more than 2 hours. Keep the freezer door closed as much as you can while you work.

## When to call an appliance technician

Call a qualified appliance technician if:

- Frost keeps coming back after a full manual defrost.
- The evaporator fan or damper needs replacing and you're not comfortable removing panels.
- **Both** the freezer and fridge are warm, and the compressor at the back is silent, clicks on and off, or runs nonstop.
- You hear hissing, see oily residue near the tubing, or smell something unusual.

Problems with the compressor or refrigerant are sealed system repairs that require a certified technician. On an older refrigerator, ask for a repair estimate before you commit, because it may cost close to the price of a new one.
