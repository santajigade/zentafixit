When your central air is running but the house stays warm, the system is moving air without removing enough heat. That can be as simple as a wrong thermostat setting or as serious as a refrigerant leak.

The most common causes are **a thermostat setting, a clogged air filter, a tripped outdoor unit, or a dirty outdoor coil**. Several of these you can fix yourself in under an hour.

> **Safety first:** Central AC runs on 240 V. Turn the system off at the thermostat before checking anything, and turn off power at the outdoor disconnect and breaker before cleaning the outdoor unit. Never open the electrical panel on the condenser: the capacitor inside can hold a dangerous charge even with the power off.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it says COOL, the fan is on AUTO, and the setpoint is a few degrees below the room temperature.
2. **Pull out the air filter** and hold it up to a light. If you can't see light through it, replace it.
3. **Wait 15 minutes, then go outside.** Is the big fan on top of the outdoor unit spinning, and can you hear the compressor humming steadily?
    - **Outdoor unit silent or only humming?** Look at power, the drain safety switch and the capacitor (causes 3, 6, 7).
    - **Outdoor unit running?** Look at airflow, the coils and refrigerant (causes 2, 4, 5, 8).
4. **Look for ice** on the copper lines at the outdoor unit or on the indoor coil. Ice means turn the system off and see cause 5.
5. **Optional:** hold a kitchen thermometer at a supply vent and at the return grille. A small difference confirms the system isn't cooling properly.

## 1. Thermostat settings

A surprising number of "not cooling" calls turn out to be a setting. If the fan is set to **ON** instead of AUTO, the blower runs all the time, including between cooling cycles, so you feel room temperature air from the vents.

**Fix:**

- Set the mode to **COOL** and the fan to **AUTO**.
- Lower the setpoint at least 3 to 5°F below the current room temperature so the system has a reason to start.
- Replace the batteries if the thermostat has them and the screen is dim or blank.
- On a smart thermostat, check for schedules, eco modes or a "hold" that is overriding your setting. The exact menus vary, so check the maker's app or manual.

## 2. Dirty air filter

A clogged filter chokes the airflow across the indoor coil. Less air means less cooling, and very low airflow can let the coil freeze (cause 5).

**Fix:**

1. Turn the system off at the thermostat.
2. Find the filter. It is usually in a slot at the indoor unit or behind a return grille in a wall or ceiling.
3. Note the size printed on the frame and the airflow arrow.
4. Slide in a new filter with the arrow pointing toward the indoor unit.

Check the filter monthly in summer. Many need replacing every 1 to 3 months.

## 3. Tripped breaker or outdoor disconnect

The indoor blower and the outdoor unit usually have separate power. If the outdoor unit's breaker has tripped, the blower keeps pushing air but nothing is cooling it.

**Fix:**

1. Set the thermostat to **OFF**.
2. At your main panel, find the AC or condenser breaker (often a double breaker). If it sits in the middle or OFF position, push it fully to **OFF**, then back to **ON**.
3. Check the **disconnect box** on the wall next to the outdoor unit. Make sure the pull-out block is fully seated or the switch is ON. Someone may have pulled it during yard work.
4. Wait a few minutes, then set the thermostat back to COOL.

If the breaker trips again, stop resetting it. A repeat trip points to an electrical fault in the outdoor unit, which is a job for a technician.

## 4. Dirty outdoor condenser coil

The outdoor unit gets rid of the heat taken from your house. When its fins are packed with grass clippings, cottonwood fluff, leaves or dirt, the heat can't escape and cooling drops, especially on hot afternoons.

**Fix:**

1. Turn the thermostat to OFF and switch off the **disconnect** and the **breaker**.
2. Clear leaves, weeds and debris from around the unit. Trim plants back so air can move freely, leaving a couple of feet of open space.
3. Rinse the fins with a **garden hose on a gentle setting**. Never use a pressure washer, which flattens the soft aluminum fins.
4. Don't remove the top fan assembly or touch the wiring. Restore power when the unit has dripped dry on the outside.

## 5. Frozen evaporator coil

The indoor coil can ice over when airflow is too low or refrigerant is low. Ice blocks airflow even more, so you get weak, warm air. You may see frost on the larger insulated copper line or water around the indoor unit as it melts.

**Fix:**

1. Set the thermostat to **OFF** and the fan to **ON**. This blows room air over the coil to melt the ice.
2. Wait for it to thaw fully. This usually takes a few hours. Don't chip at the ice.
3. Replace the filter and make sure supply vents and return grilles are open and unblocked.
4. Watch the drain pan, which may overflow while the ice melts.
5. Turn cooling back on. If it freezes again, the cause is likely low refrigerant or a blower problem, so call a technician.

## 6. Clogged condensate drain or float switch

Your AC pulls water out of the air. That water drips into a pan and runs out through a drain line. Many systems have a **float switch** that shuts cooling off when the pan or line backs up, to prevent a flood. On some systems the blower keeps running, so it looks like the AC is on but not cooling.

**Fix:**

1. Turn the system off at the thermostat and the power switch at the indoor unit.
2. Look for standing water in the pan or a small switch clipped to the pan or drain line.
3. Find where the drain line ends outside or at a floor drain, and use a **wet/dry vacuum** on that end to pull out the clog.
4. If there is a capped access tee near the indoor unit, you can pour in a cup of distilled white vinegar to help keep the line clear.
5. Once the water drains, the float switch should reset on its own. Restore power and try cooling again.

## 7. Bad capacitor (pro only)

The capacitor gives the compressor and outdoor fan motor the kick they need to start. When it fails, you often hear the outdoor unit **humming** while the fan doesn't spin, or the fan turns very slowly. The compressor may click on and off.

**Fix:** Call an HVAC technician. A capacitor can store a dangerous charge even with the power off, and testing one safely takes the right meter and training. (If you're curious why, here is how [a capacitor stores charge](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/capacitor-working-principle/).) Don't poke the fan with a stick to start it. Turn the system off until it's repaired, because a motor that can't start runs hot.

## 8. Low refrigerant (pro only)

Refrigerant isn't used up like fuel. If the level is low, it has leaked out somewhere. Signs include a frozen coil that keeps coming back, ice on the outdoor lines, a hissing or bubbling sound, and a system that runs nonstop without catching up.

**Fix:** Call a licensed HVAC technician. They need to find and repair the leak, then charge the system to the maker's specification. Under EPA rules, refrigerant work requires a certified technician. Topping up without fixing the leak just means paying again next summer.

## Breaker keeps tripping or you smell burning?

Turn the system **OFF** at the thermostat and leave it off if:

- The AC breaker trips again after one reset.
- You smell burning, see smoke, or hear loud buzzing, grinding or screeching from either unit.
- The outdoor unit hums but the fan never starts.

Running it in this state can damage the compressor, which is usually the most expensive part to replace.

## When to call an HVAC technician

Call a licensed HVAC technician if:

- The coil freezes again after you have replaced the filter and thawed it.
- The outdoor unit hums, clicks or won't start with power on.
- You suspect a refrigerant leak or see oily residue on the copper lines.
- The breaker trips repeatedly.
- It still isn't cooling after you've worked through the steps above.

Tell them what you've already checked (filter, breaker, disconnect, drain). It helps them skip straight to the likely cause.
