When an outlet stops working, the problem is usually **not the outlet itself**. Power travels through a chain of outlets on each circuit, and a reset button or loose wire somewhere upstream can leave this one dead.

The most common causes are a **tripped GFCI outlet in another room**, a **tripped breaker**, an outlet controlled by a **wall switch**, or a **loose wire connection**. Check them in that order: the first three take seconds and need no tools.

> **Safety first:** Resetting GFCIs and breakers and flipping switches is safe. Before removing any outlet cover, turn off the breaker and confirm it's dead with a non-contact voltage tester. If the outlet is **scorched, melted, warm or smells burnt**, leave the breaker off and call an electrician.

## How to tell what's causing it: quick test

1. **Confirm it's really dead.** Plug in a lamp or charger you know works, or use a plug-in outlet tester. Try **both** halves of the outlet.
2. **Look around:** which other outlets and lights in the room or nearby are also dead? Write them down.
3. **Reset GFCIs** anywhere nearby, even in other rooms or outside.
4. **Check the breaker panel** for a tripped breaker.
5. **Flip nearby wall switches** and test again.

If it's still dead after all five, the cause is in the wiring (causes 4 to 6).

## 1. A tripped GFCI somewhere else

A single GFCI outlet often protects **several regular outlets** wired after it. Those downstream outlets have no buttons, so when the GFCI trips they simply go dead. The GFCI can be in a different room: a bathroom GFCI may feed another bathroom, and a garage GFCI may feed outdoor or basement outlets.

Where the controlling GFCI is often hiding:

| Dead outlet | Check for a GFCI in |
|---|---|
| Outdoor outlet | Garage, basement, a bathroom, or another outdoor outlet |
| Bathroom outlet | Another bathroom, often the nearest one |
| Garage or basement outlet | Elsewhere in the garage or basement, sometimes behind a freezer or shelves |
| Kitchen counter outlet | Another counter outlet, often near the sink |

Homes that use **GFCI breakers** instead have the reset button in the panel, so check there too.

**Fix:**

- Check every GFCI in **bathrooms, the kitchen, garage, basement, laundry area and outside**. Press **RESET** firmly on each.
- If a GFCI won't reset or keeps tripping, see our guide to a [GFCI outlet that keeps tripping](/electrical/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/).
- Once you find it, label the dead outlet "GFCI protected" so it's quicker next time.

## 2. A tripped breaker

If a whole room or several outlets and lights went dead together, a breaker probably tripped. A tripped breaker often sits in a **middle position** and can look like it's still on.

**Fix:**

- Open the panel door and look for a handle that's out of line with the others.
- Push it firmly to **OFF**, then back to **ON**.
- If it trips again right away, unplug everything on that circuit and try once more. If it still trips, call an electrician.

## 3. It's a switched outlet

In many bedrooms and living rooms, **one half of an outlet** (often the top or bottom) is controlled by a wall switch so you can turn on a lamp from the door. If the switch is off, that half looks dead.

**Fix:**

- Flip every wall switch in the room and test both halves of the outlet again.
- If one half works only with the switch on, nothing is broken. Plug always-on devices into the other half.

## 4. A loose backstab connection

Most outlets on a circuit are **daisy-chained**: power passes through each outlet to reach the next. Many builders use **backstab** (push-in) connections on the back of the outlet. Over time these can loosen, so the outlet with the loose wire, and **every outlet after it**, goes dead while earlier outlets still work.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/outlet-not-working-chain.svg" alt="Diagram of a daisy-chained circuit: breaker panel, then a GFCI outlet, then outlet A working, then outlet B with a loose backstab wire, then outlets C and D dead because power stops at the loose connection" width="640" height="400" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>A loose wire at one outlet can kill every outlet after it.</figcaption></figure>

**Fix:**

- Map which outlets are dead. The fault is usually **at the last working outlet or the first dead one**.
- If you're experienced with wiring: turn off the breaker, confirm it's dead, pull the outlet out and check for loose or backstabbed wires. Moving them to the **side screw terminals** gives a firmer connection.
- If you're not, this is a quick job for an electrician. A loose connection can arc and heat up before it fails completely.

## 5. A worn-out outlet

Old outlets lose their grip. If plugs **fall out**, feel loose, or devices work only when the plug is wiggled, the contacts inside are worn. Poor contact can also cause heat at the plug.

**Fix:**

- Replace the outlet. New outlets usually cost a few dollars, and tamper-resistant types are typical for homes today.
- Match the type: a 15 amp outlet for a 15 amp circuit, and a GFCI where one is required. If you're unsure, have an electrician do it.

## 6. A burnt or melted outlet

A failed connection, a loose plug or an overloaded outlet can **overheat**. You may see brown or black marks, melted plastic, or smell burning. The outlet may have stopped working because the connection burned through.

**Fix:**

- **Turn off the breaker** for that circuit and leave it off.
- Unplug whatever was in it and check that device's plug for damage.
- **Call an electrician.** The wire ends and the box may also be damaged, and the cause (often a loose connection or overload) needs to be found. Our guide to an [outlet that's warm or hot](/home-safety/outlet-warm-hot/) explains the warning signs.

## Still dead after all of that?

If no GFCI is tripped, no breaker is tripped, there's no switch, and you can't see damage, the fault is hidden in the wiring: a loose wire at an outlet earlier in the chain, a failed connection in a junction box, or damaged cable. Before you call, make a quick map. It saves the electrician time and you money:

1. Note **which outlets, lights and switches** are dead, room by room.
2. Use a plug-in tester or lamp on each outlet nearby, including ones on the other side of the wall.
3. Note **when it stopped**: after a storm, after hanging a picture, after plugging in a heater, or out of nowhere.
4. Note anything unusual: a flicker before it died, a warm cover plate, a faint smell.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- You see **scorch marks**, melted plastic or smell burning at any outlet.
- An outlet or cover plate feels **warm**.
- Several outlets are dead and no GFCI or breaker is tripped.
- A breaker or GFCI won't reset or keeps tripping.
- You're not comfortable working inside an outlet box.

Most dead outlets come back with a GFCI reset or a breaker flip. When they don't, a loose wire is the usual reason, and it's worth fixing promptly.
