US homes use two kinds of special protection on top of ordinary circuit breakers. A **GFCI** (ground-fault circuit interrupter) protects **people from shock**. An **AFCI** (arc-fault circuit interrupter) protects the **house from fires** caused by arcing wires.

Where each one is required depends on the **room**, the **code edition your area has adopted**, and **when the work was done**. This guide covers the typical requirements under recent editions of the National Electrical Code (NEC), in plain English.

## How to check your home: quick walk-through

1. **Find your GFCI outlets.** Look for outlets with TEST and RESET buttons in bathrooms, the kitchen, garage, outdoors, basement and laundry area. One GFCI can protect other outlets wired after it, which may carry a small "GFCI Protected" sticker.
2. **Look at your breaker panel.** GFCI and AFCI breakers have a TEST button and are often labeled on the handle or body. Dual-function breakers protect both ways.
3. **Test each GFCI** with its TEST button. Power should cut off with a click, and RESET restores it.
4. **Make a list of gaps**, such as a bathroom or outdoor outlet with no GFCI protection. Those are the most valuable upgrades.

## 1. GFCI vs AFCI: what each one does

A **GFCI** compares the current going out on the hot wire with the current coming back on the neutral. If they differ by a tiny amount (around 5 milliamps), some current is leaking, possibly through a person, and it shuts off power in a fraction of a second.

An **AFCI** watches the electrical "signature" of the circuit for the patterns that arcing creates, such as a frayed cord, a nail through a cable or a loose connection, and shuts off power before the arc can start a fire.

Neither replaces the ordinary breaker's job of stopping overloads and short circuits. GFCI and AFCI **breakers** include that standard protection. A GFCI **outlet** does not.

## 2. Where GFCI protection is typically required

Under recent NEC editions, GFCI protection is typically required for outlets in:

- **Bathrooms:** all outlets.
- **Kitchens:** countertop outlets for many years, and the newest editions extend it to all kitchen outlets. A dishwasher outlet or circuit is often included.
- **Garages and accessory buildings** such as sheds and workshops.
- **Outdoors:** all outdoor outlets.
- **Basements:** unfinished basements for many years, and newer editions include finished basements too.
- **Crawl spaces** at or below ground level.
- **Laundry areas.**
- **Near sinks:** outlets within 6 feet of the edge of a sink, and near bathtubs and showers.
- **Pools, hot tubs and boathouses**, which have their own detailed rules.

Recent editions also extend GFCI rules in these areas beyond standard 120 V outlets to many **240 V outlets**, such as a dryer outlet in a laundry room or a 240 V outlet in a garage. If a GFCI keeps cutting out once installed, see [why a GFCI outlet keeps tripping](/electrical/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping/).

## 3. Where AFCI protection is typically required

AFCI rules started with bedroom circuits in the early 2000s and have expanded in later editions. Recent editions typically require AFCI protection on 120 V, 15 and 20 amp circuits supplying:

- **Bedrooms**
- **Living rooms, family rooms, dens and libraries**
- **Dining rooms**
- **Kitchens**
- **Laundry areas**
- **Hallways and closets**
- **Sunrooms, recreation rooms** and similar rooms

Note that this covers the whole **circuit**, including lights and smoke alarms on it, not just the outlets. Bathrooms, garages and outdoor circuits usually need GFCI but often aren't on the AFCI list. Check your local rules.

## 4. Breaker, outlet or both? Your options

- **GFCI outlet:** protects itself and any outlets wired after it through its LOAD terminals. Cheap, easy to test, and you reset it in the room where it trips.
- **GFCI breaker:** protects the entire circuit from the panel. Handy for outdoor circuits or when you can't fit a GFCI outlet.
- **AFCI breaker:** the usual way to add AFCI, because it protects the whole run of wire from the panel. Modern ones are "combination type", meaning they detect both kinds of arcing faults.
- **AFCI outlet** (outlet branch circuit type): protects outlets downstream of it. Codes allow it in certain cases, often when replacing outlets or extending a circuit in an older home.

## 5. Dual-function devices (GFCI plus AFCI)

**Dual-function** breakers and outlets combine GFCI and AFCI protection in one device. They're a natural fit for **kitchens and laundry areas**, where both are typically required, and they save panel space.

Don't confuse the terms: **"combination AFCI"** means an AFCI that detects more types of arcs. It doesn't include GFCI protection. Look for **"dual function"** on the label if you need both.

## 6. Older homes and replacing outlets

Code rules generally apply when work is done. Existing wiring that met code when it was installed doesn't usually have to be upgraded just because the code changed.

But replacing or extending things can trigger current rules:

- **Replacing an outlet** in a location where today's code requires GFCI protection usually means the replacement must be GFCI protected.
- **Replacing an outlet or extending a circuit** in an area that now requires AFCI often triggers AFCI protection, with some exceptions. Ask your electrician or building department.
- **Two-prong outlets with no ground wire** can often be replaced with a GFCI outlet labeled "No equipment ground". This adds shock protection, but it doesn't create a real ground.

Even where it isn't required, adding GFCI protection in wet areas of an older home is one of the cheapest safety upgrades you can make.

## 7. Why the rules vary by state and city

The NEC is updated every three years, but it isn't law until a state or city adopts it, and each one does that on its own timeline. Some adopt a new edition quickly, some stay on an older one for years, and many make local amendments, sometimes to GFCI and AFCI rules specifically.

So a house built in one town in a given year might have different requirements than one built across the state line. **Your local building department** can tell you which edition and amendments apply, and a permit inspection confirms the work meets them.

## Testing GFCI and AFCI devices

- **GFCI outlets and breakers:** press TEST. Power should cut off, and RESET or switching the breaker back on restores it. Most manufacturers recommend testing monthly. If it doesn't trip, replace it.
- **AFCI breakers:** press TEST with the breaker on. It should trip. Switch it fully OFF, then ON to reset. Check the label for the recommended interval.
- A plug-in tester's GFCI button won't trip a GFCI that has no ground wire, so use the device's own TEST button there.
- An AFCI that trips repeatedly may be catching a real wiring fault or reacting to certain motors or older appliances. Don't swap it for a standard breaker. Have an electrician find the cause.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician to:

- **Add GFCI or AFCI breakers** in your panel, or install dual-function breakers.
- **Upgrade an older home**, especially one with two-prong outlets, before a remodel or sale.
- **Track down repeated tripping** you can't explain.
- **Pull permits** and make sure work meets the code edition your area enforces.

Replacing a single GFCI outlet is a common DIY job for experienced homeowners, but always turn off the breaker and confirm the power is off first. If you're unsure, leave it to a pro.
